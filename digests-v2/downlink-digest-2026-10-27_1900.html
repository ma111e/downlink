Omegatech Is Disrupted, and the Pressure on Bulletproof Hosting Keeps Building

Law enforcement’s seizure of Virtualine/Omegatech reflects growing pressure on bulletproof hosting providers, but these operators can rapidly evade disruption by shifting IP ranges and customers to new ASNs and shell companies. Defenders should continuously track infrastructure patterns, newly populated ASNs, and sanctions-linked entities rather than relying on static blocklists.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The FBI seized virtualine.net on October 27, 2026, under Operation Omegaline, following an investigation led by the FBI Washington Field Office with law-enforcement partners in Germany, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom.
  • 02Silent Push observed infrastructure moving from Railnet LLC (AS214943) to Omegatech (AS202412) in February 2026 and identified Virtualine Technologies within Omegatech’s network.
  • 03Omegatech was not fully offline after the seizure, but its network address ranges showed very low routing visibility, which the article says typically indicates a serious operator-side problem.
  • 04In 2026, authorities also seized more than 800 servers associated with THE.Hosting/WorkTitans BV and arrested two men, while the EU sanctioned Media Land, ML Cloud, and general director Aleksandr Volosovik.
  • 05The article states that bulletproof hosting providers can obtain a new network identifier within two to five business days and move the same malicious internet address ranges to it, making static blocklists quickly outdated.

The FBI seized virtualine.net on October 27, 2026, under Operation Omegaline, an investigation led by the FBI Washington Field Office with law-enforcement partners in Germany, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom. Silent Push had observed a major infrastructure shift in February 2026 from Railnet LLC’s AS214943 network to Omegatech’s AS202412 network and identified Virtualine Technologies within Omegatech’s subnets. The Justice Department said Virtualine also operated as Omegatech, while Omegatech’s network prefixes remained visible at very low levels after the seizure rather than being fully offline.

The article describes broader pressure on bulletproof hosting providers, including the Dutch seizure of more than 800 servers linked to THE.Hosting/WorkTitans BV, arrests, and sanctions against Media Land and related entities. It notes that operators can acquire a new autonomous system number, or network identifier, in two to five business days and move the same IP address ranges to it, making static ASN-based blocklists quickly outdated. Silent Push recommends monitoring newly allocated ASNs that rapidly fill with IP addresses, screening providers against both US and EU sanctions lists, and tracking operating patterns and shared records that can connect apparent front companies.

  • A bulletproof hosting provider can obtain a replacement ASN in two to five business days, so blocking a known network identifier alone can become ineffective almost immediately when the operator moves its address ranges to a new network.
  • Sanctions coverage can leave operational gaps because US, UK, Australian, and EU designations do not necessarily occur at the same time, allowing a provider to continue operating through entities or facilities in jurisdictions where it is not yet sanctioned.
  • Shared email authorization records can expose links between apparent front companies: anonrdp.com and ghostynetworks.com listed the same four IP addresses in their SPF records.
  • A newly allocated ASN that rapidly accumulates a large number of IP addresses may indicate infrastructure migration; after Aeza Group’s July 2025 sanctions, AS211522 was allocated on July 10 and already contained more than 2,100 IPs.
  • Virtualine allegedly marketed an additional protection service that moved customers’ infrastructure to an unaffiliated shell company after law-enforcement requests and then restored the server.

The FBI and international partners seized a website linked to Omegatech and Virtualine, companies accused of renting internet services to criminals and keeping phishing, harmful software, and extortion-related systems online. Other similar providers have also faced server seizures, arrests, and sanctions, showing that governments are increasing pressure on this part of the criminal online economy. This matters because these companies can quickly move their customers and internet addresses to new names or networks, allowing scams and other harmful activity to return even after a takedown.
