Inside AvisLoader: A Windows Malware Loader Built to Resist Disruption

Varonis researchers uncovered AvisLoader, a Windows malware loader delivered through ClickFix lures that uses the encrypted Tox peer-to-peer network for resilient command-and-control and payload delivery. Defenders should watch for suspicious pasted terminal commands, trycloudflare.com downloads, altered shortcuts, and unexpected Tox traffic.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Varonis Threat Labs discovered AvisLoader, a Windows loader found on an exposed staging server alongside a ClickFix lure, supporting tools, and its command center.
  • 02The DocuSign-themed ClickFix lure used a fake Cloudflare verification prompt to persuade visitors to paste a command that downloaded and ran code from a Cloudflare Quick Tunnel address.
  • 03AvisLoader statically links c-toxcore and uses the encrypted Tox peer-to-peer network to receive commands and additional payloads without depending on a fixed command-and-control domain.
  • 04Recovered AvisLoader artifacts indicate shortcut-based persistence, a helper referencing UACME method 41 for a possible Windows permission-bypass technique, and a DLL that can filter a process name from process-information results.
  • 05The AvisLoader Command Center allows operators to view client information, schedule shell commands for selected clients, and stage files for delivery to clients over Tox.

Varonis Threat Labs discovered AvisLoader, a Windows loader found on an exposed staging server with a ClickFix lure, support tools, and an operator command center. The lure impersonated a DocuSign signing request and used a fake Cloudflare verification prompt to persuade visitors to copy and run a command. That command retrieved and executed code from a Cloudflare Quick Tunnel address, outside the browser’s normal download process.

AvisLoader uses the encrypted Tox peer-to-peer messaging network to receive commands and additional files, avoiding dependence on a fixed command-and-control domain or server address. Recovered artifacts indicate possible shortcut-based persistence, a helper that references a public Windows permission-bypass method, and a DLL capable of filtering a named process from process-information results. The command center allows operators to view client details, prepare shell commands for selected systems, and stage files for delivery over Tox, while defenders are advised to investigate suspicious pasted commands, altered shortcuts, and unexpected Tox traffic.

  • AvisLoader’s Tox-based control channel can survive server moves because the seller claims operators can copy the controller’s Tox save file and retain the same identity without using a domain.
  • The ClickFix command may evade simple browser-download investigations because the download occurs through a command pasted and run by the user, rather than through the browser’s normal download flow.
  • The executable’s seventeen same-sized, non-executable sections use names associated with multiple well-known packers, apparently to confuse identification rather than prove those packers were used.
  • The presence of the UACME-based helper and process-hiding DLL does not prove successful privilege elevation or that AvisLoader was hidden, so investigators should distinguish recovered capability from demonstrated use.

Researchers found a new Windows program called AvisLoader that can trick people with fake document-signing pages into copying and running a command on their own computer. Once installed, it lets an attacker send instructions and more files through an encrypted peer-to-peer messaging network, making it harder to shut down by blocking a single website or server. Windows users and organizations are affected if someone follows one of these fake verification prompts, and the wider risk is that attackers can keep control of infected computers while hiding their activity.

Ledger confirms spy chip implant behind wallet crypto thefts

Ledger confirmed that a limited number of Ledger Nano X wallets sold through Southeast Asian reseller CryptoBilis contained hidden LTE-enabled hardware implants that captured seed phrases and drained cryptocurrency. Users who bought devices from the reseller should not set them up and should move funds to a new Ledger device using a newly generated seed.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Ledger confirmed that at least one device sold through the Malaysia-based reseller CryptoBilis contained an unauthorized hardware implant, and it linked all reports of unexpected fund drains to devices sold by that reseller.
  • 02Researchers reported that implants found in Ledger Nano X devices captured display output and button presses to identify seed phrases, then sent the information to an attacker through an LTE connection using an eSIM.
  • 03Tibane Labs identified two implant variants, including one that used a smaller replacement battery and another that fit in the space beneath the OLED display while using the original battery.
  • 04Ledger advised customers who bought a device from CryptoBilis not to begin setup and advised users who already set up one of those devices to consider moving their assets to a new Ledger signer with a new seed.
  • 05Ledger’s Genuine Check can verify device authenticity but cannot detect an added implant when the original Secure Element remains intact.

Ledger confirmed that at least one device sold through Southeast Asian reseller CryptoBilis contained an unauthorized hardware implant, after users reported unexpected cryptocurrency losses. The company said all reported fund drains were linked to devices sold through CryptoBilis and described the affected volume as limited. Ledger asked the reseller to stop selling and shipping its devices while it investigates how and when the devices were altered.

Researchers examining compromised Ledger Nano X wallets found implants concealed behind the display or battery that recorded screen output and button presses to identify users’ seed phrases. The implants reportedly used an eSIM and LTE module to transmit the captured information to attackers, and Tibane Labs identified two variants with different power and placement arrangements. Ledger warned CryptoBilis customers not to set up uninitialized devices and advised existing users to consider transferring assets to a new Ledger signer with a new seed, noting that its Genuine Check cannot detect added implants if the original Secure Element remains intact.

  • Ledger’s built-in Genuine Check may still report a device as authentic even when it contains an added implant, as long as the original Secure Element remains intact.
  • In both implant versions examined by researchers, an antenna wire was a visible sign of tampering, despite the malicious circuitry being concealed behind the display or battery.
  • One implant used a purpose-built panelized flexible circuit board rather than hand-soldered wiring, while another fit beneath the OLED display and operated from the original battery.
  • Ledger told authorized resellers and distributors not to restock returned devices, highlighting returned inventory as a handling concern in its response.

Ledger says that some of its crypto wallet devices sold by the Southeast Asian retailer CryptoBilis were secretly altered with hidden parts that could record users’ recovery words and send them to thieves. People who bought these devices may have had cryptocurrency stolen, because those words can give someone full control of the money in a wallet. The incident shows that even genuine-looking security devices can be dangerous if they are changed before customers receive them, especially when bought outside approved sales channels.

Cyberattack shuts down refrigerators at French supermarkets

A cyberattack remotely triggered defrost cycles on Danfoss refrigeration controllers at an estimated 250–350 French supermarkets, spoiling fresh and frozen goods and disrupting multiple major retail chains. Investigators have not confirmed the entry point, though thousands of Danfoss systems—including many in France—are reportedly exposed online and a known authentication-bypass flaw affects the relevant platform.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Attackers reportedly hijacked older Danfoss refrigeration controllers overnight and triggered their built-in defrosting function, raising refrigerator temperatures at French supermarkets.
  • 02French reports estimated that 250 to 350 stores were affected, including stores associated with Intermarché, Coopérative U, Carrefour Market, Monoprix, Leclerc, and Super U.
  • 03Intermarché said fresh and frozen display units were out of service and that it removed products from them as quickly as possible.
  • 04The Paris prosecutor's office opened an investigation into the large-scale incident, including reported food losses and fires.
  • 05CISA warned in May 2025 of CVE-2025-41450, a high-severity authentication-bypass flaw affecting Danfoss AK-SM 8xxA systems used to manage refrigeration and HVAC equipment.

Attackers reportedly remotely defrosted refrigerators at multiple French supermarket chains overnight after hijacking Danfoss refrigeration controllers running older control software. French outlets estimated that 250 to 350 stores were affected, including locations associated with Intermarché, Coopérative U, Carrefour Market, Monoprix, Leclerc, and Super U. The attack raised refrigerator temperatures by triggering a built-in automatic defrosting system, leading to food waste and significant financial losses.

Intermarché said some fresh and frozen display units were out of service and that it removed products from those units as quickly as possible. The Paris prosecutor’s office opened an investigation into the large-scale incident, including reported food losses and fires. The method used to compromise the controllers has not been established, though CISA had previously warned of CVE-2025-41450, a high-severity authentication-bypass vulnerability affecting Danfoss AK-SM 8xxA systems, and research cited in the article found 2,752 Danfoss systems exposed online, including 1,590 in France.

  • A single incident reportedly disrupted refrigeration across several competing French supermarket chains, indicating that shared refrigeration management services or technology can create widespread consequences.
  • The attackers reportedly used the controllers' built-in defrost function rather than a separate physical action, showing how normal equipment functions can be misused to spoil food.
  • Research cited in the article found 2,752 Danfoss systems exposed online, including 1,590 in France, highlighting the scale of potentially reachable refrigeration-management equipment.
  • Although CISA had warned about a high-severity authentication-bypass issue in the affected Danfoss AK-SM 8xxA platform, the article states that there is no confirmation that this flaw was used in the incident.

Attackers remotely caused supermarket refrigerators to warm up overnight at hundreds of stores in France by taking control of equipment used to manage cooling. Chains including Intermarché, Coopérative U, Carrefour Market, Monoprix, Leclerc, and Super U were affected, forcing stores to remove fresh and frozen food and causing waste and financial losses. The case shows that digital break-ins can disrupt physical services people depend on, including the safe storage and supply of food.
