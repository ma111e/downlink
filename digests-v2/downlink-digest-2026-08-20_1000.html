Perfect Authentication, Borrowed From a Real Mailbox

A phishing email passed SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and ARC because it was sent from a genuinely compromised utility company mailbox, showing that perfect authentication does not mean a message is safe. The lure used a fake “attached document” link to an unrelated domain with a human-verification gate that blocked automated scanners.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Two mailboxes at a multi-site services organization received an “EFT payment summary” email in mid-July 2026 that passed SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and ARC validation.
  • 02The message was sent from a real employee mailbox at a real utility company through that company’s Microsoft 365 tenant, indicating the sender account was likely taken over rather than spoofed.
  • 03The email claimed a payment summary was attached, but no attachment existed, and its only link used the display text “REVIEW ATTACHED DOCUMENT HERE” while pointing to the unrelated domain engastando[.]us.
  • 04A fresh screenshot of the destination showed a human-verification interstitial with content delivery network branding and no visible username, password, or login form.
  • 05The landing domain was created in early April 2026, exactly 100 days before the email was sent, and the platform labeled the message as credential theft with 90 percent confidence.

In mid-July 2026, two mailboxes at a multi-site services organization received an email titled “EFT payment summary.” The message passed SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and ARC checks because it was sent from a real employee mailbox at a real utility company through that company’s Microsoft 365 tenant. The article says the most plausible reading is that the employee mailbox had been taken over, not that the sender domain was forged.

The email claimed that a payment summary was attached, but attachment analysis found no attachment to examine. Its only hyperlink displayed the text “REVIEW ATTACHED DOCUMENT HERE” while pointing to the unrelated domain engastando[.]us, including a path spelled hmtl. The platform’s link scanner marked both the plain URL and trailing-slash variant as malicious, and the message was auto-resolved as phishing with two mailboxes affected.

A fresh screenshot of the destination showed a human-verification interstitial with content delivery network branding, but no visible username field, password field, or login form. The article emphasizes that this gate is itself a finding because automated scanners may receive only the challenge page and stop, while a human visitor may proceed. The landing domain was created in early April 2026, exactly 100 days before the message was sent, and the platform labeled the message credential theft with 90 percent confidence based on content and sender-history signals rather than authentication failure.

  • Passing SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and ARC did not reduce the risk in this case because account takeover allowed the attacker to inherit the legitimate sender’s authentication and reputation rather than bypass it.
  • The article separates the observed evidence from the platform’s inference: the record directly establishes a human-verification gate, while the credential-theft conclusion comes from the platform’s content model and message context.
  • The nonexistent attachment was an inspection-time contradiction: the email told recipients to review an attached document, but attachment analysis found nothing and the link actually led to an unrelated website.
  • The human-verification page mattered even without a visible login form because it gave automated scanners little content to evaluate while presenting a familiar legitimacy cue to human recipients.
  • The landing domain’s 100-day age shows how attackers can avoid newly registered domain heuristics by preparing infrastructure long enough before sending the message.

Two people at a multi-site services organization received a fake payment email that looked legitimate because it came from a real employee mailbox at a real utility company that had likely been taken over. The email claimed there was an attached document, but there was no attachment; instead, it linked to an unrelated website that showed a “verify you are human” page, which can stop automated safety checks from seeing what is really behind it. This matters because trusted mailboxes can be abused to make scams look real, putting both the sender’s company and the recipients at risk of stolen sign-ins or payment-related fraud.

Two popular Rust crates arrayref and append-only-vec compromised in Supply Chain Attack

Two widely used Rust crates, arrayref and append-only-vec, were compromised to add a malicious build-time dependency that downloads and executes a remote payload when projects are compiled. Because arrayref has hundreds of millions of downloads, the incident represents a major Rust supply-chain compromise with broad potential impact.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Aikido detected that the Rust crates append-only-vec and arrayref were compromised by adding a dependency on the malicious package proc-macro1.
  • 02The proc-macro1 package downloads a remote payload during the build process and executes it on the developer’s machine, so compiling a project that pulls in either compromised crate can trigger infection.
  • 03The malicious build script targets Linux x86_64, Windows x86_64, macOS x86_64, and macOS aarch64 systems.
  • 04The affected package versions listed in the article are proc-macro-en 1.0.10, proc-macro1 1.0.107, append-only-vec 0.1.9, and arrayref 0.3.10.
  • 05The article lists 23.254.165.112:9089 as the payload delivery host and 23.254.165.112:443 as the command-and-control host passed to the executed payload.

On August 20, Aikido detected that two popular Rust crates from the same maintainer, append-only-vec and arrayref, had been compromised. The attacker added a malicious dependency on proc-macro1, a package that downloads a remote payload during the build and executes it on the developer’s machine. Because the dependency runs at build time, simply compiling a project that pulls in either crate is enough to trigger the infection, without calling any of the crates’ functionality.

The article says this incident is more significant than the previous Onering crate compromise because the affected crates have a much larger download count. append-only-vec is described as having 4 million downloads, while arrayref is described as having 244 million downloads. Aikido calls it the largest Rust crate compromise it has seen by download count.

The compromised crates’ source code appeared clean, with the malicious change limited to a single added dependency line in each manifest. The malicious logic was in the build.rs file of proc-macro1, which Cargo compiles and runs automatically during a build. The script hid its network destinations as base64 fragments, selected payloads for Linux, Windows, and macOS systems, and used 23.254.165.112:9089 for payload delivery and 23.254.165.112:443 as the host passed to the executed payload.

  • The compromised crates’ source code appeared clean because the malicious change was only a single added dependency line in each manifest.
  • The attack was especially dangerous because the malicious code ran during the build process, meaning victims did not need to call any functionality from append-only-vec or arrayref to be affected.
  • The proc-macro1 package was designed to look legitimate by typosquatting proc-macro2 and copying the real crate’s description, author name, and documentation.
  • The Windows payload launcher was intentionally built to keep running after the Cargo build finished, using wscript and a VBS launcher to escape Cargo’s job object.
  • The attacker also published proc-macro-en under the author name daveroundy, mimicking the legitimate maintainer name droundy to make the package appear more credible.

Two popular Rust software packages, arrayref and append-only-vec, were altered so that simply building a project that uses them could download and run harmful code on a developer’s computer. This affects developers and teams whose projects pulled in the compromised versions, even if they never directly used the changed package features. It matters because one of the packages has hundreds of millions of downloads, so a small hidden change could reach many machines through normal software-building work.

The malicious dependency executes during builds, so simply compiling affected projects can run the payload on developer machines, making it urgent for security professionals to review.

BTR Reforged: Weaponizing Defender’s Remediation Driver as a Kernel Operation Primitive

Researchers found that Microsoft Defender’s signed Boot-Time Removal driver, BTR.sys, can be repurposed by an attacker with driver-loading privileges to perform kernel-level file and registry operations without exploiting a bug. The key takeaway is that trusted security components can become powerful living-off-the-land primitives, so defenders need behavioral detection around driver loading, ADS configuration, and unusual system-level changes.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Check Point Research reverse engineered Microsoft Defender’s Boot-Time Removal driver, BTR.sys, and found that its encrypted transaction format can be constructed to make the signed driver perform file and registry operations from kernel mode.
  • 02The driver reads an RC4-encrypted configuration blob from an Alternate Data Stream named :changelist, validates structures with a modified CRC-32 scheme, and supports actions including file deletion, directory deletion, file move, registry key deletion, registry value deletion, and registry value creation or modification.
  • 03The researchers built BTR_CLI, a proof-of-concept tool that extracts BTR.sys from MpEngine.dll, creates valid encrypted transactions, stages the driver as a service, and can trigger execution immediately or at boot.
  • 04The article demonstrates that staging BTR.sys as a Start=1 driver in the Boot Bus Extender group can run it during an early boot window before Defender’s main user-mode service starts, enabling deletion of Defender components and bypassing Tamper Protection in the tests described.
  • 05The article states that Microsoft MSRC did not classify the findings for immediate servicing because the technique requires existing administrative privileges, and recommends detection based on behavior such as ADS creation, unusual driver loading, service registry staging, and System-process file deletion.

Check Point Research reverse engineered Microsoft Defender’s Boot-Time Removal driver, BTR.sys, and its proprietary transaction format. The driver is a Microsoft-signed remediation component embedded in MpEngine.dll and is dropped to disk when Defender needs to perform reboot-time cleanup, such as deleting a locked file. The research found that valid encrypted transactions can instruct the driver to perform file and registry operations from kernel mode without exploiting a vulnerability or relying on memory corruption.

The article describes a proof-of-concept tool, BTR_CLI, that extracts the driver, builds RC4-encrypted configuration payloads, calculates the required integrity checks, stages the driver service, and triggers execution immediately or at boot. The supported actions include deleting files and directories, moving files, deleting registry keys and values, and setting registry values. The researchers state that all analyzed 64-bit Microsoft-signed versions of BTR.sys shared the same hard-coded 256-byte RC4 key and consistent transaction structure across tested Windows versions from Windows 7 through Windows 11 25H2.

The research demonstrates that staging BTR.sys as a Start=1 driver in the Boot Bus Extender group creates a boot-time window where the filesystem is writable but high-level security services have not yet started. In tests, this enabled deletion of Defender components and registry keys, bypassing Tamper Protection and preventing Defender services or drivers from loading later. The article recommends behavioral detection around Alternate Data Stream creation, unusual driver loading, service registry staging with :changelist, and System process file deletion, while noting that MSRC did not classify the issue for immediate servicing because it requires existing administrative privileges.

  • All 18 unique 64-bit Microsoft-signed BTR.sys versions analyzed used the same hard-coded 256-byte RC4 key and the same transaction structure, making the technique reliable across the tested Windows versions from Windows 7 through Windows 11 25H2.
  • BTR.sys returns STATUS_DELETE_PENDING on successful execution, which causes Windows to unload the driver and mark it for deletion rather than leaving it resident in memory.
  • The driver’s built-in BootClean.log artifact can be removed by including a file-deletion action in the same transaction, allowing the driver to create and then delete its own log before unloading.
  • A high-fidelity detection opportunity is Sysmon Event ID 15 for Alternate Data Streams where a .sys file receives a :changelist stream, because that ADS is central to both legitimate BTR.sys operation and the demonstrated abuse.
  • The current BTR_CLI proof of concept creates service registry keys directly and calls NtLoadDriver rather than using the Service Control Manager, so service-key creation with an Args value containing :changelist and no corresponding Event ID 7045 is an abuse indicator described in the article.

Researchers found that a legitimate Microsoft Defender driver, meant to remove threats during a restart, can be misused by someone who already has administrator-level power to delete or change files and Windows settings from a highly trusted part of the system. This affects Windows systems from Windows 7 through Windows 11 versions tested in the article, because the same driver behavior worked across all of them. It matters because attackers could use a built-in, Microsoft-signed component to weaken security tools like Defender without bringing their own suspicious driver, making behavior-based monitoring more important than simply trusting signed Microsoft files.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a specific, technically detailed finding affecting a Microsoft-signed Defender component across many tested Windows versions, with a working proof of concept for kernel-level file and registry operations and EDR/AV neutralization. It is not reported as exploited in the wild and requires existing administrative driver-loading privileges, so it is highly relevant but not in the must-read emergency range.

UAT-10147 deploys SPECTRE: A cross-platform implant with Linux rootkit and BYOVD capabilities

Cisco Talos reports that Chinese-speaking actor UAT-10147 is using a new cross-platform backdoor called SPECTRE to compromise IIS and Linux servers, combining SEO fraud with advanced persistence, credential theft, process injection, BYOVD-based EDR bypass, and a Linux kernel rootkit. Talos also found signs the actor may be using AI-assisted development to build or refine parts of its malware toolkit.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cisco Talos reports that UAT-10147 is a Chinese-speaking intrusion actor targeting IIS and Linux servers with a multi-platform post-exploitation ecosystem that combines SEO fraud monetization with persistence and defense evasion techniques.
  • 02Talos identified SPECTRE as a new cross-platform C backdoor with Windows and Linux variants that support command-and-control operations, file and process management, credential theft, anti-analysis checks, and other post-exploitation functions.
  • 03The Windows version of SPECTRE includes process injection, named pipe impersonation for privilege escalation, browser and Windows credential theft features, and BYOVD capabilities using vulnerable MSI RTCore64.sys or Dell DBUtil_2_3.sys drivers to interfere with EDR visibility.
  • 04The Linux version of SPECTRE can load a kernel rootkit called Specter, disguised as acpi_pad.ko, which supports process hiding, module hiding, privilege elevation, persistence through a systemd service, and signal-based communication with the implant.
  • 05Talos assessed with medium confidence that parts of the Specter Linux rootkit may have been developed with AI assistance, based on source-code documentation style, uniform formatting, and multi-method explanatory comments.

Cisco Talos reports that UAT-10147 is a highly capable Chinese-speaking intrusion actor targeting internet-facing IIS and Linux servers. The actor uses a multi-platform post-exploitation ecosystem that combines SEO fraud monetization with persistence and defense evasion techniques. Talos identified a new cross-platform backdoor called SPECTRE, written in C, with both Windows and Linux variants.

The Windows version of SPECTRE includes runtime API resolution, per-string encryption, anti-sandbox scoring, HTTP-based command-and-control, process injection, privilege escalation, and credential theft capabilities. It can also use BYOVD techniques by loading vulnerable MSI RTCore64.sys or Dell DBUtil_2_3.sys drivers to interfere with EDR visibility. Talos observed one version attempting to read its C2 configuration from an NTFS Alternate Data Stream at C:\Windows\System32\drivers\etc\hosts:cache.

The Linux version of SPECTRE includes 29 commands and can load a kernel rootkit called Specter, disguised as acpi_pad.ko. Specter uses the Linux kernel ftrace framework to hook syscall handlers, supports process hiding, module hiding, UID 0 elevation, and persistence through a systemd service named hardware-monitor.service. Talos also assessed with medium confidence that parts of the Specter rootkit may show signs of AI-assisted development, and the campaign additionally used SEO fraud tools, Potato-family privilege escalation tools, web shells, Meterpreter, Noodle RAT, QuasarRAT, and Gh0stCringe.

  • One SPECTRE Windows sample attempted to load its command-and-control configuration from the NTFS Alternate Data Stream at C:\Windows\System32\drivers\etc\hosts:cache, allowing the actor to change infrastructure without recompiling the implant.
  • The Linux Specter rootkit avoids syscall table patching and instead uses the kernel’s ftrace framework with FTRACE_OPS_FL_IPMODIFY, which the article says creates less noise in kernel integrity checks.
  • The Linux rootkit persistence mechanism uses a systemd service named hardware-monitor.service configured with Before=sysinit.target, causing it to run on every boot before security tooling initializes.
  • Talos connected parts of the SEO fraud tooling to “x神” with medium confidence based on PDB paths in BadIIS samples and a service installer, while other campaign components also used “X” strings such as X-seo and the X-ID web shell header.
  • The campaign showed a specific Vietnamese focus through the ASHX SEO engine’s vn[.]xyz domains, targeting of the Cốc Cốc crawler, and a QuasarRAT Campaign ID containing a derogatory Chinese phrase toward Vietnamese elderly people.

Cisco Talos describes a Chinese-speaking group called UAT-10147 that breaks into public IIS and Linux servers and uses them for fake search results, hidden access, and further control. The group uses a new tool called SPECTRE that works on both Windows and Linux and can steal passwords, hide itself, and shut off or bypass security tools, including by using unsafe drivers on Windows and a hidden low-level component on Linux. This matters because website owners and people who visit affected sites can be harmed, and the group’s mix of custom tools, public tools, and possible AI-assisted development shows that such attacks are becoming faster, stealthier, and harder to spot.

UAT-10147: Chinese-speaking adversary integrates agentic AI into post-compromise operations

Cisco Talos found that the Chinese-speaking cybercrime group UAT-10147 is using agentic AI tools to scale real intrusions against vulnerable Windows and Linux web servers, automating exploitation, reconnaissance, payload deployment, validation, persistence, and troubleshooting. The campaign shows how AI can lower the skill and time needed for complex post-compromise operations while increasing attack volume and efficiency.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cisco Talos identified UAT-10147, a Chinese-speaking cybercrime group, targeting vulnerable Windows and Linux web servers globally across government, education, media, technology, and gaming sectors.
  • 02Talos found a target list on the actor’s command-and-control server containing approximately 170,000 URLs, which the actor split into 17 files of about 10,000 URLs each.
  • 03UAT-10147 used publicly disclosed vulnerabilities and offensive tools including Metasploit, ysoserial, PentestGPT, DeepAudit, and multiple privilege escalation exploits in its intrusion operations.
  • 04On Windows systems, the actor used scripts to download tools and payloads, add IIS directories to Windows Defender exclusions, deploy backdoors such as QuasarRAT, and establish persistence through scheduled tasks or rogue user accounts.
  • 05Talos assessed that UAT-10147’s use of AI-generated exploitation guidance, automation, validation workflows, and operational documentation helps scale complex attacks while reducing the expertise traditionally required for advanced post-compromise operations.

Cisco Talos identified UAT-10147, a Chinese-speaking cybercrime group, targeting vulnerable Windows and Linux web servers globally. Affected systems were located in Brazil, Bolivia, China, Canada, and Vietnam, and belonged to organizations in government, universities, media, technology, and gaming. Talos also found a target list of approximately 170,000 URLs in the actor’s exposed command-and-control infrastructure.

The actor used publicly disclosed vulnerabilities to gain initial access at scale and then deployed malware for SEO fraud, data theft, persistence, and remote control. On Windows, UAT-10147 used batch scripts, certutil downloads, EfsPotato, Windows Defender exclusions, scheduled tasks, rogue users, QuasarRAT, BadIIS, and other implants. On Linux, the actor used a web shell, multiple local privilege escalation exploits, and implants including NoodleRAT, SPECTRE, and Meterpreter.

Talos assessed that UAT-10147 is part of an emerging class of financially motivated intrusion operators using agentic AI systems to operationalize offensive tradecraft at scale. The actor used AI-driven tools and generated materials for exploit refinement, reconnaissance, payload generation, validation, troubleshooting, documentation, and post-exploitation automation. Talos said this shift enables threat actors to scale complex attacks more efficiently while reducing the expertise traditionally required for advanced post-compromise operations.

  • Talos observed a shift from basic AI-assisted scripting to semi-autonomous offensive orchestration, with AI-generated materials covering exploit refinement, troubleshooting, validation, post-exploitation automation, and operational documentation.
  • The actor used HTTP 500 error differences in ASP.NET ViewState attacks to validate stolen MachineKey material without meaningful command execution, making a server error response part of the attacker’s success-checking workflow.
  • The recovered AI-generated guide documented that time-delay tests were ineffective for confirming ViewState command execution because Process.Start() returns immediately, so the actor switched to out-of-band HTTP and DNS callbacks.
  • UAT-10147 used legitimate-looking services and administrative paths for stealth, including webhook.site for callback and exfiltration workflows and a Nacos configuration server as an asynchronous sink for exploitation verification.
  • The actor’s own exposed directory revealed operational details such as a 170,000-URL target list, installed AI offensive frameworks, generated scripts, and even records of an active intrusion, showing that their tooling scale was undermined by poor operational security.

Cisco Talos found a Chinese-speaking cybercrime group called UAT-10147 breaking into vulnerable website servers around the world, including systems used by government, universities, media, technology, and gaming organizations. The group used artificial intelligence tools to help find weak targets, prepare break-in steps, test whether attacks worked, and keep access after getting in. This matters because it shows criminals can use AI to make complicated attacks faster and easier, which could affect many more organizations with exposed or outdated web servers.

It is especially important because Talos documents real-world use of agentic AI for exploitation, validation, payload deployment, persistence, and post-compromise automation, showing a significant operational shift for financially motivated attackers.
