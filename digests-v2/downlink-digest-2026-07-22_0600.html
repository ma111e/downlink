Chick-fil-A discloses data breach after credential stuffing attacks

Chick-fil-A is notifying customers after attackers used stolen third-party credentials in a June credential stuffing campaign to access some Chick-fil-A One accounts. Exposed data may include names, emails, membership/payment identifiers, rewards balances, partial card numbers, and stored contact details, prompting the company to log out affected users, remove payment methods, restore balances, and urge password changes.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Chick-fil-A is notifying an undisclosed number of customers after suspicious login activity led it to discover that some Chick-fil-A One accounts had been hacked.
  • 02The company said unauthorized parties used credentials obtained from a third-party source in an automated attack against its website and mobile application between June 17 and June 19, 2026.
  • 03Information potentially accessed includes customers’ names, email addresses, Chick-fil-A One membership numbers, mobile pay numbers, QR codes, Chick-fil-A credit amounts, the last four digits of credit or debit card numbers, and, if stored, birth dates, phone numbers, and addresses.
  • 04Chick-fil-A told the Texas Attorney General that the breach impacts 2,182 Texans and also sent notification letters to residents of several other U.S. states and the District of Columbia.
  • 05In response, Chick-fil-A logged out impacted accounts, removed payment methods, restored Chick-fil-A One account balances, added rewards to affected accounts, and advised users to change their passwords.

Chick-fil-A is notifying an undisclosed number of customers after some Chick-fil-A One accounts were hacked in recent credential stuffing attacks. The company detected the incident after identifying suspicious login activity in certain accounts. In breach notification letters, Chick-fil-A said unauthorized parties launched an automated attack against its website and mobile application between June 17 and June 19, 2026 using account credentials obtained from a third-party source.

The exposed information may include customers’ names, email addresses, Chick-fil-A One membership numbers and mobile pay numbers, QR codes, Chick-fil-A credit amounts, and the last four digits of credit or debit card numbers. If stored in the compromised accounts, birth dates, phone numbers, and addresses may also have been accessed. Chick-fil-A told the Texas Attorney General that 2,182 Texans were impacted and also sent notification letters to residents of Iowa, the District of Columbia, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, Oregon, Vermont, and Rhode Island.

In response, Chick-fil-A logged out all impacted accounts, removed payment methods, restored Chick-fil-A One account balances, and added rewards to affected accounts as an apology. The company also advised impacted users to change their passwords because the accounts were compromised using stolen credentials. The article notes that Chick-fil-A confirmed a similar credential stuffing incident in March 2023 involving more than 71,000 customers whose personal information was accessed and whose stored rewards balances were used.

  • The incident shows that Chick-fil-A One accounts can be compromised even when the stolen passwords come from a third-party source, making password reuse across services a direct risk for customers.
  • Chick-fil-A’s response of restoring account balances and adding rewards indicates that loyalty credits and rewards balances were a meaningful target or impact of the account takeovers, not just personal profile data.
  • This is the second similar Chick-fil-A credential stuffing incident described in the article, after a 2023 case involving more than 71,000 customers whose personal information and stored rewards balances were accessed.
  • The exposed data was broader than basic contact information, because compromised accounts could reveal membership numbers, mobile pay numbers, QR codes, Chick-fil-A credit amounts, and partial payment card details.

Chick-fil-A is telling some customers that attackers got into their Chick-fil-A One accounts in June by trying email and password combinations stolen from another source. The affected people may have had details seen such as their names, email addresses, membership numbers, mobile pay numbers, QR codes, Chick-fil-A credit amounts, the last four digits of payment cards, and in some cases birthdays, phone numbers, and addresses. This matters because reused passwords can let criminals break into accounts, steal personal and payment-related information, and use or sell it for identity theft or other harmful purposes.

Police Dismantle Kratos Phishing Kit Built to Steal Microsoft 365 Sessions and Bypass MFA

German and US law enforcement dismantled the Kratos phishing-as-a-service infrastructure, taking over 200 servers offline and disrupting a Microsoft 365 session-theft kit used by about 1,800 customers. The kit could bypass ordinary MFA by stealing session cookies, so affected users may need session revocation and phishing-resistant sign-in, not just password resets.
KEY POINTS
  • 01German and US law enforcement took down the core infrastructure of the Kratos phishing kit, and Indonesian authorities arrested the man they say developed and operated it.
  • 02The Frankfurt public prosecutor's cybercrime unit and Germany's Federal Criminal Police Office said they pulled more than 200 servers offline and estimated that about 1,800 paying customers used Kratos to run roughly 15,000 phishing campaigns a month.
  • 03Kratos was designed to steal Microsoft 365 session cookies as well as passwords, allowing attackers to bypass two-factor authentication and access accounts as the user.
  • 04ANY.RUN found that Kratos offered a plain PHP credential-harvesting mode and a Node.js reverse-proxy mode that relayed logins to Microsoft in real time and captured the resulting session.
  • 05Authorities said victims since late 2024 number in the hundreds of thousands across more than 30 countries, with concentrations in Europe and the United States.

German and US law enforcement took down the core infrastructure of Kratos, a criminal phishing kit, while Indonesian authorities arrested the man they say developed and ran it. The Frankfurt public prosecutor’s cybercrime unit and Germany’s Federal Criminal Police Office said they pulled more than 200 servers offline. Investigators estimate about 1,800 paying customers used Kratos to run roughly 15,000 phishing campaigns a month.

Kratos targeted Microsoft 365 logins and was designed to steal session cookies as well as passwords, which the BKA said could let attackers get past two-factor authentication and enter accounts as the user. ANY.RUN found the kit had two modes: a plain PHP page that harvested credentials and a Node.js reverse proxy that relayed logins to Microsoft in real time and captured the resulting session. Microsoft Threat Intelligence tracks the same kit as SneakyLog and linked it to a tax-themed campaign that sent W-2 documents with personalized QR codes to about 100 organizations.

Authorities said victims since late 2024 number in the hundreds of thousands across more than 30 countries, mainly in Europe and the United States. The operators are estimated to have earned more than 300,000 euros since 2024, and stolen Microsoft logins could be used for further phishing, sold to other criminals, or turned into a foothold inside companies. The BKA says Kratos-powered campaigns cannot continue for now, but the takedown did not touch the roughly 1,800 customers or the kit code they already hold.

  • A password reset alone may not remove access if Kratos captured a live Microsoft 365 session, because the article says those sessions can survive the reset and must be revoked separately.
  • ANY.RUN identified a practical hunting signal for Kratos exposure: its login pages almost always load barr.svg and lg.svg and then post credentials to endpoints such as next.php or save.php, with 90% recall and near-zero false positives.
  • The takedown disrupted Kratos’ central servers but did not affect the roughly 1,800 customers or copies of the kit code they may already have, leaving open the possibility that similar campaigns could reappear under a new name.
  • Kratos lowered the barrier for less-skilled criminals by packaging adversary-in-the-middle Microsoft 365 phishing into a franchise-like service with cryptocurrency payments, a website, and a Telegram shop.

Police in Germany and the US shut down more than 200 servers used by Kratos, a paid phishing service, and Indonesian authorities arrested the man they say ran it. The service helped about 1,800 customers run fake Microsoft 365 login campaigns that affected hundreds of thousands of people in more than 30 countries, especially in Europe and the United States. It matters because the stolen logins could let criminals enter work accounts even when extra sign-in checks were turned on, then use those accounts for more scams or to break further into companies.

Trojanized Newtonsoft.Json Fork Hides Game-Rigging Code in a Working Library

A typosquatted NuGet package posing as Newtonsoft.Json was a working trojanized fork designed to manipulate Digitain crash-game results and later exfiltrate rigged outcomes to an attacker-controlled server. The malware only activates in Digitain’s specific backend context, making it difficult for other developers who installed it to notice anything wrong.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cybersecurity researchers discovered a NuGet typosquat named Newtonsoftt.Json.Net that masquerades as the Newtonsoft.Json library and contains code designed to rig live game results on Digitain.
  • 02Seven versions of Newtonsoftt.Json.Net were published to NuGet by the owner MagicalPuff96, and the package has been downloaded about 1,200 times.
  • 03JFrog said the trojanized library activates after JsonConvert.DefaultSettings is initialized and targets servers running Digitain’s FG-Crash crash-game backend.
  • 04Later versions of the package exfiltrate rigged round results to an attacker-controlled server at 185.126.237[.]64:5341 using the header X-Seq-ApiKey: theperfectheist2025.
  • 05Developers are advised to remove the typosquat package, block the command-and-control address, and pin Newtonsoft.Json to a known-good version via packages.lock.json.

Cybersecurity researchers discovered a NuGet typosquat named Newtonsoftt.Json.Net that masquerades as the Newtonsoft.Json library while hiding code designed to rig live game results on Digitain. Seven versions of the package were published to NuGet by the owner MagicalPuff96, and the package has been downloaded about 1,200 times. The package has been unlisted by its owner, but the artifacts remain available for download from the registry.

JFrog said the trojan targets Digitain’s FG-Crash online betting game and, in later versions, exfiltrates rigged round results to an attacker-controlled server using the header X-Seq-ApiKey: theperfectheist2025. The malicious behavior begins only after the host initializes JsonConvert.DefaultSettings, and it can only succeed on systems exposing the target’s specific game backend method. This made the package appear to be a real, working Newtonsoft.Json build for non-targeted users.

The package evolved across three generations, with changes in obfuscation, rigging strategy, and exfiltration path, while version 11.0.11 was reportedly left completely unobfuscated. JFrog said package metadata leaked an internal Digitain repository URL in every version, indicating the author had access to FG-Crash source code. Developers are advised to remove the typosquat package, block the command-and-control address, and pin Newtonsoft.Json to a known-good version via packages.lock.json.

  • The package was unusually targeted because it functioned as a real Newtonsoft.Json build for most users and only attempted its malicious behavior when the host exposed Digitain’s specific FG-Crash backend method.
  • The malicious code used a randomized delay after JsonConvert.DefaultSettings was assigned, which JFrog said helped it avoid detection before triggering its payload.
  • The package metadata leaked an internal Digitain repository URL in all seven versions, which JFrog said indicated the author had access to FG-Crash source code.
  • The attacker appeared to iterate across three generations of the package, changing obfuscation, rigging strategy, and exfiltration behavior before version 11.0.11 was published completely unobfuscated.
  • Unlisting the package removed it from NuGet search results but did not remove the artifacts from the registry, so the package remained available for download.

Researchers found a fake NuGet package that pretended to be the popular Newtonsoft.Json software library but secretly contained code meant to rig results in Digitain’s FG-Crash online betting game. Most people who installed it would see a working library, but the hidden behavior was designed to activate only on Digitain’s specific game backend and later send rigged results to a server controlled by the attacker. This matters because it shows how a package that looks harmless can be used to quietly tamper with real-money game outcomes, and the full extent of the exposure is still unknown.

It is broadly relevant to developers and security teams monitoring package integrity, though the payload appears narrowly targeted at Digitain rather than widely damaging.

Agentic SOC Autonomy: Earn It One Decision at a Time

AI agents in the SOC should earn autonomy gradually, starting with low-risk tasks like enrichment and only moving to automated remediation after their decisions are measurable, auditable, and reversible. The key is to grant trust one decision class at a time, with clear outcome metrics and rollback plans before any agent is allowed to act on its own.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The article argues that autonomy in a security operations center is not a feature to switch on, but a level of trust an AI agent must earn one decision class at a time.
  • 02The author distinguishes between agents that recommend actions and agents that take actions, noting that bad automated remediation can disrupt business operations and damage trust in the security team.
  • 03The article recommends separating agent work into detection, enrichment, correlation, recommendation, and action, then granting trust to each stage independently.
  • 04The author says every agent decision should be logged, attributable, and reconstructable so teams can explain why the agent acted during a post-incident review.
  • 05The article states that automated remediation should not be enabled unless there is a tested, fast, and well-understood rollback plan for when the agent is wrong.

The article argues that an autonomous security operations center should be treated as a destination rather than something teams simply switch on. The author says AI agents must earn trust one decision class at a time, using evidence collected in the organization’s own environment. Vendor promises of agents that triage, investigate, decide, and remediate while analysts sleep are described as compelling but potentially risky in many environments.

A central distinction in the article is between an agent that recommends and an agent that acts. A bad recommendation may only cost a few minutes of review, while a bad automated remediation could revoke a credential, quarantine a production workload, or lock an account at a damaging moment. The author recommends separating agent work into detection, enrichment, correlation, recommendation, and action, then granting autonomy to each stage independently.

The article says AI agents should be instrumented like new employees on probation, with scoped work, reviewed output, and a full paper trail. Every decision should be logged, attributable, and reconstructable so teams can explain why the agent acted during a post-incident review. Before any automated remediation is enabled, teams should have a tested and fast rollback plan, and they should measure outcomes such as adversary resilience and reduced material risk rather than only alert volume or time-to-triage.

  • Treating an AI agent like a new employee on probation gives teams a practical model for expanding its authority: start with scoped work, review its output, and require a paper trail before granting more access.
  • The article frames rollback planning as a prerequisite for automation, not an afterthought, by saying unclear answers about how to reverse a wrong action should keep the agent in recommend-only mode.
  • The author warns that faster ticket closure and lower alert volume can be misleading if they do not also show reduced material risk or improved resilience against adversaries.
  • The fastest payoff from agentic security work may come from the “unglamorous middle” of the workflow, such as enrichment and reducing repetitive analyst toil, rather than from fully autonomous final decisions.

The article argues that companies should not let AI security tools make important decisions on their own right away, even though vendors often promise a fully automated security team. It says these tools should first prove they can do smaller jobs reliably, keep clear records of every choice, and have a fast way to undo mistakes. This matters because a wrong automatic action, like locking an account or stopping a production workload, can disrupt the business and damage trust in the security team.
