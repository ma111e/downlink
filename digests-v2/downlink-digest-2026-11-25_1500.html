CISA warns of critical unpatched flaw in Danfoss refrigeration controllers

CISA warns that Danfoss AK-SM 8xxA refrigeration controllers have an unauthenticated critical buffer-overflow flaw (CVE-2026-129713) that could allow remote code execution, with no patch currently available. Organizations should keep affected controllers off the public internet, segment them from business networks, and use VPNs for necessary remote access.
KEY POINTS
  • 01CISA warned of CVE-2026-129713, a critical 9.8-rated stack-based buffer overflow in Danfoss AK-SM 8xxA refrigeration controllers.
  • 02An unauthenticated attacker could trigger the flaw to restart the controller’s server and potentially execute code with the web server’s privileges.
  • 03No patch is currently available, although Danfoss said the issue will be addressed in an upcoming 5.5.x software release.
  • 04CISA recommends keeping controllers off the public internet, separating control systems from business networks with firewalls, and using up-to-date VPNs for remote access.
  • 05Recent attacks in Europe remotely shut down refrigeration equipment or changed settings at supermarkets, food retailers, and cold-storage facilities.

CISA has warned of CVE-2026-129713, a critical 9.8-rated stack-based buffer overflow affecting Danfoss AK-SM 8xxA Series controllers used by food retailers for commercial refrigeration, HVAC, and lighting. An unauthenticated attacker can trigger the issue, restart the controller’s server, and potentially run code with the web server’s privileges. Danfoss calls the affected line the AK-SM 800A Series, including the 820A, 850A, and 880A models.

No patch is currently available, though Danfoss said an upcoming 5.5.x release will address the flaw and remove the option to disable Strict mode. CISA recommends keeping the controllers off the public internet, separating them from business networks with firewalls, and using up-to-date VPNs for remote access. The warning follows recent European incidents in which attackers remotely shut down refrigeration or changed settings at supermarkets, food retailers, and cold-storage facilities, causing significant losses and disruption.

  • Danfoss plans to remove customers’ ability to disable “Strict mode” in the upcoming 5.5.x release that is intended to address the flaw.
  • The affected controllers can manage not only refrigerated cases and compressor racks but, depending on the model, also store lighting and HVAC, broadening the potential operational impact.
  • The incidents described span multiple European countries and include a supplier for the French national blood service, showing that disruptions have affected both retail refrigeration and cold-storage operations.
  • CISA says the controllers are deployed worldwide, but the number of deployments is not known.

U.S. cyber officials warn that a serious, unpatched weakness affects Danfoss controllers used by supermarkets and convenience stores to run refrigeration, and sometimes heating, air conditioning, and lighting. Someone could use it remotely without logging in to restart the controller or possibly take control of it. This matters because recent attacks in Europe have already shut down or changed refrigeration at supermarkets and food retailers, risking spoiled food, financial losses, and disruption to cold storage.

Australia passes tough legislation requiring big tech to preempt foreseeable harms

Australia passed the Digital Duty of Care Act, requiring online platforms to proactively identify and reduce foreseeable harms, especially to minors, with potentially multibillion-dollar penalties for noncompliance.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Australia’s Parliament passed the Digital Duty of Care Act, creating broad online-safety obligations for online providers.
  • 02The law requires platforms to identify and reduce risks from harmful content, including threats of violence and rape, and to strengthen protections for users under 18 against pornography, suicide, and eating disorders.
  • 03Online providers must complete annual written risk assessments and identify major platform design changes, such as endless scrolling, that may create new harms.
  • 04Users will be able to opt out of algorithmic feeds, and people will be required to prove their age to access pornography.
  • 05U.S. officials criticized the legislation as potentially threatening free speech and requiring American technology companies to alter their services to avoid liability.

Australia’s Parliament passed the Digital Duty of Care Act, a sweeping online-safety law supported by Prime Minister Anthony Albanese. The law could expose major technology companies to billions of dollars in fines if they fail to prevent foreseeable online harms before they occur. Albanese described it as a way to place responsibility on platforms to provide a safer online environment.

Online providers will have to detect and reduce risks from harmful content, including threats of violence and rape, and increase protections for people under 18 from pornography, suicide, and eating disorders. They must conduct annual written risk assessments and identify major design changes, such as endless scrolling, that may introduce new harms; users will also be able to opt out of algorithmic feeds, while pornography access will require age verification. Similar laws exist in the United Kingdom and European Union, but U.S. officials have criticized Australia’s measure over possible effects on free speech and the business models and design of U.S. technology companies.

  • The law shifts responsibility toward preventing reasonably foreseeable harms before they occur, rather than only responding after harmful content or behavior is reported.
  • Platform design choices, including endless scrolling, are treated as potential sources of harm that providers must identify when making major changes.
  • The requirement to let users opt out of algorithmic feeds gives Australians direct control over whether platforms use recommendation systems to select content for them.
  • Australia’s approach follows similar laws in the United Kingdom and European Union, while drawing formal objections from U.S. officials over potential effects on speech and U.S. technology companies’ services.

Australia’s Parliament passed a law that makes online platforms responsible for taking steps to prevent foreseeable harms, including violent material, sexual abuse threats, pornography, and content linked to suicide or eating disorders. Companies must assess risks each year, report major design choices that could create harm, give people a way to avoid personalized feeds, and verify age for pornography access. The law particularly aims to protect children and could lead to billions of dollars in fines, while critics in the United States warn it could restrict speech and force major changes to online services.

  • When a patch cannot be applied immediately, documented temporary measures such as disabling HTTP/2 for some nginx2root flaws or disabling SSH password login for SSHole can reduce exposure while upgrades are prepared.

The AI Threat Horizon: Why Speed, Edge AI and Baselines Are Our Best Defense

AI-driven attackers can execute reconnaissance, privilege escalation, and other intrusion activity at machine speed, making traditional hours-long detection and response cycles insufficient. Organizations should combine edge-based AI detection, cross-environment behavioral baselines, and automated isolation/remediation to stop threats within minutes.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Unit 42 reports observing attackers use AI agents to execute context-aware commands and make real-time decisions during attacks.
  • 02In one observed campaign, a persistent threat actor using AI generated 117 unique scripts in multiple languages and executed more than 8,000 unique commands over 12 days.
  • 03During an agentic AI phase of an observed attack, commands had a median interval of 1.4 seconds, compared with roughly 24 seconds between commands during the human-operated phase.
  • 04The article advocates using AI on endpoints and next-generation firewalls to process telemetry locally and rapidly terminate or isolate malicious activity.
  • 05The article states that combining data from network, endpoint, identity, and cloud sources can establish normal-behavior baselines and support automated detection and response.

Unit 42 reports that AI-driven attackers are moving from theoretical risk to observed real-world activity, using AI agents to issue context-aware commands and make decisions during intrusions. In one observed campaign, a persistent threat actor generated 117 scripts across multiple languages and executed more than 8,000 unique commands in 12 days. In another attack, human-operated commands averaged roughly 24 seconds apart, while the agentic AI phase had a median gap of 1.4 seconds and produced commands without syntax errors.

The article argues that this speed requires detection and response to occur in minutes rather than hours. It recommends using AI directly on endpoints and next-generation firewalls so malicious activity can be detected and stopped locally, including when network connectivity is disrupted. Defenders can also combine network, endpoint, identity, and cloud data to establish normal-behavior baselines, identify rapid reconnaissance and environment-mapping activity, and automatically isolate or remediate threats before systems or data are compromised.

  • The article identifies an attacker’s lack of knowledge about a target environment as a defender advantage: rapid reconnaissance and enumeration can stand out against established patterns of normal activity.
  • Edge-based AI is presented as useful not only for speed but also for continuing local detection when network traffic is disrupted and for avoiding the need to send sensitive data to the cloud for analysis.
  • The observed contrast between error-prone human commands and zero-syntax-error AI-generated commands, combined with much shorter command intervals, suggests that command quality and execution cadence can help distinguish AI-assisted activity from normal operations.
  • The article argues that detecting an intrusion within minutes is still insufficient unless it is paired with immediate automated isolation and remediation, because an AI agent can cause substantial damage during that interval.
  • Rather than replacing existing security tools, the article recommends changing how they are used by connecting endpoint, network, cloud, and identity signals into a unified security operations platform.

The article says criminals are using AI tools to carry out computer break-ins far faster than people can, rapidly searching systems and trying to gain more access. In one case, the attackers created 117 scripts and ran more than 8,000 commands in 12 days, while an AI-controlled phase issued commands about every 1.4 seconds. This affects organizations whose computers, cloud services, employee accounts, and data could be searched, copied, locked, or damaged before human responders can react. The article argues that organizations need faster, automated defenses that recognize unusual activity and immediately limit an intruder’s access.
