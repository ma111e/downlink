Pro-Russian hackers target European officials, offer rewards for personal information

Pro-Russian hacktivist group NoName057(16) published personal data of senior European officials and offered cryptocurrency rewards for further information on them and their families, apparently in retaliation for a 2025 international law-enforcement disruption. Italy is investigating the campaign, while G7 foreign ministers are considering joint responses to pro-Russian cyber threats.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Pro-Russian hacktivist group NoName057(16) published personal information for 18 European politicians, law-enforcement officials, and journalists on a dedicated dark-web site.
  • 02The published profiles included phone numbers, email addresses, social-media accounts, and sometimes home addresses, and the group offered cryptocurrency rewards for further information about targets and their relatives.
  • 03Italian Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani said the campaign targeted him and his family to intimidate him over Italy's support for Ukraine.
  • 04The campaign appears linked to retaliation for a July 2025 multinational law-enforcement operation that took more than 100 NoName057(16) servers offline.
  • 05Italian prosecutors are investigating the publication of the personal information, and Tajani said G7 foreign ministers would discuss recent cyberattacks attributed to pro-Russian groups.

Pro-Russian hacktivist group NoName057(16) published personal information about 18 European politicians, law-enforcement officials, and journalists on a dedicated dark-web site. The information included phone numbers, email addresses, social-media accounts, and in some cases home addresses, while the group offered cryptocurrency rewards for additional details about targets and their relatives. Italian Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani said he and his family were being targeted for intimidation connected to Italy’s support for Ukraine.

The targets reportedly include officials from Eurojust, Europol, Italy’s Postal Police, the German Federal Criminal Police Office, and journalists who investigated Russian hackers and influence operations. The campaign appears to be retaliation for a July 2025 international law-enforcement operation that took more than 100 NoName057(16) servers offline, and several listed individuals were reportedly involved in that investigation. Although the group is primarily known for flooding websites with traffic to make them unavailable, researchers said it has expanded into paid information gathering through the Tor network; Italian prosecutors are investigating, and G7 foreign ministers were expected to discuss possible joint measures.

  • NoName057(16), previously best known for overwhelming websites with traffic, has expanded into paid collection and publication of personal information through a Tor-based platform.
  • Several people named by the group were reportedly involved in the 2025 investigation that disrupted its infrastructure, indicating that law-enforcement personnel became direct targets after the operation.
  • The group continued claiming attacks on European targets after more than 100 of its servers were taken offline in the multinational 2025 operation.
  • Claims by NoName057(16) and another pro-Russian group to have disrupted supermarket refrigeration systems have not been independently verified, and researchers warned against assigning blame without technical evidence.

A pro-Russian hacker group posted personal details about Italian Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani and other European officials, police leaders, and journalists on a hidden part of the internet, while offering cryptocurrency payments for more information about them and their families. The group appears to be retaliating after an international police operation shut down more than 100 of its servers in 2025. This puts the named people and their families at greater risk of threats and intimidation, while showing that cyber groups can target individuals as well as disrupt public services in countries supporting Ukraine.

Alleged AI-aided cyberattack exploits assistant interface to breach German web host

German hosting provider Mittwald said attackers exploited a flaw in its Model Context Protocol server to compromise 56 customer projects, remaining undetected for eight days. The company suspects the intruders used AI to accelerate their activity, though it provided no conclusive evidence beyond rapid actions in its logs.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Mittwald CM Service said attackers exploited a flaw in its Model Context Protocol server, which connects customer AI assistants to its mStudio website and hosting management platform.
  • 02The company reported that 56 customer projects were compromised and that the attacker remained undetected for eight days.
  • 03Mittwald managing director Robert Meyer said logs showed the attacker moving through systems unusually quickly, but the company provided no further evidence that AI tools were used.
  • 04Mittwald said external forensic experts are assisting the investigation, relevant authorities have been informed, and repeated scans had found no additional attacker activity as of Friday.

Mittwald CM Service, a German hosting provider for web agencies, said attackers compromised 56 customer projects after exploiting a flaw in its Model Context Protocol (MCP) server. The server allows customers to connect AI assistants to mStudio, Mittwald’s website and hosting management platform. According to the company, the intruder remained undetected for eight days.

Managing director Robert Meyer said logs indicated the attacker moved through the environment unusually quickly, with some steps occurring seconds apart, and suggested AI tools may have been involved. Mittwald provided no additional evidence separating possible AI assistance from conventional automated scripts, and it remains unclear whether the flaw was in Mittwald’s code or open-source MCP libraries. The company said external forensic experts are assisting, authorities have been notified, and repeated scans had found no additional attacker activity as of Friday.

  • The claimed AI involvement is not established: Mittwald cited unusually fast activity in its logs but did not provide evidence distinguishing AI assistance from conventional automated scripts.
  • The intrusion targeted an interface intended to connect customer AI assistants with hosting-management accounts, making it a compromise path tied to an emerging customer integration feature rather than the hosted projects themselves.
  • The attacker reportedly used disguised access attempts and diversionary tactics while Mittwald was responding to the broader PHP2Shell patching effort.
  • Mittwald’s reported impact was limited to 56 customer projects, and its subsequent forensic reviews and repeated internal scans had not identified further attacker activity as of Friday.

A German company that hosts websites said someone broke into a connection point that lets customers link AI assistants to its website-management service. The intruder accessed 56 customer website projects and went unnoticed for eight days, although the company says later checks found no further activity. The company believes AI may have helped the intruder move unusually quickly, highlighting concerns that these tools could make future break-ins faster and harder to handle.

⚡ Weekly Recap: SSHole Exploited, APT Root RCE, Robot Takeovers, 15K Hacked Sites, and More

This weekly roundup highlights active exploitation of the SSHole OpenSSH flaw, supply-chain compromises of server-update mechanisms, and critical bugs affecting APT, Zyxel switches, and other widely deployed systems. The main takeaway is that unpatched internet-facing services, weak configurations, and compromised update channels remain high-impact entry points for attackers.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Attackers have attempted to exploit CVE-2026-123158, known as SSHole, which can allow sign-ins to passwordless accounts on Linux systems using OpenSSH with musl and PAM disabled, including Alpine Linux’s default configuration.
  • 02A flaw in Debian and Ubuntu’s APT package manager, CVE-2026-109518, can allow an attacker positioned between a host and package mirror to cause installation of an unverified package when HTTP sources and retry settings are used, with package scripts running with elevated privileges.
  • 03Zyxel patched CVE-2026-107441 in GS1900 network switches after attackers exploited it to compromise nearly 1,000 devices and steal configuration files and credentials.
  • 04Unknown attackers used compromised credentials to access OPSSHIELD’s update-distribution infrastructure and distribute a backdoor and data-stealing script through the cPGuard update channel.
  • 05The roundup also reports that roughly 15,579 websites are infected with malicious JavaScript used for credit-card skimming, ClickFix lures, iOS exploitation kits, and malicious redirects.

This weekly cybersecurity roundup reports active attempts to exploit SSHole, an OpenSSH flaw identified as CVE-2026-123158 that can permit sign-ins to accounts without passwords on affected Linux systems using musl with PAM disabled. It also covers a Debian and Ubuntu APT issue, CVE-2026-109518, where an attacker able to intercept network traffic could cause installation of an unverified package under specific HTTP-source and retry-setting conditions. These cases highlight how configuration choices and exposed services can turn relatively limited weaknesses into unauthorized system access.

The article also describes compromised update channels and widespread attacks against connected devices and online services. A trojanized Control Web Panel server component delivered the CWPdoor backdoor, while attackers used OPSSHIELD’s compromised cPGuard update infrastructure to distribute a backdoor and data-stealing script. Other reported activity included exploitation of Zyxel GS1900 switches, malicious JavaScript on about 15,579 websites, phishing campaigns targeting Microsoft 365 and financial accounts, and flaws that could allow control of Reachy Mini robot motors. The overall message is that unpatched software, unsafe updates, forgotten settings, and internet-connected systems continue to create practical opportunities for attackers.

  • CWPdoor is designed to blend into normal web-server activity: it activates only on requests containing special headers and suppresses logs for those requests, leaving no access-log record of the attacker interaction.
  • The APT issue shows that package verification can be bypassed only under a specific combination of HTTP package sources, retry settings, and an attacker able to intercept traffic between a system and its package mirror.
  • Compromised refrigeration controllers were reportedly used as proxy exits and spam senders, demonstrating that internet-connected building equipment can be repurposed as abuse infrastructure.
  • A misspelled email-service domain in a U.S. county’s SPF record authorized at least 32,066 IP addresses to send mail on the county’s behalf, despite the government domain itself remaining registered.
  • Reachy Mini could reportedly be compromised either through a direct attack chain or merely by an operator visiting a malicious web page, with the chained flaws ultimately allowing control of the robot’s motor.

This weekly report describes attackers taking advantage of weak settings, unpatched software, and even compromised software-update systems to get into computers and networks. Those affected include Linux and Windows server owners, banks and financial organizations, website operators, users of password managers and crypto services, and organizations running devices such as network switches, cameras, refrigeration controllers, storage systems, and companion robots. It matters because a small overlooked problem, such as an account with no password or an unsafe update, can let criminals steal passwords and money, secretly control equipment, or disrupt important services.

Default 'admin:admin' Server Password Helps Researchers Hack Two Automakers, Unlock and Start Cars

Researchers found that two automakers' connected-car identity systems exposed an Apache Felix admin console with default admin:admin credentials, enabling takeover of customer accounts and remote vehicle unlock/start commands. The flaw was fixed shortly after disclosure, highlighting the risk of leaving development consoles and default passwords in production.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Researchers Shubham Shah and Sam Curry found that the login systems for two car brands exposed an Apache Felix administrator console using the documented default credentials admin:admin.
  • 02By exploiting differences in how the internet-facing edge and the Java server interpreted web addresses, the researchers reached the otherwise blocked administrator console.
  • 03Access to the identity servers allowed the researchers to change a target account’s email address and password, sign in through the normal app, and obtain the account’s access tokens.
  • 04Using those tokens, the researchers unlocked doors and started the engine only on cars they owned or had permission to test, and the affected United States systems were fixed about two hours after reporting.
  • 05A separate Australian attack used identity-server administrator credentials left in the app’s code for a test setup, and the researchers said the combined attacks could affect cars in Australia, the United States, and Canada.

Security researchers Shubham Shah and Sam Curry found that the customer-login systems behind two car brands’ mobile apps could be taken over through a default administrator password. The affected systems used ForgeRock, now part of Ping Identity, and exposed an Apache Felix administrator console that retained the documented admin:admin credentials. The researchers said they reported the issue in February 2026, and the affected United States systems were fixed roughly two hours later.

Although the console was blocked from the open internet, the researchers used specially formed web addresses that the site’s edge and its Java server interpreted differently to reach it. From the console, they ran code on the identity server, changed a target account’s email address and password, and signed in through the normal app to obtain the same access tokens used by the customer. Those tokens enabled vehicle commands such as unlocking doors and starting an engine, which the researchers tested only on cars they owned or had permission to use. A separate Australian attack used identity-server administrator credentials left in the app’s code for a test setup, and the researchers said the combined attacks could affect cars in Australia, the United States, and Canada.

  • Blocking an administrator page at the internet-facing edge was insufficient because the edge and the server interpreted specially formed web addresses differently, allowing the researchers to reach the page.
  • Taking over an account could be difficult for an owner to notice because changing the email address and password did not log the owner out, and account alerts could be redirected to the researchers’ phone.
  • The issue was a production configuration failure rather than a newly discovered ForgeRock product flaw, so it had no CVE or vendor advisory despite allowing full account and vehicle-command access.
  • The researchers found separate weaknesses in different regions: default administrator credentials on the United States systems and test administrator credentials embedded in the app code for Australian systems.

Researchers found that the customer-login systems for two car brands still used the simple factory-set administrator password “admin:admin.” This let them take over customer accounts and, for connected cars, unlock doors and start engines; they tested those actions only on their own cars or with owners’ permission. The affected systems in the United States were fixed about two hours after the report, but the case shows that a forgotten default password can put large numbers of car owners and their vehicles at risk.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a concrete, high-impact finding in connected-car identity systems that enabled customer-account takeover and remote unlock and engine-start commands. Although the affected United States systems were fixed quickly and there is no reported malicious use, the default-credential and path-parsing bypass issues are broadly relevant to teams operating ForgeRock/PingIDM.
