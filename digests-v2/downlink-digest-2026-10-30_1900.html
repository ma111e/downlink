Silent Push says its infrastructure tracking identified hundreds of high-risk Binance-themed domains and links campaigns to Lazarus and Contagious Interview activity.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The February 2025 Bybit theft began after North Korean operators reportedly tricked a Safe{Wallet} engineer into running a fake stock-trading project that contacted getstockprice[.]com.
  • 02About two weeks after gaining that foothold, the operators planted code in the wallet interface used by Bybit’s signers, according to Bybit.
  • 03Silent Push linked bybit-assessment[.]com to a known North Korean operator persona and said the domain was registered hours before the Bybit theft.
  • 04Silent Push reported that North Korean operators used three U.S.-registered front companies—BlockNovas, Angeloper Agency, and SoftGlide—to deliver malware to crypto developers through fake job interviews.
  • 05As of October 30, Silent Push said its feeds contained 206 Binance-named domains rated at its maximum risk score, with at least 64 returning live pages in web scans that week.

North Korean-linked cryptocurrency theft operations begin with publicly accessible infrastructure such as lure domains, fake projects, and fake job opportunities, according to Silent Push. The February 2025 Bybit theft reportedly began when operators tricked a Safe{Wallet} engineer into running a fake stock-trading project that contacted getstockprice[.]com. About two weeks later, they used that access to plant code in the wallet interface used by Bybit’s signers. Binance has also attributed a $6.3 billion theft to a compromised DevOps engineer’s laptop, a role that North Korean operators have targeted for years.

Silent Push said it linked bybit-assessment[.]com to a known North Korean operator persona and found that the domain was registered hours before the Bybit theft. In a separate April 2025 investigation, the company identified BlockNovas, Angeloper Agency, and SoftGlide as U.S.-registered front companies used to deliver malware to crypto developers through fake job interviews; the FBI seized the BlockNovas domain that week. Silent Push stated that, as of October 30, its feeds listed 206 Binance-named domains at its highest risk score, with at least 64 returning live pages during that week’s scans. The company argues that identifying this infrastructure early can allow defenders to block lures before targets open them.

  • The article emphasizes that the visible on-chain theft is the final stage of an operation that begins earlier with public web infrastructure such as newly registered lure domains.
  • A single compromised engineer can provide a path to code used by high-value transaction approvers, as described in the Bybit incident.
  • Fake job interviews and U.S.-registered front companies are presented as a repeatable way to reach cryptocurrency developers, rather than relying only on direct technical intrusion.
  • The reported volume of Binance-themed domains, including dozens of live pages, indicates that defenders can look for and block impersonation infrastructure before a prospective target interacts with it.

The article says North Korean-linked groups prepare cryptocurrency thefts by creating fake websites, fake companies, and fake job opportunities that trick developers into running harmful software. In the February 2025 Bybit theft, a developer was fooled by a fake stock-trading project, helping attackers later alter the wallet website used to approve transfers. Crypto companies, their employees, and people whose money is held on those platforms can be affected when attackers gain access this way. The broader concern is that these scams can be identified and blocked at the website stage, before someone is tricked into opening them.

Binance’s $6.3 Billion Hack Puts Developer Machine Security in Focus

Binance says its $6.3 billion incident may have begun with a compromised DevOps laptop and led to unauthorized withdrawal requests through signing infrastructure, though the root cause remains under investigation and no private-key leak has been confirmed. The case underscores how compromised developer tools can expose credentials and access paths to critical systems.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Binance estimates that its October 29 security incident affected approximately $6.3 billion in assets and initially described a possible path from a compromised DevOps engineer’s laptop to unauthorized withdrawal transactions.
  • 02Binance later reported additional breach points, paused withdrawals, said deposits were unaffected, and reported that partners had frozen approximately $3 billion.
  • 03Binance said it found no evidence of a wallet private-key leak, while TRM Labs assessed that attackers gained access to signing infrastructure.
  • 04TRM Labs reported that the Ethereum activity included 50 signed EIP-7702 authorizations across consecutive transaction counters, and Elliptic reported that a delegation to an attacker-controlled contract remained active for further fund sweeps.
  • 05The article cites recent compromises involving Nx packages, Nx Console, OVHcloud CLI releases, and a Claude Code extension as examples of developer tools being used to steal credentials or establish persistent access.

Binance estimates that its October 29 security incident affected approximately \(6.3 billion in assets. Its initial investigation described a possible path from a compromised DevOps engineer’s laptop to an internal service container and unauthorized withdrawal transactions, although Binance later identified additional breach points. Withdrawals were paused, deposits remained unaffected, and Binance said roughly \)3 billion had been frozen with partners’ assistance.

Binance said it found no evidence of a wallet private-key leak, while blockchain researchers reported activity consistent with misuse of signing infrastructure. TRM Labs described 50 signed EIP-7702 authorizations covering consecutive transaction counters, and Elliptic reported that an attacker-controlled Ethereum delegation remained active for additional fund sweeps as funds arrived. The article frames the incident alongside recent compromises of developer packages, IDE extensions, and command-line tools, emphasizing that developer machines can expose credentials, persistent access, and pathways to sensitive internal systems.

  • Protecting wallet private keys alone may not prevent unauthorized transfers, because an attacker who can manipulate requests sent to legitimate signing systems can obtain valid signatures for unauthorized operations.
  • The reported EIP-7702 delegation remained active after the first transfer, allowing further sweeps when new funds arrived, showing that a single authorized change can create an ongoing risk rather than a one-time transaction.
  • A malicious software release can have lasting effects even when it is available briefly: the affected Nx Console version was published for about 11 minutes but installed a persistent macOS backdoor.
  • Removing a compromised developer extension may not fully remediate an incident, because the trojanized Claude Code extension created standalone persistence artifacts and attempted to establish remote access.
  • Installation histories are important during incident response because updating a package or extension later can hide evidence that a machine previously ran an affected version.

Binance says a security incident may have started with a DevOps engineer’s laptop and led to unauthorized transfers totaling about $6.3 billion, although its investigation is still ongoing. The company says it found no evidence that the secret keys controlling its wallets were stolen, but attackers may have gained access to systems that could approve transfers. The incident affects Binance, its customers, and partners helping freeze stolen funds, while showing that harmful software hidden in everyday coding tools can give criminals a path into important company systems.

Achieving Cloud Readiness Takes More than Tabletops

Cloud breach readiness requires continuous collection, normalization, and long-term retention of forensic data across cloud and SaaS environments—not just incident-response plans and tabletop exercises. A centralized forensic data lake helps teams investigate and respond quickly without critical visibility gaps.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Incident response plans and tabletop exercises are important, but they have limited value during a cloud breach if the necessary forensic data and tools are unavailable.
  • 02Cloud and SaaS forensic data is difficult to prepare because sources use differing and changing formats, generate large volumes of data, retain logs for varying periods, and require specialized staffing and expertise.
  • 03SIEMs are valuable security tools but are not designed to collect and make useful the large volume of cloud forensic data needed for investigations, leading many organizations to limit data volume, variety, and retention.
  • 04Forensic data readiness combines continuous collection, normalization, and enrichment of cloud and SaaS data with a centralized forensic data lake for investigation and response.
  • 05Centralized, retained forensic data can eliminate blind spots, preserve historical evidence, reduce complexity across multiple sources, and support threat hunting and incident response.

Cloud breach readiness requires more than incident-response plans and tabletop exercises. Organizations also need the forensic data and tools required to investigate a real incident across their cloud and SaaS environments. Without that information, mock exercises and emergency playbooks have limited value when a breach occurs.

Collecting forensic data is difficult because cloud and SaaS sources use many changing formats, generate large data volumes, and retain logs for different periods. Organizations may also lack the staff and specialized expertise needed to manage, normalize, and retain this data. Existing SIEMs are valuable but often cannot collect and preserve the volume, variety, and duration of cloud forensic data needed for full investigations.

The article recommends continuous collection, preprocessing, normalization, and enrichment of security events, audit logs, configurations, and other records before an incident. This data should be stored in a centralized forensic data lake that supports investigation and response across the cloud environment. Such readiness can reduce blind spots, preserve historical evidence, support threat hunting, and help analysts investigate and respond more quickly.

  • Under the cloud shared-responsibility model, organizations may not be able to obtain critical forensic information from providers when an incident is already underway, so that data must be collected and stored in advance.
  • Forensic blind spots created by fragmented cloud-data collection may remain undiscovered until a real incident forces investigators to reconstruct what happened.
  • Cloud readiness requires preserving more than basic event logs, including audit records and configurations, so investigators can reconstruct past activity and effectively "rewind time."
  • A centralized forensic data lake can make data from many cloud and SaaS providers usable for investigations by normalizing and enriching inconsistent source records before an incident occurs.

The article says companies using cloud services and online business software need more than practice drills to prepare for a break-in. They should continuously save and organize records of activity from all of those services, because providers may keep those records only briefly and the information may be missing when an incident occurs. Without those records, security teams may be unable to determine what happened or respond quickly, leaving organizations with major blind spots as cloud-based threats become more complex.

This is an evergreen best-practice perspective on collecting, retaining, and centralizing cloud and SaaS forensic data. It reports no specific incident, active threat, vulnerability, or urgent remediation action.

Why With Ransomware Response, Knowledge is Power

Organizations can improve ransomware resilience by validating cloud log coverage, creating detections for early attack stages, and retaining searchable logs for rapid incident response. Automated cloud forensics can help identify ransomware activity before encryption or data destruction occurs.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Ransomware actors may exploit publicly available cloud resources and compromised credentials to access an organization’s cloud assets.
  • 02Effective ransomware response requires the right logs to be configured, stored, pre-processed, and easily queried before an incident occurs.
  • 03Default cloud logging may not provide sufficient visibility, so organizations should simulate attacks and review the resulting logs to validate coverage.
  • 04Organizations should create detections for stages of ransomware activity, including initial access, enumeration, lateral movement, collection, data exfiltration, and impact.
  • 05Automated forensic tools can continuously hunt properly collected and retained cloud logs for early signs of ransomware activity.

Ransomware remains a significant threat to organizations as expanding cloud use increases the systems and data that attackers can target. Attackers may exploit publicly available cloud resources or compromised credentials to access cloud assets. The article argues that understanding how an attack begins and progresses helps organizations make better decisions and defend themselves earlier.

Effective response depends on having the right cloud logs configured, retained, prepared for searching, and available before an incident occurs. Default logging may not provide enough visibility; for example, missing Amazon RDS data events or database-level logs can make it difficult to determine how a compromise occurred and what happened afterward. Organizations should validate their log coverage, simulate attacks to assess visibility, build detections for stages from initial access through data theft and impact, and use automated forensics to continuously hunt for early ransomware activity.

  • Turning on default cloud logging is not enough: organizations need to verify that the collected records contain the specific events and fields needed to reconstruct an incident.
  • Simulating an attack and examining the resulting logs can reveal undocumented cloud event names or log fields and test whether visibility is sufficient for investigation.
  • For an Amazon RDS compromise, missing data-event and database-level logging can severely limit investigators’ ability to determine how the compromise occurred and what actions followed.
  • The most valuable detection opportunity is before encryption or data destruction, during earlier activity such as account access, discovery, movement between systems, data collection, and data theft.

The article explains that ransomware criminals can take advantage of poorly configured cloud services or stolen account passwords to reach an organization’s online systems and data. Organizations of all sizes are affected as they store more information in the cloud. Keeping detailed records of activity in those systems can help teams spot warning signs early, understand what happened, and reduce the damage before files are locked, destroyed, or taken.

From packaging to declaring: the next generation of Mac app deployment

Jamf’s App Lifecycle Management Engine, bundled with Self Service+ 2.33.0, enables declarative installation and automatic updating of vendor-direct non-App Store Mac apps through blueprints. The signed ALME daemon verifies checksums and signatures, and Jamf Pro 11.33.0 grants it Full Disk Access for updates in protected macOS folders.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Jamf’s App Lifecycle Management Engine (ALME) lets administrators add cataloged non-App Store Mac apps to blueprints, set maintenance windows, and deploy them for automatic installation and updates.
  • 02ALME downloads apps directly from vendors over TLS and checks each downloaded binary’s checksum and developer signature before installation.
  • 03Self Service+ 2.33.0 includes the ALME binary and background components, which remain idle and install nothing until an App Catalog app is added to a blueprint.
  • 04Jamf Pro 11.33.0 automatically provides Full Disk Access through a PPPC profile for Self Service+, allowing ALME to update apps in protected macOS folders such as Desktop, Documents, and Downloads.
  • 05Without Full Disk Access, ALME can still install and update applications in /Applications, but updates to apps stored in protected folders fail.

Jamf describes a declarative approach to Mac application deployment in which administrators specify which apps should be present, while devices work to install and maintain them. Its App Lifecycle Management Engine (ALME) provides a growing catalog of non-App Store Mac apps that can be added to blueprints with administrator-defined maintenance windows. Apps are downloaded directly from vendors, installed automatically, and kept current; removing an app from a blueprint stops updates but leaves the installed app in place.

ALME verifies every download by checking its checksum and developer signature, and it skips installation if verification fails. Self Service+ 2.33.0 includes ALME as a background component, with ALME.app located inside Self Service+ and the ALME and alme-agent processes launched through specified macOS service files. Jamf Pro 11.33.0 automatically grants Full Disk Access through a PPPC profile for Self Service+, allowing ALME to update apps in protected folders; without that permission, it can still update apps in /Applications but not in Desktop, Documents, or Downloads.

  • Removing an app from a Jamf blueprint does not remove the app from a Mac; it only stops future updates for that app.
  • ALME’s Full Disk Access is inherited through its Self Service+ host application, so Jamf can grant the permission using a PPPC profile for Self Service+ rather than a separate profile for ALME.
  • Organizations may need to recognize ALME and alme-agent as expected Jamf components in endpoint-security tools, including their JAMF Software signature and team ID 483DWKW443.

Jamf has added a new tool to its Mac management software that can download, install, and keep approved non-App Store apps updated automatically. It affects organizations that use Jamf to manage Mac computers, especially their IT and security teams, because the tool runs in the background and may be identified as new software by security products. The tool checks that downloads come from the expected developer and have not been changed, while giving administrators control over when updates occur.
