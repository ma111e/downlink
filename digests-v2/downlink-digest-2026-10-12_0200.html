No Context, No Crown: Why Your AI SOC Is Starving for Context

AI SOC effectiveness depends less on model sophistication than on pre-engineered, investigation-grade context: normalized, correlated, retained, and attributed telemetry. Mitiga argues that feeding agents raw logs raises costs and produces unreliable decisions, while its Helios AIDR platform uses a contextualized security data lake to automate investigation and triage.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The article argues that AI security operations are constrained more by the quality and organization of their data than by the sophistication of the AI model.
  • 02It defines investigation-grade context as data that is normalized across systems, correlated into activity timelines, retained for more than 1,000 days, and attributed to the responsible human or non-human identity.
  • 03The author says that sending raw logs to AI agents makes investigations more expensive because the agents must repeatedly reconstruct context and consume additional processing tokens.
  • 04The article cites vendor activity by CrowdStrike, Torq, Databricks, and Cribl as evidence that security operations and data-platform vendors are investing in context and data capabilities.
  • 05Mitiga states that its Helios AIDR product uses engineered context to autonomously triage alerts and provide structured analyses.

The article argues that an AI-powered security operations center is limited less by the intelligence of its model than by the quality of the data it receives. It says raw logs from cloud, SaaS, identity, third-party, and AI services are commonly fragmented across incompatible systems, making it difficult for an agent to reconstruct an attack path. The author calls the preparation of that information “context engineering” and presents it as the key layer for dependable machine-speed security decisions.

According to the article, investigation-grade context must be normalized into a consistent format, correlated into cross-platform activity timelines, retained for more than 1,000 days, and attributed to human, non-human, and agent identities. It argues that feeding agents raw logs increases both storage and AI processing costs because agents must repeatedly rebuild context, while upstream enrichment provides smaller, higher-signal inputs. Mitiga positions its Cloud Security Data Lake and Helios AIDR as an agentic substrate built on this engineered context, claiming that Helios autonomously triages alerts and produces structured investigation analyses.

  • A less capable AI agent can outperform a state-of-the-art model when it receives a small, trusted, connected record of activity rather than raw logs from many incompatible systems.
  • The article identifies data retention as an AI decision-quality issue: investigations can fail when relevant records expire after 90 to 180 days, even if the AI model itself is capable.
  • The proposed practical test for AI SOC vendors is whether they prepare and connect the evidence before the AI is invoked, rather than simply pointing an agent at existing logs.
  • The article argues that preparing data once upstream can reduce both storage costs and AI processing costs by preventing agents from repeatedly rebuilding the same investigation context.

The article argues that AI tools used by security teams cannot make reliable decisions if they are given scattered, incomplete records from different systems. Organizations using cloud services, workplace apps, login systems, outside services, and AI tools are affected because an intruder’s actions may be spread across all of them. The author says companies need to organize, connect, keep, and identify this information before giving it to AI, or the AI may make fast but wrong decisions while increasing costs.
