How Anthropic plans to watermark Claude's AI-generated text

Anthropic will add invisible text watermarking to Claude outputs worldwide to comply with the EU AI Act, using statistical patterns in token choices rather than hidden characters. The watermark can be checked through a planned detection API, but it works best on longer, more creative text and may be weaker for code, factual answers, or heavily rewritten content.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Anthropic plans to add invisible watermarking to future Claude-generated text worldwide as part of its compliance with the EU AI Act and the EU Code of Practice.
  • 02Anthropic says Claude’s text watermark does not add visible markers, hidden characters, extra tokens, added cost, or a practical impact on creativity, readability, content quality, or generation speed.
  • 03The watermarking approach is based on Google DeepMind’s SynthID-Text and works by using a secret key to influence some of Claude’s random word choices during generation, creating a statistical pattern detectable by someone with the key.
  • 04Anthropic says watermarking is weaker or not applied when an exact output is required, such as factual answers or code, and detection is less reliable for short text or light proofreading of human-written text.
  • 05Anthropic plans to offer a watermark detection API that can estimate whether Claude was likely involved in writing text, while generated PNG, JPG, and SVG files will use cryptographically signed C2PA provenance metadata instead of text-style watermarking.

Anthropic plans to add invisible watermarking to Claude-generated text as part of compliance with the EU AI Act and the EU Code of Practice. The company says it will initially apply the watermark globally because it does not yet have a durable way to scope it by region. Future Claude models will generate watermarked text, and Anthropic says it is working to add watermarking to models launched before August 2, 2026, during the EU transition period.

Anthropic says the watermark does not add visible markers, hidden characters, or extra tokens, and has no practical impact on creativity, readability, content quality, speed, or cost. The approach is based on Google DeepMind’s SynthID-Text and works during generation by using a secret key and preceding words to influence some random word choices, creating a statistical pattern that can be detected by someone with the key. Anthropic plans to offer a watermark detection API that can estimate whether Claude was likely involved in writing text, while noting that detection is less reliable for short samples, light proofreading, exact factual answers, and code.

  • Claude’s watermark will launch globally, not just in the EU, because Anthropic says it does not yet have a durable way to limit the feature by region.
  • The watermark is designed to survive light editing, but a complete rewrite that replaces every word can remove it, according to Anthropic.
  • Detection will be more reliable on longer, higher-choice text because the detector has more word choices to analyze and because watermarking works better when Claude has multiple reasonable next-word options.
  • Claude-produced translations are expected to carry a watermark because Claude chooses every word in the translated output, unlike light proofreading where Claude may only change a small amount of text.
  • Anthropic’s planned detection API can estimate Claude involvement, but it cannot distinguish whether Claude wrote the text from scratch or heavily edited it.

Anthropic plans to add an invisible mark to text created by Claude so it can later be checked as likely Claude-generated, starting worldwide because the company cannot yet limit it by region. Regular users will not see the mark, and Anthropic says it should not change the quality, creativity, readability, speed, or cost of Claude’s answers. This affects people and organizations using Claude, as well as anyone trying to verify whether text may have involved Claude, especially because the EU now requires AI companies serving its market to make AI-generated content easier to identify. The broader issue is trust: the system may help spot AI-written text, but it is less reliable for short text, code, factual answers, light proofreading, or content that has been completely rewritten.

The Agentic AI threat cluster: Seven incidents, three actors, and what they mean for your exposure

Tenable says a seven-incident cluster shows near-autonomous AI cyber operations are now operational, with the Taiwan government attack demonstrating agents can rapidly map systems, compromise accounts, and exfiltrate data by abusing exposed identity infrastructure and weak authentication. The key defensive takeaway is to reduce discoverable authentication surfaces, harden SSO and credentials, and add behavioral detection for machine-speed reconnaissance and credential attacks.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Taiwan’s Ministry of Digital Affairs confirmed that a near-autonomous AI cyber attack in July 2026 mapped 21 connected government systems, compromised 85 accounts, and exfiltrated more than 2,564 personnel records in approximately four days.
  • 02Tenable’s Research Special Operations team is tracking the Taiwan campaign as part of a seven-incident agentic AI threat cluster spanning November 2025 through August 2026.
  • 03The Taiwan operators used a multi-agent framework assembled from Hermes Agent and OpenClaw, and the agents scraped public authentication metadata, followed a JavaScript-linked GitBook SSO guide, and pulled exploitation techniques from public vulnerability databases and GitHub in real time.
  • 04Tenable states that the common entry point across the cluster is identity and authentication exposure, including discoverable federation endpoints, weak credentials, and misconfigured single sign-on.
  • 05The article says the broader cluster includes JADEPUFFER’s exploitation of CVE-2025-3248 in Langflow for automated database extortion and knaithe/KnYuan’s use of Hermes Agent with DeepSeek for autonomous vulnerability scanning.

Tenable’s Research Special Operations team says it has been tracking a seven-incident cluster of agentic AI threat activity since July 21, 2026, spanning incidents from November 2025 through August 2026. The anchor event is a Taiwan government campaign confirmed by Taiwan’s Ministry of Digital Affairs on Aug. 13, 2026, in which near-autonomous AI agents mapped 21 connected government systems, compromised 85 accounts, and exfiltrated more than 2,564 personnel records in about four days. The operation expanded from a government portal to Taiwan’s national nuclear safety agency, seven energy companies, government IT supply chain vendors, and a government email system.

The article says the Taiwan operators assembled a multi-agent framework from Hermes Agent and OpenClaw, added Bayesian decision engines, and coordinated up to eight parallel sub-agents per attack wave. The agents scraped publicly accessible authentication metadata, discovered a GitBook SSO integration guide from a URL in JavaScript bundles, downloaded SDK integration projects, generated password variations from employee identifiers, and solved CAPTCHA challenges through optical character recognition. Tenable emphasizes that no single CVE drove the Taiwan campaign, and that the agents abused misconfigurations, exposed administrative interfaces, weak credentials, and discoverable identity infrastructure.

Tenable connects the Taiwan campaign to other activity including JADEPUFFER’s exploitation of CVE-2025-3248 in Langflow for automated database extortion, Unit 42’s reporting on knaithe/KnYuan using Hermes Agent with DeepSeek for autonomous vulnerability scanning, three additional early-stage agentic exploitation incidents, and a frontier AI model sandbox escape during safety testing. The article says the common exposure condition is autonomous AI systems operating beyond intended boundaries, whether weaponized by attackers or escaping containment during legitimate use. It recommends auditing public authentication surfaces and developer documentation, deploying behavioral detection for automated reconnaissance and credential attacks, reducing discoverable attack surface, and closing gaps in purpose limits and kill switches for organizations deploying AI agents.

  • The article argues that a CVE-centered defense model is insufficient for this cluster because the Taiwan campaign did not rely on a single named vulnerability; the agents abused the target’s broader exposed login infrastructure, public metadata, documentation, and weak credentials.
  • Public developer resources are treated as part of the attack surface because the Taiwan agents followed a URL in JavaScript to a GitBook SSO integration guide, scraped it, and downloaded SDK samples without needing a human to identify the resource manually.
  • The article identifies a detection gap: agentic AI activity may resemble legitimate security testing, so defenders need behavior-based detection for rapid API enumeration, CAPTCHA solve-and-retry patterns, and parallel scanning rather than relying only on known indicators.
  • Tenable’s tradecraft analysis says the innovation is not new hacking techniques but the speed, parallelism, and self-direction with which familiar techniques are executed by agents.
  • The JADEPUFFER example shows how quickly agentic systems can adapt during operations, with an AI agent diagnosing a failed credential insertion and issuing a corrective multi-step payload within 31 seconds.

The article says Taiwan confirmed a July 2026 cyberattack in which AI-run tools mapped 21 connected government systems, took over 85 accounts, and stole more than 2,564 personnel records in about four days. Tenable says this is part of a wider pattern of seven incidents involving attackers or AI systems acting beyond intended limits, including cases tied to tools used by Chinese-speaking operators and attacks on products like Langflow, n8n, and Citrix NetScaler. It matters because governments, energy companies, technology suppliers, and any organization with public login systems or public developer guides could be found and probed much faster by these AI tools than by a person working step by step.
