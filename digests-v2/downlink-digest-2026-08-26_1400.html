Top Minimus alternatives in 2026

Minimus images will stop receiving updates when its registry goes offline on October 22, 2026, so users need to migrate before frozen images accumulate CVEs. The article recommends Aikido Security as the preferred alternative because it patches existing base images as drop-in replacements rather than forcing a move to a vendor-specific distro.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Minimus is shutting down, and its registry at reg.mini.dev will go offline on October 22, 2026, after which no new image updates or patches will be available.
  • 02Already pulled Minimus images will keep running, but they will freeze in their current state and accumulate newly discovered vulnerabilities over time.
  • 03The article compares Aikido Security, Chainguard, Docker Hardened Images, RapidFort, and Echo as alternatives for teams migrating away from Minimus.
  • 04The article names Aikido Security as its top pick because it patches the base images teams already use, provides drop-in replacements, and includes SBOM, VEX, and SLSA provenance with each image.
  • 05The article says some alternatives require moving to a vendor-controlled distribution or repeatedly rolling forward to new image versions, which can create migration, testing, and approval work.

Minimus is shutting down, and its registry, reg.mini.dev, will go offline on October 22, 2026, after a 60-day maintenance period that began on August 24. Images that have already been pulled will keep running, but there will be no new updates after the shutdown. The article says this creates a migration deadline for teams running Minimus images because frozen images will continue to accumulate newly discovered vulnerabilities.

The article compares Aikido Security, Chainguard, Docker Hardened Images, RapidFort, and Echo as alternatives to Minimus. It presents Aikido Security as the top pick because it patches the base images teams already use, provides drop-in replacements, and opens a pull request for the change through AutoFix. The article says Aikido keeps rebuilding patched images, includes SBOM, VEX, and SLSA provenance, covers OS-level CVEs under a defined patch-creation SLA, and continues patching versions even after end-of-life.

A key technical issue discussed is the difference between backporting fixes into pinned versions and rolling forward to new image versions or digests. The article says roll-forward models can require repeated testing and approval, and digest-pinning can stop patches from reaching deployed images. It also notes that hardened images do not cover everything, so teams still need security coverage for application code, dependencies, infrastructure-as-code, secrets, and cloud posture.

  • A replacement that backports fixes into pinned image versions can avoid the repeated re-testing and approval cycle created when vendors only remediate by shipping a new image digest.
  • Digest-pinning improves reproducibility, but the article notes that in roll-forward models it can also prevent security patches from reaching deployed images.
  • The article treats security coverage after upstream end-of-life as a major selection factor, because older or pinned bases may otherwise keep known critical and high vulnerabilities indefinitely.
  • Runtime profiling can shrink images further, but the article warns that it is brittle because code paths not observed during profiling may be removed and later fail in production.
  • The article argues that hardened images alone are insufficient because they do not cover application code, dependencies, infrastructure-as-code, secrets, or cloud posture.

Minimus, a service that provides ready-made software building blocks, is shutting down its online registry on October 22, 2026, so people and companies using its images will stop getting new fixes. Anything already downloaded will keep running, but newly discovered security problems in those images will no longer be repaired. This matters because teams that rely on Minimus now have to move to another provider, and the wrong choice could force them to change how their software is built and tested all over again.

Behind the Leak

Threat actors quickly repackaged the GTA VI Cyberleek hype into fake playable builds that profile visitors, report data through Telegram bots, and silently install abused ScreenConnect remote-access clients. The investigation links multiple lure sites through a shared playbook rather than shared infrastructure, with IOCs for detection.
KEY POINTS
  • 01On August 18, 2026, gameplay footage and map assets from GTA VI began circulating online under the name Cyberleek, and fake copies of a supposed playable Cyberleek build soon appeared on torrent sites, Telegram, and websites.
  • 02The Cyberleek leak campaign was tied to a Solana token called $CYBERLEEK, with a liquidity pool seeded with 730 million tokens and 330 SOL and a separate unlocked allocation of 270 million tokens.
  • 03Samples from cyberleek[.]click and cyberleek[.]info installed a legitimate but abused ScreenConnect remote access client configured for unattended access to an attacker-controlled relay at gg.noktaarts[.]com.
  • 04The lure pages profiled visitors using services such as api.ipify.org and ipapi.co, used Cloudflare Turnstile, and reported visitor and download events through Telegram bots.
  • 05A related docsn[.]cc lure used a fake DocuSign signing request and the same overall playbook, but with a different ScreenConnect instance ID, different relay hostnames, and a different Telegram bot token.

On August 18, 2026, GTA VI gameplay footage and map assets began circulating online under the name Cyberleek, and the article says the material was widely assessed as authentic. The leak was released gradually, with clips dropped after Discord polls using tokens to decide what came next. The article links the leak’s pacing to a Solana token called $CYBERLEEK, whose creator reportedly earned trading fees and held an unlocked token allocation.

The Cyberleek hype was quickly used by threat actors distributing fake “playable Cyberleek build” downloads through websites, torrents, fake ISOs, and Telegram channels. Samples from cyberleek[.]click and cyberleek[.]info did not install GTA VI content, but instead installed a legitimate ScreenConnect remote access client configured for unattended access to an attacker-controlled relay. The lure pages profiled visitors with IP and geolocation services, used Cloudflare Turnstile, and sent visitor and download events to operators through Telegram bots.

The article also traces a related docsn[.]cc lure using a fake DocuSign signing request, separate ScreenConnect infrastructure, and a different Telegram bot token. Although the cyberleek and docsn[.]cc chains used different relays, instance IDs, and bot tokens, the article says they shared the same playbook of profiling visitors, reporting through Telegram, and installing ScreenConnect. The research closes with confirmed malicious indicators, additional supporting services, ScreenConnect instance IDs, and crypto addresses tied to the fake premium-access scam.

  • The ScreenConnect instance IDs in the install paths, e934a059a3e150c6 and 3bd96abf95efe08e, are especially useful detection and pivoting points because they are tied to specific ScreenConnect relay instances rather than to the lure pages alone.
  • The Cyberleek and docsn[.]cc campaigns did not share relay infrastructure, bot tokens, or lure themes, so the strongest link between them was the repeated operational pattern of visitor profiling, Telegram reporting, and ScreenConnect installation.
  • Telegram Bot API responses provided investigative value: the first bot returned 401 Unauthorized, while the second was initially live and later returned “Logged out,” showing different states for the two bot tokens.
  • Pivoting on a shared DocuSign image hash produced nearly 200 urlscan results, but most were unrelated noise, showing that common asset hashes can create misleading clusters unless validated with other evidence.
  • The article separates confirmed malicious indicators from benign supporting services such as GoDaddy Website Builder, ipify, ipapi, and image-hosting domains, which helps avoid treating common third-party services as malicious on their own.

After leaked GTA VI footage drew attention online, fake “playable” versions of the leak appeared on websites, torrents, and Telegram, but they installed software that lets criminals remotely access a victim’s computer instead of anything related to the game. People trying to download the supposed game build could have their location, device details, and other browsing information collected before being pushed into the scam. The article matters because it shows how quickly criminals turn major online hype into traps, using trusted tools and fake pages to make the activity harder to spot.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

The article reports a specific, active malware campaign abusing GTA VI leak hype to distribute ScreenConnect remote-access clients through fake download and DocuSign lure sites. It is broadly useful for security professionals because it provides concrete infrastructure, ScreenConnect instance IDs, Telegram bot behavior, and IOCs for detection, though it does not describe a major breach or critical vulnerability.
