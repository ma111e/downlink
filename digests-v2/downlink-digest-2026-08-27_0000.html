What Cloud Threat Hunting Is and How to Run One | Mitiga

Cloud threat hunting is a hypothesis-driven investigation process that complements detection by digging into suspicious activity across cloud and SaaS environments. Its effectiveness depends heavily on collecting and retaining the right cloud logs before providers delete them or licensing gaps leave activity unrecorded.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cloud threat hunting is a hypothesis-driven process for investigating security events in cloud environments and complements detection by digging into suspicious indicators in more detail.
  • 02Detection uses lightweight pattern recognition and may generate false positives, while hunting invests time and resources into investigating activity that appears to be an actual attack.
  • 03The article describes three approaches to cloud hunting: strategic hunts focused on adversary behavior, event-driven hunts based on malicious events affecting others, and continuous hunts that regularly check cloud and SaaS environments for attack indicators.
  • 04Cloud threat hunting depends on access to logs, but cloud and SaaS log availability can be limited by provider access, service licensing, and vendor retention periods.
  • 05Organizations should capture cloud logs into a security data lake before cloud vendors delete them so they have the data needed for hunting.

The article explains cloud threat hunting as a hypothesis-driven process for investigating security events in cloud environments. It says hunting complements detection because a single indicator, such as an authentication failure, may be either a harmless user mistake or a sign of an attempted account compromise. Detection uses lightweight pattern recognition and may produce false positives, while hunting involves deeper investigation into activity that appears to be an actual attack.

The article describes three approaches to cloud hunts: strategic, event-driven, and continuous. Strategic hunts focus on adversary behavior in a specific technology or platform, such as data exfiltration from object storage or compromise of a SaaS authentication process. Event-driven hunts use details from malicious events that happened elsewhere, while continuous hunts run ongoing checks in cloud and SaaS environments against indicators of attacks.

A major focus of the article is that cloud hunts depend on logs, but cloud and SaaS logging can be difficult to control. Some cloud provider logs may not be accessible, logging may depend on licensing, and vendors may keep logs only for limited periods. The article warns that attackers can exploit licensing-based logging gaps and recommends capturing logs into a security data lake before cloud vendors delete them.

  • An attacker could temporarily remove a user’s license, perform malicious activity through that account, and then restore the license so that the user may not notice and no logs are left behind.
  • Cloud logging gaps are not only a visibility problem for defenders; the article says attackers can actively exploit differences between free and enterprise logging tiers.
  • Cloud threat hunts may require more complicated log analysis than detection because detection is designed to quickly flag possible targets of interest, even at the cost of false positives.
  • For event-driven hunts, researchers may try to replicate an attack in a research and development environment after gathering information about a malicious event that affected someone else.

The article explains how security teams look more closely at suspicious activity in cloud services to decide whether it is harmless or part of someone trying to break into an account or steal data. This matters for organizations that use cloud and online business tools because the records needed to understand what happened may be limited, tied to paid licenses, or deleted by the provider after a short time. If those records are missing, attackers may be able to act without leaving enough evidence behind.
