FBI warns ARTEX AI hacking tool is powering global cyberattacks

The FBI warns that threat actors are using the ARTEX AI-driven penetration-testing framework to automate global intrusions at unprecedented speed, including the ConnectWise supply-chain compromise affecting Microsoft 365 tenants. Organizations should monitor for ARTEX-related indicators and unusual automated, cloud, identity, and data-transfer activity.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The FBI warned that multiple threat actors have used the AI-powered ARTEX penetration-testing framework since at least August 2026 to target organizations in financial, government, technology, defense, healthcare, and other sectors.
  • 02ARTEX uses external AI models and specialized agents to automate tasks such as finding targets, identifying weaknesses, gaining access, escalating privileges, and stealing data.
  • 03The FBI said some ARTEX-enabled intrusions progressed from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours, while allowing attackers to conduct operations against multiple targets simultaneously.
  • 04ARTEX was linked to the October ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain attack, in which compromised application identities were used to access Microsoft 365 customer environments.
  • 05The FBI advisory provides ARTEX-related indicators and recommends that organizations monitor for unusual automated requests, suspicious cloud activity, unexpected credential or privilege changes, and large data transfers.

The FBI warned that multiple threat actors have used the AI-powered ARTEX penetration-testing framework since at least August 2026 to target organizations worldwide. ARTEX is an autonomous Go-based framework that uses AI agents and external large language models to perform reconnaissance, find vulnerabilities, exploit systems, raise privileges, and steal data with limited human involvement. The agency said some intrusions moved from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours, enabling attackers to operate against multiple targets at once.

ARTEX was linked to the October ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain attack, where compromised application identities were used to access Microsoft 365 customer environments; Huntress identified at least 162 affected tenants. Microsoft attributed that campaign to Storm-0940, which used ARTEX for reconnaissance, password spraying, and abuse of cloud application permissions, while other reported campaigns affected South Korean financial institutions and organizations in 14 countries. Although the original GitHub repository was removed, ARTEX remains available through forks, archives, localized versions, and ARTEX PRO; the FBI advises organizations to monitor for automated request bursts, suspicious cloud activity, identity changes, and large data transfers.

  • Removing the original ARTEX repository did not stop its availability because community forks, archived copies, localized versions, and a commercial ARTEX PRO edition remain accessible.
  • ARTEX is described as especially effective against often-overlooked entry points such as partner portals, employee applications, legacy services, and third-party integrations.
  • Researchers identified more than 1,600 publicly exposed ARTEX consoles as of early November, while the FBI believes the true deployment count is significantly higher.
  • The article cautions that ARTEX-related IP-address indicators can become outdated or produce false positives, so defenders should also look for behavioral signs such as bursts of automated requests and unexpected identity changes.

The FBI says criminals and espionage groups are using ARTEX, a tool that uses AI to speed up break-ins to computer systems around the world. It has been connected to attacks on Microsoft 365 customer accounts through a breach involving ConnectWise, South Korean banks, hospitals, schools, businesses, and more than 200 organizations in 14 countries. The concern is that the tool lets people with limited technical skill run many attacks at once and move from choosing a target to gaining powerful access in less than three hours, putting personal information, company data, and online services at greater risk.

TSMC employee data breach linked to cloud provider

TSMC confirmed that personal data belonging to current and former employees was exposed through a cloud service provider, while stating there is no evidence its own systems or customer operations were compromised. A threat actor calling itself SmithBianl is auctioning an alleged 6.9GB dataset containing roughly 46,000 employee records.
KEY POINTS
  • 01TSMC confirmed that unauthorized online material included personal data belonging to some current and former employees.
  • 02TSMC said the leaked data matched information stored in the system services of a cloud service provider and that it found no evidence its own systems were compromised.
  • 03A threat actor using the alias SmithBianl claimed to have a 6.9GB dataset containing 46,000 TSMC employee records and offered it for auction starting at 3 BTC.
  • 04The alleged dataset includes national ID numbers, addresses, contact information, and banking details.
  • 05TSMC is notifying affected staff, providing support, and working with a third-party cybersecurity firm.

TSMC confirmed that personal data belonging to some current and former employees was made available online without authorization. The company said the information matched data stored in the system services of a cloud service provider it uses, indicating that the exposure occurred outside TSMC’s own infrastructure. TSMC said it has found no evidence that its systems were compromised and that operations and customers were not affected.

A threat actor using the alias SmithBianl posted a 499-row sample on November 8 and claimed to possess a 6.9GB dataset containing 46,000 employee records. The alleged information includes national ID numbers, addresses, contact details, and banking information, and the actor offered the dataset for auction starting at 3 BTC. TSMC is notifying impacted employees, providing support, and engaging a third-party cybersecurity firm to assist with the incident.

  • TSMC’s confirmation that the material matched data held in a cloud provider’s system services points to the provider environment rather than TSMC’s own infrastructure as the identified source of exposure.
  • The alleged records include both identity and banking information, combining several types of sensitive employee data in the claimed dataset.
  • The actor released a 499-row sample and set a one-week deadline before threatening a free public release if the dataset was not bought.
  • TSMC said the incident affects current and former employees while reporting no evidence of impact to its systems, operations, or customers.

TSMC says personal information about some current and former employees was posted online without permission after data held by one of its cloud service providers was exposed. The information may include national ID numbers, home addresses, contact details, and bank information, and a person using the name SmithBianl claims to be selling a larger set of about 46,000 employee records. TSMC says it has not found evidence that its own systems, operations, or customers were affected, but the exposed employee details could put the people involved at risk.
