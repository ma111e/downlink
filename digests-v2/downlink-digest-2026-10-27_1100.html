Whole-of-state defense: How SLED agencies can secure critical infrastructure in the AI era

AI is expanding the cyberattack surface for state, local, and education organizations that operate critical infrastructure, making under-resourced utilities, schools, and local governments attractive entry points. The article advocates a whole-of-state model with shared visibility, governance, services, and prioritized management of IT, OT, identity, cloud, and AI exposures.
KEY POINTS
  • 01SLED organizations operate critical infrastructure including water treatment plants, transportation systems, public hospitals, schools, and emergency communications, and nearly 90% of state CIOs rate cyberattacks on critical systems as a high concern.
  • 02AI expands SLED cyber risk because employees can build unapproved AI-enabled applications, vendors add AI features and connectors that can expand data access, and attackers use automation to conduct reconnaissance faster.
  • 03Federal agencies warned in July that cyber threat actors were targeting internet-connected operational technology in the water and wastewater sector, including programmable logic controllers whose passwords and IP addresses were altered to lock out operators.
  • 04A whole-of-state approach treats state agencies, local governments, schools, and utilities as one defensive perimeter through shared governance, visibility of assets and security findings, and cybersecurity services.
  • 05The article recommends maintaining an inventory of critical assets and AI tools, prioritizing exposures by exploitability and public-safety impact, sharing visibility across jurisdictions, and establishing policies for AI use.

State, local, and education (SLED) organizations run services such as water systems, hospitals, transportation, schools, and emergency communications, making them frequent targets for cyber attackers. The article says attackers may first compromise smaller, less-resourced organizations, such as a town water utility or rural school district, and then attempt to move toward larger targets. Nearly 90% of state CIOs rated cyberattacks on critical systems a high concern in cited NASCIO research. Consequences can include disrupted water service, halted 911 dispatches, and exposure of resident data.

AI is increasing risk because employees can create unapproved applications, software vendors can add AI features and connections that expand data access, and attackers can automate reconnaissance. The article highlights federal warnings about targeting of internet-connected operational technology in water and wastewater systems, including programmable logic controllers whose passwords and IP addresses were altered to lock out operators. According to the cited CISA alert, this activity led to boil-water notices and extended manual operations. Aging operational technology, broad remote access, compromised credentials, and former employee accounts further increase exposure.

The article advocates a whole-of-state approach that treats state agencies, local governments, school districts, and utilities as a shared defensive perimeter. It recommends clear responsibility for prevention and response, shared visibility into assets and security findings, shared services such as patching and incident response, and rapid information sharing. It also advises organizations to inventory critical assets and AI tools, prioritize risks that could affect public safety, secure remote access and internet-exposed controllers, govern AI use, and seek available State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program funding.

  • Small towns, rural school districts, and special-purpose utilities can become indirect paths toward larger state targets, because attackers may breach less-defended organizations first and then move through connected environments.
  • In water-sector incidents observed by CISA, attackers changed programmable logic controller passwords and IP addresses, locking out operators and causing boil-water notices and prolonged manual operations.
  • The article notes that it is often unclear which agency is responsible for responding to a critical-infrastructure attack when legal authority, governance, or partnership arrangements are absent.
  • Utah’s shared-services program provides endpoint protection, patching, training, and incident response to roughly 80% of local government entities, illustrating how a state can extend cyber capabilities to smaller organizations.
  • The article argues that local governments’ trust in statewide cyber programs depends on practical outcomes such as faster remediation, lower costs, and fewer surprises rather than assurances alone.

The article explains that hackers increasingly target state and local services such as water plants, hospitals, schools, transportation, and emergency dispatch, often starting with smaller organizations that have fewer resources. New AI tools can make it easier for employees to create unapproved apps and for criminals to find weak points faster. This matters because a successful break-in can disrupt safe water, 911 calls, health care, and other services people depend on, so states and local communities need to share information, tools, and responsibility.

An insecure neighbor: how a vulnerability chain led to an isolation bypass in cloud infrastructure

A Kaspersky-led bug bounty assessment found that chained flaws in a major cloud provider’s managed Kubernetes and internal management infrastructure could let a normal tenant escape a worker node, compromise control-plane systems, and bypass isolation between tenants. The issues included insecure privileged pod controls, a Konnectivity SSRF-to-RCE path, flat overlay networking, exposed monitoring/logging data, and weaknesses in managed database and SaltStack services; the provider reportedly fixed the findings.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Researchers participating in an official bug bounty program found that a managed Kubernetes cluster allowed tenants to create privileged pods with host access, enabling root access to a worker node.
  • 02A Konnectivity service token found on the worker node could be used with a flaw in the Konnectivity server to make network connections from the Kubernetes control plane and ultimately execute commands there.
  • 03The provider’s shared overlay network exposed managed-service infrastructure, while VictoriaMetrics and VictoriaLogs endpoints revealed other tenants’ control-plane targets and logs containing root SSH private keys.
  • 04Researchers identified additional paths to compromise managed Kafka, Redis/Valkey, PostgreSQL, and MySQL services through exposed or improperly configured components, potentially allowing access to other tenants’ data or command execution on their hosts.
  • 05A vulnerable custom SaltStack script enabled SQL injection against the provider’s cloud-management database, exposing Salt master authentication tokens that could run commands on cloud minions; the provider was notified and fixed the reported issues.

Researchers conducting an official bug bounty assessment of an anonymized major cloud provider found a chain of vulnerabilities and logic errors that could bypass isolation between cloud tenants. Starting as an ordinary managed Kubernetes customer, they were able to create a privileged pod, gain root access to a worker node, and obtain a Konnectivity service token. The provider was notified under responsible disclosure, and the article says all identified issues were fully fixed.

The Konnectivity service accepted connection requests that let the researchers make connections from the Kubernetes control-plane environment, eventually leading to command execution and then root-level access through an OverlayFS issue on Ubuntu 22.04. A shared overlay network and exposed monitoring, logging, database, and management components then enabled paths toward other tenants’ Kubernetes clusters, Kafka, Redis/Valkey, PostgreSQL, MySQL, backups, and cloud-management infrastructure. The researchers also found a custom SaltStack script vulnerable to SQL injection, which exposed Salt master authentication tokens that could be used to run commands on managed cloud systems.

  • A service intended to let the Kubernetes control plane communicate with worker machines accepted connection requests from a worker, allowing the researchers to make the control plane connect to arbitrary network addresses and local system sockets.
  • Shared monitoring systems became a cross-tenant exposure point: VictoriaMetrics revealed targets associated with other customers, while VictoriaLogs stored root SSH private keys printed by cloud-init scripts.
  • Several managed services were reachable on a flat internal network, so weaknesses in ancillary components such as a Kafka JMX registry, Redis exporter, or PostgreSQL etcd service could affect resources beyond the researchers’ own tenant.
  • The final SaltStack path did not rely on a public SaltStack flaw; it relied on a provider-written script that built database queries unsafely from metadata supplied by managed machines.
  • Credentials and configuration data found on compromised PostgreSQL hosts could provide access to S3 storage containing backups for databases beyond the current instance.

Researchers in an official bug bounty program found a chain of weaknesses in a major cloud provider that could let an ordinary customer break out of their own rented service and reach the provider’s internal systems. From there, they could potentially access or take control of other customers’ Kubernetes clusters, databases, storage backups, and cloud services. This matters because cloud customers depend on the provider to keep each customer separate, and a failure of that separation can expose many organizations at once. The provider was notified and says the reported issues were fully fixed.

Identifying AI agents: Bringing visibility and control to Castle

Castle has introduced AI-agent identification that uses Web Bot Auth, browser fingerprinting, network signals, and user-agent strings to identify agents such as ChatGPT agent and Perplexity Comet. The company emphasizes that cryptographic authentication offers stronger identity assurance than fingerprinting or user-agent detection, while agent identification remains distinct from bot and abuse detection.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Castle has added AI-agent identification that associates identified agent identities with application events and exposes the information through its API.
  • 02Castle identifies agents using cryptographic Web Bot Auth where available, as well as browser fingerprinting, network-level information, and user-agent strings.
  • 03Web Bot Auth verifies that requests were signed by a key associated with a known agent operator, while fingerprinting and user-agent strings do not provide the same authentication guarantee.
  • 04Castle distinguishes AI agents, AI browsers, and browser automation, including agents such as Meta Muse, OpenAI Dots, Grok Bot, ChatGPT agent, and Instinct, plus Perplexity Comet.
  • 05Castle states that AI-agent identification, bot detection, and abuse detection are complementary because automated AI-agent traffic is not necessarily malicious.

Castle has introduced AI-agent identification to help websites recognize automated clients acting on behalf of users, including Meta Muse, OpenAI Dots, Grok Bot, ChatGPT agent, Instinct, and Perplexity Comet. The company associates an identified agent’s identity with its application events and exposes the information through its API. This allows customers to investigate agent activity, measure adoption, and create agent-specific policies.

Castle uses cryptographic Web Bot Auth where available, along with browser fingerprinting, network-level signals, and user-agent strings. Web Bot Auth provides stronger assurance because it verifies that a request was signed by a key associated with a known operator, while fingerprints and user-agent strings can be imitated. Castle emphasizes that recognized automated traffic is not inherently abusive: agent identification, bot detection, and abuse detection serve different purposes, and websites must still determine what actions an authenticated agent may perform.

  • A cryptographically verified AI agent is not automatically trustworthy: Web Bot Auth confirms who signed a request, but each website must still decide which actions that agent may perform.
  • Websites can apply different rules by action, such as allowing recognized agents to view public content while requiring added checks for registrations, payments, or account-setting changes.
  • The article says consumer-facing AI agents are not currently the main source of malicious automation; attackers are more likely to use purpose-built bots for credential stuffing and fake-account creation.
  • AI can still strengthen malicious automation indirectly by helping attackers reverse engineer defenses and develop and maintain bots that are harder to detect.

Castle says it can now help websites recognize when AI assistants such as ChatGPT agent, Meta Muse, or Perplexity Comet are visiting and acting for users. This gives online stores, software services, and financial applications a way to see what these assistants do, measure their use, and decide whether to allow, limit, or ask for extra checks before sensitive actions. The company warns that a browser’s claimed identity can be faked, so stronger proof is needed when a website grants special access. This matters as more people use AI assistants to search, fill in forms, and make purchases online.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a vendor announcement and trend-oriented discussion of Castle’s AI-agent identification capability, rather than a report of an active incident, breach, or urgent vulnerability. It offers useful context on distinguishing authenticated agents from fingerprinted or user-agent-identified automation, but does not require immediate action.

Tricks from the Past deployed via signed ChatGPT and Claude binaries

Check Point Research uncovered the Yoba campaign, which uses fake GitHub pages for purported ChatGPT and Claude desktop apps to distribute malware through DLL sideloading of legitimate signed OpenAI and Anthropic executables. The malware persists via scheduled tasks and retrieves attacker-controlled payloads through Telegram-seeded DGA domains and DNS-over-HTTPS, with XMRig cryptomining observed as the final payload.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Check Point Research tracked a campaign called Yoba that used fake GitHub repositories and GitHub Pages promoting purported ChatGPT and Claude desktop applications, including GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, and Claude Opus.
  • 02The malicious installers packaged a DLL named winmm.dll alongside legitimate signed OpenAI or Anthropic Setup.exe files, allowing the malware to run through DLL sideloading.
  • 03YobaDropper creates a ProgramData\WMProcess directory, copies the legitimate Windows WMPDMC.exe program there, drops the YobaLoader as wmpdui.dll, and creates a scheduled task running as Local System for persistence.
  • 04YobaLoader uses a Telegram channel profile image to generate a domain, queries Google DNS-over-HTTPS for a signed and encrypted DNS TXT command, and can download a next-stage payload from Catbox.
  • 05Researchers observed the loader injecting an XMRig 6.26.0 cryptocurrency miner into svchost.exe, while noting that the loader can also deliver other PE or PowerShell payloads.

Check Point Research identified the Yoba campaign, which uses fake GitHub repositories and GitHub Pages for purported AI desktop applications including GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, and Claude Opus. The pages direct victims to malicious ZIP releases hosted on GitHub and appear in prominent Google search results, which researchers attribute most likely to SEO poisoning. The installers bundle malware with legitimate signed OpenAI or Anthropic Setup.exe files, using DLL sideloading to execute the malicious winmm.dll.

The YobaDropper checks for its expected host process, delays for roughly 12.73 days when that check fails, excludes several CIS keyboard languages, and requires administrator privileges. It creates a ProgramData\WMProcess directory, copies the legitimate Windows WMPDMC.exe file, drops the YobaLoader as wmpdui.dll, and establishes a scheduled task running as Local System. The loader uses a Telegram channel image to generate a domain, queries Google DNS-over-HTTPS for a signed and encrypted DNS TXT command, and downloads payloads from Catbox; researchers observed it injecting an XMRig miner into svchost.exe, but note it can also deploy other executable or PowerShell payloads.

  • A legitimate digital signature on the bundled OpenAI or Anthropic executable did not make the installer safe, because the attackers placed a malicious DLL beside it for the signed program to load.
  • The loader can change its command domain without updating the malware by deriving the domain from the last 50 bytes of the profile image of a Telegram channel.
  • The campaign avoids dedicated command-and-control servers by combining Telegram, Google DNS-over-HTTPS, DNS TXT records, Catbox hosting, and Tor, making its traffic resemble use of legitimate services.
  • The malware delays analysis for about 12.73 days when it is not launched under its expected host name, while also requiring administrator privileges and excluding several CIS keyboard languages.
  • Although XMRig mining was the observed final payload, the loader can also download and run arbitrary Windows executables or PowerShell scripts, so its operators can change the final payload.

Researchers found fake GitHub projects offering supposed ChatGPT and Claude desktop apps, such as “GPT-6 Astra” and “Claude Opus,” that actually install harmful software. The files use real, signed programs from OpenAI and Anthropic to help the harmful code appear trustworthy, then can stay on a victim’s computer and download more software. People who search for and install unofficial AI tools are affected, and the campaign shows how criminals can exploit interest in popular AI products and trusted websites to secretly use victims’ computers for cryptocurrency mining or deliver other harmful programs.

It provides concrete indicators, detection rules, persistence details, and payload behavior that are actionable for defenders.

The Impact of AI on CVEs: INDUSTRY CHALLENGES

AI is rapidly increasing both legitimate and fabricated CVE reports, overwhelming maintainers and vulnerability databases while making accurate severity assessment and patch prioritization harder. Organizations need to validate real-world exposure rather than rely solely on volume or CVSS base scores.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Generative AI is increasing the volume of both legitimate vulnerability discoveries and low-quality or fabricated reports, creating verification burdens for maintainers and vulnerability databases.
  • 02The curl project ended its bug bounty program in January 2026 after AI-generated submissions contributed to a flood of invalid reports, with confirmed vulnerabilities representing less than 5% of submissions.
  • 03NIST changed NVD prioritization in April 2026 to focus on CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog, federal software, and critical software, while moving older backlogged CVEs to “Not Scheduled.”
  • 04JFrog found that 54 of 55 advisories published by a newly created GitHub account, including reports targeting SQLite, were fabricated and contained nonexistent code references, impossible line numbers, or unsupported fix claims.
  • 05AI-assisted discovery is increasing remediation queues, while organizations still need to verify reports, identify affected systems, test fixes, and deploy updates.

Generative AI is increasing the speed of legitimate vulnerability discovery while also enabling a surge of low-effort, hallucinated, and fabricated security reports. The article says this has reduced the signal-to-noise ratio for vulnerability intelligence and placed growing verification burdens on open-source maintainers, the National Vulnerability Database, and enterprise security teams. Curl ended its bug bounty program in January 2026 after invalid AI-generated submissions increased and confirmed vulnerabilities fell below 5% of reports, while GNOME shortened its disclosure timeline to manage the volume of AI-assisted reports.

NIST changed NVD prioritization in April 2026, focusing analysis on CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog, federal software, and critical software while moving older backlogged CVEs to “Not Scheduled.” The article also highlights severity inflation, citing JFrog’s assessment that some libcurl flaws rated High or Critical by CISA were client-side crashes or depended on niche configurations, rather than practical network-based threats. JFrog found that 54 of 55 advisories issued by one new GitHub account were fabricated, including SQLite reports with nonexistent functions, impossible line numbers, and unsupported claims of fixes; meanwhile, AI can also help turn public flaw information into working exploits faster than maintainers can verify, fix, test, and release patches.

  • High or Critical CVSS ratings can substantially overstate real-world danger when they do not account for whether vulnerable code is exposed, optional features are enabled, or attacker-controlled conditions are actually possible.
  • In JFrog’s review of libcurl CVEs, flaws that maintainers assessed as Low or Medium were often rated High or Critical by CISA, including client-side crashes and issues requiring niche configurations.
  • The article reports that fabricated advisories can enter downstream sources such as GitHub Security Advisories and enterprise scanners because the CVE process can assign scores without requiring a working proof of concept.
  • AI-assisted discovery creates a bottleneck after reporting: finding flaws may accelerate, but maintainers must still validate each issue, determine affected versions, build and test fixes, and release them safely.
  • Public vulnerability details may become more dangerous sooner because AI can help researchers turn disclosures into weaponized exploits during the period before organizations deploy patches.

The article says AI is helping experts find software flaws faster, but it is also producing many false or exaggerated reports that waste the time of open-source volunteers, government databases, and company security teams. Projects such as curl and GNOME have changed how they handle bug reports after being flooded with low-quality submissions, while NIST has had to focus its review efforts on the most important cases. This matters because organizations must sort through more alerts and software updates than they can easily handle, while criminals may also be able to turn public flaw details into working attacks more quickly.

Introducing the Cloudflare PHP Capture the Flag challenge

Cloudflare launched a HackerOne-backed PHP Capture the Flag challenge inviting researchers to bypass its WAF, exploit PHP 8.5.11 vulnerabilities CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, and retrieve a sandboxed flag. Valid, materially distinct RCE-and-flag-recovery bypasses submitted under the program rules may qualify for a bounty.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cloudflare launched a limited-time PHP Capture the Flag challenge through its HackerOne bug bounty program to test whether researchers can bypass its web application firewall protections.
  • 02The challenge targets CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 in stock PHP 8.5.11 running with Apache 2 via mod_php in a Cloudflare Container.
  • 03Participants must use an authorized POST request to achieve attacker-controlled PHP code or command execution, recover the current contents of /flag, and verify the flag through the challenge interface.
  • 04To claim a bounty, researchers must submit a private, complete, reproducible report to Cloudflare on HackerOne, and awards require a materially distinct bypass method or exploit payload.
  • 05Cloudflare states that its web application firewall can help protect exposed systems but is not a substitute for applying relevant PHP security updates.

Cloudflare has launched a limited-time PHP Capture the Flag challenge through its HackerOne bug bounty program. Security researchers are invited to bypass Cloudflare’s web application firewall protections, exploit a deliberately vulnerable PHP environment, and retrieve the contents of a /flag file. Cloudflare says the challenge is intended to collect real-world information that can help test and strengthen its defenses.

The challenge focuses on CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, the PHP vulnerabilities associated with php2shell. The target uses stock PHP 8.5.11 with standard extensions, Apache 2 through mod_php, an empty index.php, and a Cloudflare Container, while default WAF rules are enabled in front of it. Eligible submissions must demonstrate code or command execution through an authorized POST request, recover and verify the current flag before the sandbox resets, and provide a complete private report to Cloudflare on HackerOne.

Cloudflare states that only materially distinct and reproducible bypass methods or exploit payloads may qualify for awards, while equivalent encodings and cosmetic changes are treated as duplicates. Testing is authorized only against the challenge hostname’s WAF-protected /php/* endpoints using POST requests, and denial-of-service activity, high-volume scanning, direct infrastructure access, and attacks on other systems are out of scope. The company emphasizes that WAF protection is not a replacement for applying PHP security updates, because blocking a response cannot undo actions already performed at the origin.

  • Cloudflare is using a deliberately vulnerable, WAF-protected public sandbox to collect real-world evidence of bypass techniques rather than treating its initial protections as a final solution.
  • A successful file read alone is not enough for a reward: participants must demonstrate remote code or command execution, use it to retrieve the current flag, and complete prompt interface verification before the sandbox resets.
  • Cloudflare will treat submissions using equivalent payload encodings or cosmetic variations as duplicates, making the underlying bypass method—not a newly generated flag—the basis for uniqueness.
  • The challenge permits testing only through POST requests to the WAF-protected /php/* endpoints, with direct infrastructure access, high-volume scanning, and denial-of-service activity explicitly excluded.

Cloudflare has launched a public challenge that asks security researchers to try to get past its protective filters and access a protected file on a deliberately vulnerable test website. The challenge focuses on two recently identified flaws in PHP, software used by a large part of the web, and lets Cloudflare find new ways attackers might get around its defenses. This matters to people and organizations running PHP websites because protective filters can help reduce risk, but they do not replace installing security updates that fix the underlying problem.
