Nothing to Block: A 1990s Domain and a Google Form

A phishing email sent from a compromised, decades-old legitimate mailbox passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC and used a Google Forms link for credential harvesting, leaving no obvious reputation-based indicators to block. The key takeaway is that clean authentication and trusted hosting do not prove an email is safe; detection must evaluate message content, sender relationship, and behavior.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A general manager at a national in-home care services provider received an unbranded payment notice from a legitimate property-management business domain registered in the late 1990s.
  • 02The email passed DKIM, SPF, and DMARC because it was sent from a real compromised mailbox rather than from spoofed infrastructure.
  • 03The single hyperlink in the message was a Google Forms shortlink that redirected to a form page on Google's own document platform.
  • 04The lure used fabricated transaction details, including a customer identifier, paid date, BILLPAY memo, and reference number, while omitting the payment amount and showing a mismatch between the subject and body reference numbers.
  • 05IRONSCALES' Themis Adaptive AI classified the message as credential theft at 83 percent confidence based on content and behavior rather than infrastructure reputation signals.

A general manager at a national in-home care services provider received an unbranded payment notice that appeared to report a settled debit request. The message contained a plain bordered table with a customer identifier, paid date, BILLPAY memo, and check and draft reference number, but no dollar amount. Its only hyperlink was labeled as a way to view transaction details and resolved through a Google Forms shortlink to a form hosted on Google’s own domain.

The email came from a legitimate property-management business domain registered in the late 1990s, and the recipient’s mail system recorded DKIM, SPF, and DMARC passes with an intact relay chain. The article says these clean results occurred because the mailbox was real and had been taken over, allowing the operator to inherit the domain’s authentication and reputation. The destination also offered little reputation signal because the collection page was hosted on Google’s document platform.

The article highlights several signs in the message itself, including a mismatch between the reference number in the subject line and the one in the body, the missing payment amount, and visible sender and recipient fields that matched each other while actual delivery went elsewhere. IRONSCALES’ Themis Adaptive AI classified the message as credential theft at 83 percent confidence and labeled the recipient as high value based on content and behavior rather than infrastructure indicators. The incident is mapped to MITRE techniques for spearphishing links, phishing for information through a link, and use of valid accounts.

  • Clean SPF, DKIM, and DMARC results can be a downstream consequence of account takeover, so they should not be treated as evidence that a message is safe.
  • A trusted destination domain can hide malicious intent when attackers use a legitimate platform such as Google Forms as the data-collection endpoint.
  • The missing payment amount was part of the lure’s design, because it created a reason for the recipient to click the transaction-details link.
  • A mismatch between the reference number in the subject line and the reference number in the body was a visible clue that the payment notice was fabricated.
  • A self-addressed visible sender and recipient, combined with actual delivery to another mailbox, indicated a blind-copied blast from the hijacked mailbox rather than normal correspondence.

A general manager at a national in-home care services provider received a fake payment notice that came from a real, long-established business email account and linked to a form hosted by Google. Because both the sending email account and the Google form looked legitimate, the usual trust checks did not clearly warn that it was a scam. This matters because people can be tricked into giving away information or access even when a message appears to come from trusted places, and losses from this kind of business email fraud can be very large.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a specific real-world phishing case showing how a compromised legitimate mailbox and Google Forms can bypass reputation and authentication-based defenses. It is broadly relevant for security professionals because it demonstrates why clean SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and trusted hosting are insufficient indicators of safety, though it is not a major breach or critical active exploitation alert.
