SIEM Is Not Enough: Why You Need DAM for Your Databases

The article argues that sending database audit logs to a SIEM is costly and lacks the data sensitivity, identity, and behavior context needed to detect real risk, especially as AI agents increase database access. It promotes Varonis Next-Gen DAM as a way to monitor all databases through native audit collection and turn raw activity into actionable security findings.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The article argues that sending native database audit logs to a SIEM creates high ingest costs while producing raw events that lack database security context.
  • 02The article says legacy agent-based DAM deployments often take years, cover only a fraction of databases, and generate many undifferentiated alerts.
  • 03Varonis Next-Gen DAM uses native audit collection for most major database engines and the Varonis Gatekeeper for hypercritical databases that need inline enforcement such as blocking or dynamic masking.
  • 04Varonis says its platform turns database activity into findings by combining data classification, identity resolution, and user behavior analytics.
  • 05The article highlights AI agents as a growing database security challenge because they can generate large volumes of queries through shared service accounts while obscuring the originating user or workflow.

The article argues that the long-standing database security approach of deploying legacy Database Activity Monitoring on a small number of critical databases and sending the rest of the audit logs to a SIEM no longer works. It says SIEM-based monitoring creates large ingest bills, produces alerts without database context, and still leaves audit reporting as a manual process. The article also says legacy agent-based DAM can take years to deploy, cover only part of the database estate, and overwhelm teams with undifferentiated alerts.

The article highlights AI agents as a major reason this model is failing. In its example, an internal customer support agent may run thousands of database queries through a shared service account, while native logs show only that service account accessing data. The article says a SIEM lacks context about sensitive data, originating users or workflows, and normal behavior, making it hard to tell whether this activity is risky.

Varonis presents its Next-Gen DAM as an alternative that brings databases into its unified Data Security Platform through native audit collection, with Varonis Gatekeeper reserved for hypercritical databases needing inline enforcement such as blocking or dynamic masking. The article says Varonis turns raw database activity into findings by combining data classification, identity resolution through Active Directory or Entra, and user behavior analytics. It promotes a free Varonis Data Risk Assessment that can map sensitive data, resolve database accounts to identities, surface live activity alerts, and provide a remediation report.

  • The article frames SIEM-based database monitoring as “compliance theater” because teams may still need to manually query and format audit reports even after paying to ingest large volumes of logs.
  • The article claims the historical performance argument against native database auditing has weakened because modern native audit overhead is described as well under five percent and comparable to agent-based collection.
  • The article identifies shared service accounts used by AI workflows as a practical blind spot because database logs may show only the service account, not the human prompt or workflow that triggered the query.
  • The article presents a split deployment model as an alternative to all-agent DAM: use native audit collection for most databases and reserve inline enforcement through Varonis Gatekeeper for a smaller set of hypercritical systems.
  • The article suggests organizations can repurpose database audit streams already being sent to Splunk by pointing the same streams to Varonis instead, reducing the setup burden for broader database monitoring.

The article says many companies have been protecting only a few important databases while sending records from the rest into general logging tools, which creates huge costs but often does not show whether anyone is actually doing something risky. It argues this is becoming more dangerous because automated AI systems may make thousands of database requests using shared accounts, hiding which person or workflow caused them. The people affected are security teams, database owners, auditors, and any organization storing sensitive customer or regulated information. The broader risk is that companies may have lots of records but still miss unusual access to private data unless those records are connected to what the data is, who is using it, and what behavior is normal.

13 Malicious Packagist Themes Deliver iOS Spyware That Steals Crypto Wallet Seeds

Socket researchers found 13 malicious Packagist Composer themes for Vietnamese streaming CMS sites that inject JavaScript to run ad-fraud redirects and, on vulnerable iPhones, a FUNNULL-hosted exploit chain that installs spyware. The payload can steal sensitive iOS data including keychain contents, messages, photos, location history, and crypto wallet seeds, so affected site operators should remove the packages and users should update iOS immediately.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Socket’s Threat Research Team found 13 malicious Composer theme packages on Packagist across five vendor namespaces that inject JavaScript into Vietnamese movie and comic streaming sites that install them.
  • 02The injected JavaScript runs a mobile ad-fraud and gambling-redirect chain, and on iPhones it can trigger a WebKit-to-kernel exploit chain that installs spyware.
  • 03Apple confirmed that the kernel escape used by the iOS chain was already fixed in iOS and macOS 26.1 before Socket’s report, while the two WebKit entry points are public and listed in CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog.
  • 04The spyware payload can collect keychain databases, Wi-Fi passwords, SMS, address book, Photos, browser cookies, call history, location history, account databases, and crypto-wallet seed or mnemonic data from several wallet apps.
  • 05The article recommends that site operators remove any themes from the vsmov, vsphim, haiau009, chilltvcms, or ophimcms vendors, rotate credentials handled on the host, inspect theme scripts, and block the listed network indicators.

Socket’s Threat Research Team found 13 malicious Composer theme packages on Packagist across five vendor namespaces: vsmov, vsphim, haiau009, chilltvcms, and ophimcms. The themes target Vietnamese movie and comic streaming sites built on OphimCMS and KKPhim by injecting JavaScript into every page of sites that install them. The injected code runs a mobile gambling and ad-fraud redirect chain, and on iPhones it can load a WebKit-to-kernel exploit chain that installs spyware.

The iOS chain pulls stages from FUNNULL-hosted infrastructure and uses WebKit entry points identified in the article as CVE-2025-31277 and CVE-2025-43529, followed by a kernel escape involving AppleM2ScalerCSCDriver. Apple confirmed to Socket that the kernel escape was already fixed in iOS and macOS 26.1 before the report, while the WebKit entry points are public, patched, and listed in CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. The article says the chain targets devices that have not updated past iOS 18.6.x, with no offset tables for iOS 18.7 or iOS 26.

On successful exploitation, the spyware can collect keychain databases, Wi-Fi passwords, SMS, contacts, Photos, browser cookies, call history, location history, and account databases, then encrypt and upload them to command-and-control domains. A redeployed payload added iOS keychain queries for crypto-wallet seed and mnemonic material from Bitget, BitKeep, Bitpie, Phantom, Tonkeeper, Trust Wallet, and OKX. Socket recommends that site operators remove themes from the listed vendors, rotate credentials handled on the host, inspect theme scripts, and block the listed network indicators.

  • The same five Packagist vendor namespaces publish additional themes that currently carry no active payload, but the article says they should still be treated as untrusted because they share operators, activation mechanisms, and in one case opaque ionCube-encrypted account code with confirmed malicious packages.
  • The operators redeployed the iOS chain under new filenames in August 2026, but the exploit tables still only cover iOS 18.4 through 18.6.x and not iOS 18.7 or iOS 26, indicating the campaign is focused on devices left on older versions rather than current iOS releases.
  • The campaign combines two different monetization paths in the same poisoned themes: broad mobile gambling and ad-fraud redirects for mobile visitors, and higher-value spyware and crypto-wallet seed theft for vulnerable iPhone visitors.
  • The article separates the Vietnamese theme operators, the iOS exploit-kit operators, and a Chinese npm gambling operation as distinct tenants sharing FUNNULL infrastructure, rather than attributing all activity to one actor.
  • The themes’ admin “Custom JS” fields are rendered into every page unescaped, which means an inactive theme from the same operators could be armed through a package update or a stored settings change without requiring a newly installed package.

Researchers found 13 poisoned website theme packages used by Vietnamese movie and comic streaming sites; when a site installed one, every visitor could be shown gambling redirects, and some iPhone visitors could be silently hit with spyware. People using iPhones that had not been updated past certain older versions were most at risk, because simply visiting an affected page in mobile Safari could let attackers collect private data such as messages, photos, contacts, location history, saved passwords, and crypto wallet recovery words. The site owners were victims too, because they unknowingly served the harmful code to their own users, showing how one tainted software add-on can put many ordinary visitors at risk.
