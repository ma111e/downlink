Why AI Confidence Is Not Validation

AI-generated findings in digital forensics and incident response are not validation: practitioners must independently verify evidence, document the exact tools and versions used, and retain human authority over irreversible or legally consequential actions. SANS advocates separate AI governance frameworks for forensics and incident response because their speed and evidentiary-risk requirements differ.
KEY POINTS
  • 01AI confidence scores do not validate investigative findings, which must be independently confirmed against the original source files and evidence.
  • 02SANS created separate Digital Forensics & AI Investigations and Incident Response & AI Investigations frameworks, mapped respectively to SWGDE standards and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0.
  • 03Investigators should document the tool that produced an AI-assisted finding, and record the precise version when using a general-purpose model because capabilities change over time.
  • 04Incident response can allow more AI-assisted speed during active events when tools have been built and validated, while digital forensics requires validation of every source file.
  • 05AI should not make irreversible decisions such as collecting cloud data, choosing an acquisition approach, or deploying tools to capture activity across systems; human practitioners remain responsible for those choices.

AI is already used in digital forensics and incident response, but the article argues that confidence in an AI output is not proof that it is correct. SANS introduced separate Digital Forensics & AI Investigations and Incident Response & AI Investigations frameworks, mapped to SWGDE standards and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0. Both frameworks maintain that AI may accelerate work, but human examiners remain the final authority.

For forensic work, AI findings must be checked against the original source files, and investigators should document the tool involved and, for general-purpose models, its exact version. The article distinguishes incident response, where validated AI can support faster triage and containment under human monitoring, from forensics, where every source file requires validation and findings may later be scrutinized in reports, depositions, or court. It recommends that AI automate reversible actions such as hash verification but not independently make irreversible or legally consequential decisions, including evidence collection or collecting cloud data.

  • Many examiners may already rely on AI-derived confidence scores through built-in functions such as skin detection, facial similarity, and image categorization, even if they say they do not use AI.
  • Recording an AI model’s exact version is important because technology may change substantially between an investigation and later testimony, allowing legal challenges to claims about what the tool could do at the time.
  • The article offers a practical boundary for AI use: it can automate reversible tasks such as hash verification, but it should not independently take actions that cannot be undone, including collecting cloud data.
  • During a live incident, requiring human approval for every AI-recommended action can overwhelm analysts; continuous human monitoring can be workable when the organization has built and validated the AI tooling.

The article argues that AI can help people investigate computer incidents and examine digital evidence, but a confident answer from an AI tool is not proof that it is correct. Investigators and incident-response teams need to check AI claims against the original files and record which tool and version they used, especially when evidence may be questioned in court. This matters because an unchecked AI error could lead to a false conclusion in a company investigation, legal report, deposition, or court case, while overly slow review can also hinder response during an active incident.
