Microsoft Azure flaw exploited to steal customers' API credentials

Microsoft fixed an actively exploited flaw in Azure API Management Classic that could let unauthorized actors access stored secrets, subscription keys, and management tokens. Affected customers must rotate potentially exposed credentials and investigate for misuse, as the fix does not revoke credentials stolen before remediation.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Microsoft said an exploited vulnerability in Azure API Management Classic tiers could allow unauthorized parties to access stored secrets and perform administrative operations when instances were not configured according to recommended security practices.
  • 02Potentially exposed information included Named Values secrets, identity and OpenID Connect client secrets, subscription keys, and tokens for direct management APIs.
  • 03Microsoft identified the issue on November 18, 2026, closed the unauthorized access path on November 19, and rotated Microsoft-controlled credentials where appropriate.
  • 04Microsoft warned that its remediation does not automatically invalidate customer credentials that may have been accessed before the fix, and urged affected customers to rotate them and review systems and logs for suspicious activity.
  • 05Norway's National Security Authority linked the issue to incidents affecting Norwegian organizations, including energy companies Equinor and Skagerak Energi, while stating that organizations in other countries and sectors were also affected.

Microsoft warned that a vulnerability in Azure API Management (APIM) Classic tiers was exploited to access secrets stored in affected customer instances. The company identified the issue on November 18, 2026, deployed a mitigation on November 19, and said APIM v2 tiers were not affected. The flaw could permit unauthorized management functions when Classic instances were not configured according to recommended security practices, enabling administrative actions including access to stored secrets.

Potentially exposed data included Named Values secrets, identity-provider and OpenID Connect client secrets, subscription keys, and tokens used for direct management APIs. Microsoft rotated Microsoft-controlled keys where appropriate but cautioned that its remediation does not automatically invalidate customer credentials that may have been accessed before the fix. It urged affected customers to rotate potentially exposed credentials, investigate systems reachable with those credentials, and review available logs for unauthorized activity.

Norway’s National Security Authority said an unknown actor targeted access tokens, secrets, and named values, with the issue first discovered in Norway’s energy sector and affecting organizations in other countries and industries. Equinor and Skagerak Energi reported cyberattack attempts, though both said they found no evidence of compromised information or operational impact. NSM also advised organizations to verify that their APIM instances received the security update, warning that some instances below specified runtime versions could remain vulnerable.

  • Microsoft's service-side fix closed the access route but did not revoke customer credentials that may already have been copied, making credential rotation an essential follow-up step rather than an optional precaution.
  • The impact can extend beyond Azure API Management itself because exposed keys and tokens may grant access to connected applications, backend systems, or other services according to their existing permissions.
  • The issue was limited to Azure API Management Classic tiers; Microsoft said v2 tiers were not affected, which gives organizations a clear way to scope their exposure.
  • Norway's National Security Authority advised customers to confirm their individual instances received the security update, noting that instances below certain runtime versions could remain vulnerable despite Microsoft's stated global remediation.

Microsoft fixed a weakness in an older version of its Azure API Management service that attackers used to access customers’ stored passwords, access keys, and login tokens. Organizations using the affected service, including Norwegian energy companies, may be at risk because those stolen credentials could open the door to other company systems and data. Microsoft closed the access route, but customers still need to replace any credentials that may have been copied and check for suspicious activity.

OpenRouter drops AI providers over leaked prompts and secrets

OpenRouter removed Together AI, Phala, and Novita after tests found prompts and embedded secrets could leak from their infrastructure, and it suspended Zero Data Retention status for 125 endpoints. Affected customers are being notified and advised to rotate any credentials sent in requests.
KEY POINTS
  • 01OpenRouter removed Together AI, Phala, and Novita after Dragos detected signs that data from at least three AI providers had been exposed during a two-million-prompt test.
  • 02OpenRouter revoked Zero Data Retention eligibility for 125 endpoints as a precaution and notified potentially affected customers.
  • 03Together AI attributed exposed prompt samples to a GitHub repository credential leaked by an AI coding agent, while Phala reported a compromised request-processing server and Novita cited a routing misconfiguration.
  • 04Atlas Cloud voluntarily withdrew its models from OpenRouter while security testing proceeds following a third-party security incident.
  • 05OpenRouter plans to use adapted Dragos tests to check providers for forwarding or leaking user requests and responses before restoring Zero Data Retention eligibility.

OpenRouter removed Together AI, Phala, and Novita after Dragos found indications that data handled by at least three AI providers had been exposed. Dragos sent two million test prompts containing unique tracking links or decoy credentials and observed attempts to use those credentials against its controlled systems and canary AWS accounts. OpenRouter said its own systems were not breached and attributed the issue to the provider level.

As a precaution, OpenRouter revoked Zero Data Retention eligibility for 125 endpoints and notified potentially affected customers. Together AI said leaked GitHub repository credentials allowed unauthorized access to stored prompt samples, Phala reported that an attacker compromised a request-processing server, and Novita cited a routing configuration that sent some requests to MiniMax’s official API. Atlas Cloud also voluntarily withdrew its models pending security testing, while the affected providers advised users to rotate any secrets included in requests.

  • Dragos’ unique links and decoy credentials did more than identify possible exposure: their use in attempts to access controlled systems and canary AWS accounts showed that leaked prompt contents reached outside parties.
  • The incidents resulted from different operational failures rather than a single shared flaw: a leaked GitHub credential at Together AI, a compromised processing server at Phala, and a routing error at Novita.
  • OpenRouter’s response extends beyond removing providers, as it revoked Zero Data Retention eligibility for 125 endpoints and plans to require testing before restoring that status.
  • Users who placed secrets in requests may be affected even when OpenRouter itself was not breached, because request data was processed by third-party inference providers.

OpenRouter stopped using three AI service providers after tests showed that private requests sent through its platform, including fake passwords and tracking links, could end up outside the intended systems. Some test credentials were then used to try to access controlled accounts, showing that leaked information could be misused. Developers and organizations that sent sensitive information through the affected services may need to change passwords, access keys, or other secrets. The incident highlights that using one service to reach many AI providers can expose private business data if any provider handles it carelessly or is compromised.

South Korea’s power giant KEPCO confirms data breach impacting 1.5 million

South Korea’s state-controlled power utility KEPCO confirmed that attackers accessed its billing and procurement systems, exposing personal and financial information for more than 1.5 million customers, employees, and company representatives. A threat actor using the alias Noiem claimed responsibility, though attribution remains unconfirmed.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) confirmed unauthorized access to its KEPCO ON electricity billing platform and its SRM electronic procurement system on November 20.
  • 02The incident exposed data belonging to more than 1.5 million people, including 650,487 KEPCO ON customers, 426,827 company representatives, and 442,934 KEPCO employees.
  • 03Exposed customer information included names, identification details, contact and address information, bank and payment-card details, and electricity billing information.
  • 04KEPCO blocked external IP access, suspended the affected services, and is directly notifying affected individuals while coordinating with data-protection, government, and financial authorities.
  • 05A threat actor using the alias Noiem claimed the breach and said it possessed KEPCO user, supplier, bidding, and contract data, but attribution remains unconfirmed.

Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO), South Korea’s main state-controlled power company, confirmed unauthorized access to its KEPCO ON electricity billing platform and SRM electronic procurement system on November 20. The company said the incident exposed information belonging to more than 1.5 million people before it blocked access from external IP addresses and suspended the affected services. KEPCO described the cause only as the use of “advanced technologies such as AI” and did not provide details about how the intruders initially gained access.

The affected records include customer contact, address, bank-account, payment-card, and electricity-billing information, as well as data on company representatives and KEPCO employees. KEPCO is notifying affected people, reported the incident to data-protection authorities, and is coordinating with government and financial authorities to prevent misuse such as unauthorized transactions. A threat actor using the alias Noiem claimed responsibility and said it had KEPCO user, supplier, bidding, and contract data, but the article says this attribution remains unconfirmed.

  • The exposed customer records combine contact details with bank or card information and electricity billing data, creating a particularly detailed basis for targeted phishing or impersonation attempts.
  • The breach affected both KEPCO’s customer billing platform and its procurement system, extending the impact beyond electricity customers to company representatives, employees, suppliers, and contracting-related records.
  • A hacker’s claimed data theft was reported to KEPCO by email on November 19, one day before the company confirmed unauthorized access on November 20.
  • KEPCO attributed the incident generally to advanced technologies such as AI but did not disclose how access was obtained or the sequence of events in the intrusion.

South Korea’s main electricity company, KEPCO, says someone got into two of its internal systems and viewed information belonging to more than 1.5 million people. The exposed details include customer names, addresses, phone numbers, bank and card information, billing records, and some employee and contractor information. This matters because criminals could use the information to trick people with convincing messages or impersonate them, and the company operates important national power infrastructure.

Hardened container images: STIGs, FIPS and FedRAMP explained

Hardened container images reduce attack surface by removing unnecessary components and enforcing secure configurations against baselines such as DISA STIGs, while FIPS separately governs use of validated cryptographic modules. FedRAMP requires hardened, regularly scanned images and pipeline enforcement, but these practices benefit any organization seeking stronger container security.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A hardened container image removes software the application does not need, such as shells, package managers, and compilers, and locks down the remaining configuration.
  • 02DISA STIGs are U.S. Department of Defense configuration standards with individual checks that can be tested and documented, including checks that do not apply to containers because they are handled by the host.
  • 03FIPS 140 concerns validated cryptographic modules and does not by itself harden a container image or ensure that every application component uses approved cryptography.
  • 04FedRAMP requires container images to be hardened against a benchmark from NIST’s National Checklist Program, scanned before deployment, and assessed within the previous 30 days before running in production.
  • 05Minimal, distroless, and scratch images can reduce attack surface, but they do not necessarily meet a defined secure-configuration baseline.

The article explains that a hardened container image removes software an application does not need, such as shells, package managers, and compilers, while enforcing secure settings including non-root execution and read-only file systems. It distinguishes hardened images from minimal, distroless, scratch, and FIPS images, noting that a smaller image does not necessarily meet a defined configuration baseline. The author argues that these controls reduce attack surface and limit what an attacker can do after gaining access, whether or not an organization is federally regulated.

DISA STIGs provide Department of Defense configuration checks that can be applied, scanned, and documented for container contents, while host-level checks that do not apply in containers should be recorded as not applicable. FIPS 140 is separate from hardening because it validates cryptographic modules and requires cryptographic operations to use approved modules and algorithms. FedRAMP brings these layers together by requiring images to be hardened against a NIST National Checklist Program benchmark, scanned before deployment, assessed within the previous 30 days, monitored in registries, and tracked in inventory. The article emphasizes that image hardening is ongoing: images need rebuilding and rescanning as new CVEs and updated STIG baselines emerge, and secure image settings can be undone by unsafe runtime deployment choices.

  • A scratch image may have the smallest footprint, but it can create operational and security maintenance issues because it normally lacks trusted certificate authorities, user databases, and debugging tools unless they are added manually.
  • Many STIG checks for kernel, bootloader, audit logging, and SSH do not apply inside a container because those functions are inherited from the host; they should be documented as not applicable rather than ignored.
  • A container can have zero known CVEs yet still be insecurely configured if it runs as root or includes setuid binaries and a shell.
  • A FIPS-capable base image can be undermined when an application uses its own cryptographic implementation, such as a statically linked Go binary or vendored OpenSSL, rather than the validated module.
  • Pinning an image to a digest improves reproducibility but also prevents patches from arriving automatically, since fixes require updating to a new digest.

The article explains how organizations can make the software packages used to run applications in containers safer by removing unnecessary tools and locking down risky settings. It describes strict U.S. government rules, including FedRAMP, that require these packages to meet tested security baselines and be checked regularly before use. Although these rules directly affect cloud services used by U.S. government agencies, the same practices can help any company limit the damage if an attacker gets into one of its applications.

This is an evergreen educational guide to container hardening, STIGs, FIPS, and FedRAMP requirements rather than reporting a new incident, vulnerability, or urgent change. It is useful background for teams operating containers or pursuing U.S. government compliance, but it has no immediate operational urgency.

SASE in the Age of AI: Why Architecture Matters More Than Ever

AI and autonomous agents dramatically increase traffic, operational dependency, and security risks, but SASE’s core distributed, edge-delivered zero-trust architecture remains the right foundation. Organizations should prioritize globally distributed, single-pass SASE implementations with continuous, context-aware inspection rather than treating SASE as a checklist of features.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The article argues that AI increases security and networking demands because users now include both people and autonomous agents that generate large volumes of globally distributed traffic.
  • 02It warns that large numbers of autonomous agents can cause data leaks, proprietary-model theft, compliance violations, cost overruns, and malicious code or threats.
  • 03The author says SASE should remain primarily cloud delivered and highly distributed, while acknowledging that some desktop-based agents may require on-premises controls.
  • 04The article recommends least-privilege access with continuous context- and behavior-based monitoring of agents, including detailed inspection of API and JSON transactions.
  • 05It argues that edge-based, single-pass traffic delivery is needed for data sovereignty, consistent performance, and time-sensitive AI applications.

The article argues that AI does not require a replacement for secure access service edge (SASE), but instead represents an incremental evolution the author calls “SASE 1.2.” AI agents, alongside human users, will generate large volumes of globally distributed traffic and increasingly perform critical business functions. As a result, outages, slowdowns, and poorly controlled agent behavior can have material business consequences.

The author warns that autonomous and coordinated agents can create risks including data leaks, proprietary-model theft, compliance violations, cost overruns, and malicious code or threats. SASE should provide least-privilege access, continuous context- and behavior-based monitoring, and detailed inspection of API and JSON transactions to determine what an agent is doing. It should also be primarily cloud delivered and globally distributed, while retaining on-premises controls where desktop-based agents and local tools cannot be observed by cloud services.

The article emphasizes edge-based, single-pass routing to support data sovereignty and consistent performance for AI services. It says organizations may need to prevent agents from interacting across prohibited sovereign regions, and that even network transit through such regions can pose unacceptable risk. The author concludes that organizations should evaluate the underlying SASE architecture rather than choosing platforms based only on feature lists, because centralized or on-premises-heavy implementations may become limiting as AI use expands.

  • The author characterizes the AI-driven evolution of SASE as “SASE 1.2,” arguing that it requires extensions such as AI-aware inspection rather than a replacement of the original architecture.
  • Agent access should not be approved solely at the start of a task: the article calls for just-in-time, just-enough access that is continuously reassessed as an agent’s behavior changes.
  • The article highlights that some AI agents will run directly on desktops and use local tools, meaning cloud-delivered security services may not observe all relevant activity and on-premises controls may still be necessary.
  • The author argues that data sovereignty can require preventing agents from interacting with other agents outside a permitted region, and that even routing traffic through prohibited areas can be unacceptable.
  • The article presents milliseconds of AI application performance as potentially commercially decisive, using competing pharmaceutical companies filing for a drug patent as an example.

The article argues that as companies use more AI helpers that can act on their own, they need strong systems for controlling what those helpers can access and do. These AI helpers can create huge amounts of activity and may leak data, misuse company resources, break rules, or cause costly outages if they behave unexpectedly. Businesses, employees, and customers could be affected, especially where delays or downtime can cost significant money, so companies should choose security and networking designs that work reliably worldwide and continuously check AI activity.

This is an opinion and best-practice perspective on adapting SASE architectures for AI agents, not a report of a specific incident, vulnerability, or urgent security event. It offers useful architectural considerations but is evergreen and non-urgent.

Dragos Canary Research: AI Data Security Risks | Cyera

Dragos researchers found that AI prompts—including those marked for zero data retention—were exposed through compromised inference providers, marketplace resellers, and undisclosed routing paths. Organizations should treat every prompt as external data exposure, restrict provider routing, prevent credentials from entering agent context, and retain prompt audit trails for incident response.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Dragos researchers embedded unique trackable secrets in about two million prompts sent to widely used AI services and reported that prompts were retained, shared with other companies, and read by people.
  • 02The research found attackers inside three AI hosting providers, including an incident in which attackers captured requests at a provider entry point and used stolen AWS keys to make tens of thousands of AWS API calls.
  • 03Dragos observed marketplace resellers opening secret URLs and manually attempting passwords from customer prompts, while one Chinese AI lab opened secret links from about two-thirds of requests marked for zero data retention or no data collection.
  • 04OpenRouter said on November 22 that it removed three providers and 125 endpoints from zero-data-retention eligibility, while stating that its findings showed no breach of OpenRouter itself.
  • 05The article recommends restricting routing for sensitive AI work, inspecting prompts and agent tool results for credentials before they leave, using canary credentials, and retaining records of agent activity.

Dragos researchers placed unique, trackable secrets in about two million prompts sent to widely used AI services and monitored whether those secrets were used. They reported that attackers accessed three AI hosting providers for as long as four months, marketplace resellers could read customer prompts, and one Chinese AI lab opened secret links from many requests marked for zero data retention. The research concluded that, in practice, prompts were retained, shared with other companies, and read by people.

The findings show that AI requests may pass through routers, hosting providers, and model developers, each of which can access the full prompt and response under its own policies. In the largest reported breach, attackers captured requests at a provider entry point, obtained AWS keys, and used them to search cloud services and attempt to maintain access. The article recommends that organizations inventory their AI providers and routes, restrict sensitive work to approved providers, inspect prompts and agent tool results for credentials, use canary credentials, and retain records of what agents send so exposed secrets can be identified and rotated.

  • A request can be exposed even if the AI provider rejects it before a model processes it, because researchers found attackers copying requests at a hosting provider’s entry point.
  • A zero-data-retention setting does not protect data from every company in the delivery chain: routing services may pass prompts to multiple providers, each operating under its own handling policy.
  • AI agents create a distinct exposure path because they can automatically include credentials or database results in later requests without a person seeing or approving that content.
  • When a provider later removes an endpoint’s zero-data-retention status, organizations need records of the prompts and agent sessions sent during the affected period to determine which secrets may require rotation.
  • The impact of a leaked credential depends not only on whether it appears in a prompt, but also on the cloud resources and sensitive data that credential can access.

Researchers placed unique secret links in about two million requests sent to popular AI services and found that some were accessed after the requests had supposedly been kept private. They found attackers inside three companies that host AI systems, marketplace sellers who could read customer requests, and one Chinese AI lab that opened many requests marked “do not retain.” This can affect any company whose employees or automated AI helpers send customer information, source code, contracts, or passwords to AI tools. The findings show that privacy promises may not protect information once it leaves a company, so organizations need to limit what they send and be able to identify and replace any exposed passwords.

This is a should-read report on concrete findings from roughly two million AI prompts, including compromises at three inference providers and failures of zero-data-retention claims. It has broad relevance because organizations may expose credentials, source code, customer data, and agent-generated context through AI routing and hosting supply chains.
