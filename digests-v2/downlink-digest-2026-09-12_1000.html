When the Whole Company Adopts AI: What It Does to Your SOC

Enterprise AI use is creating a rapidly growing SOC alert stream that is overwhelmingly benign noise, while the more consequential risks are unsafe agent configurations, public tunnels, credential exposure, and third-party AI data sharing. SOCs should tune legacy detections for normal agent behavior and proactively monitor permission-bypass use, OAuth grants, and risky agent actions.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Across roughly 16.9 million SOC alerts reviewed, about 73,000, or 0.43%, were related to AI activity, and AI-related alert volume grew 685% from February to June 2026.
  • 02The article classified 94.1% of AI-related alerts as noise, 5.8% as genuine security risks, and 0.02% as confirmed real attacks.
  • 03Most AI-related alerts were handled without human review: 79.8% received benign verdicts, 81.7% were automatically suppressed, and 5.4% were escalated to analysts.
  • 04The article identified unsafe AI use including coding agents run with permission safeguards disabled, reverse tunnels opened with ngrok, macOS keychain dumps, and OAuth access or uploads involving third-party AI services.
  • 05The article recommends tuning legacy detections for routine AI-agent behavior and proactively monitoring permission-bypass flags, unauthorized tunnels, risky OAuth grants, and information sharing with third-party AI platforms.

Intezer reviewed AI-related activity across enterprise SOC environments and found that AI-triggered alerts represented about 73,000 of roughly 16.9 million alerts, or 0.43% of the total. Although still a small portion of alert volume, these alerts grew 685% between February and June 2026. The review classified 94.1% of AI-related alerts as benign noise, 5.8% as genuine security risks, and 0.02% as confirmed attacks. Most alerts were automatically handled, with 81.7% suppressed and only 5.4% escalated to a human analyst.

The article says many false positives occur because coding agents legitimately launch shells, PowerShell, network tools, package downloads, and other actions that older detections associate with intrusions. Examples of genuine exposure included agents launched with permission safeguards disabled, an AI coding editor opening an ngrok reverse tunnel, an agent dumping the macOS keychain into a temporary file, and OAuth access or generative-AI uploads involving third-party services. Confirmed attack activity primarily involved phishing lures impersonating familiar AI brands such as Anthropic, Gemini, and OpenAI rather than compromises caused by organizations’ own AI agents. The article recommends tuning noisy legacy detections, monitoring permission-bypass flags, tunnels, OAuth grants, and data sharing, and isolating AI tools in containers or virtual machines with restricted access.

  • High-severity labels can be misleading for AI activity: one detection accounted for 55% of critical verdict alerts by flagging normal developer shell setup involving the Windows Expand.exe binary as lateral tool transfer.
  • The confirmed attacks described did not result from organizations' own AI agents being compromised; instead, attackers used familiar AI brands such as Anthropic, Gemini, and OpenAI as phishing lures.
  • Permission-bypass options such as --yolo and --dangerously-skip-permissions are both a meaningful exposure and a major source of false positives, making them especially important for proactive monitoring.
  • Because AI tools can execute actions under a user's credentials without the user knowing each action occurred, SOC investigations must first determine whether suspicious behavior came from an AI agent or from another source.
  • Running AI tools in restricted environments such as containers or virtual machines can both limit their access to credentials and sensitive data and make their activity easier to separate from the user's own actions.

The article examines how companies’ growing use of AI assistants is creating many new security warnings, mostly from normal work such as developers using AI coding tools. The warnings are growing quickly, but almost all are harmless; the more important concerns are AI tools being allowed to act without approval, opening connections to the public internet, accessing saved passwords, or sending company information to outside AI services. Companies and their security teams are affected because too many false alarms can hide the smaller number of situations that could expose sensitive information or systems.

Authenticated, Unbranded, and Nothing to Block

A callback-phishing email bypassed traditional controls by using a legitimately authenticated, long-established compromised domain and no malicious links or attachments; its only payload was a phone number. The case shows that authentication passes do not validate message intent, making content analysis and verification of contact-change requests essential.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Three mailboxes at a digital-asset investment management firm received variants of the same callback-phishing email within about two and a half hours.
  • 02The emails passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC checks on an unrelated domain registered roughly 25 years earlier, while containing no attachments, QR codes, or credential-harvest links.
  • 03The visible message asked recipients to call +1 (888) 857-9196, while a separate California Employment Development Department-styled block below the fold included a second number, 866-217-1218, sharing the same final four digits as a masked number in the upper card.
  • 04The message used a display name containing Cyrillic lookalike characters that rendered as a crypto analytics provider, while the agency-styled lower section reused the California Employment Development Department's real postal address and social-media profiles.
  • 05IRONSCALES reported that its Themis Adaptive AI quarantined all three messages automatically within six to seven seconds based on content analysis and community signals.

Three mailboxes at a digital-asset investment management firm received variants of a callback-phishing email within about two and a half hours. The message passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC authentication on a long-established but unrelated domain, contained no attachments, QR codes, or credential-harvest links, and directed recipients to call +1 (888) 857-9196. Its display name used Cyrillic lookalike characters to resemble a crypto analytics provider, although that provider was not named anywhere else in the email.

The visible card claimed that the recipient’s primary contact had changed, while a fully readable but below-the-fold section copied California Employment Development Department-style outreach, including the agency’s real address and social-media profiles. The two otherwise unrelated sections were separated by an 822-pixel spacer and shared a clue: the lower signature number, 866-217-1218, ended in the same four digits as the masked number in the upper card. The article emphasizes that email authentication confirms an authorized sending path rather than safe content, and that phone-number-based scams can bypass controls designed to inspect links, files, and suspicious domains. IRONSCALES said its Themis Adaptive AI quarantined all three emails within six to seven seconds using content analysis and community signals.

  • An email can pass authentication checks while remaining malicious because those checks confirm that the sending path was authorized, not that the message content or claimed identity is trustworthy.
  • The scam avoided the usual infrastructure indicators entirely by making a telephone number the only actionable payload, leaving link-reputation, attachment-sandboxing, and newly registered-domain checks with nothing to inspect.
  • The two unrelated body sections contained a useful correlation clue: the California Employment Development Department-style signature number and the masked number in the upper card shared the final four digits, 1218.
  • The message's DMARC result was recorded under a monitor-only p=none policy, which the article notes is a measurement outcome rather than an enforcement decision.
  • A recipient can independently verify a claimed contact change by using a communication channel that existed before the email arrived, rather than calling a number supplied by the message.

Three employees at a digital-asset investment firm received emails that looked legitimate because they came from a real, long-standing company email account, but the messages urged recipients to call a scam phone number. The emails mixed a fake contact-change notice with copied California employment-agency details placed farther down the page, making the message look more credible while hiding contradictions. This matters because scams can avoid suspicious websites and files entirely, so a message can pass normal authenticity checks while still trying to trick someone into calling criminals and giving up account access or other sensitive information.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a concrete callback-phishing incident that demonstrates how legitimate authentication, an old compromised domain, and the absence of malicious links or attachments can bypass common email defenses. It is broadly relevant to security professionals because it provides actionable indicators and shows why phone-number lures and message content require scrutiny.
