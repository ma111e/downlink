Bitdefender researchers identified a malware campaign called Midnight Mimosa that is embedded in the firmware of some low-cost Android phones using MediaTek chipsets. The malware has system-level privileges, allowing it to silently install or remove apps, grant permissions, and run code downloaded from remote servers. It affected thousands of devices across more than 150 countries over about two years, with the highest victim counts in Mexico, France, Italy, the United States, Germany, Brazil, and Spain.

The malicious components imitate Android system packages and cannot be removed through normal app uninstallation. Their downloaded apps conduct advertising fraud by loading ads in hidden windows and automatically registering impressions or clicks; the malware also temporarily disables the Google Play Store before installations and can make apps appear to have come from Google Play. A component disguised as an app locker can register phones as residential proxies that forward network traffic, although Bitdefender could not confirm active traffic forwarding during its tests.

Bitdefender found 13 Google Play apps with the same ad-fraud code and links to known Midnight Mimosa infrastructure, though they lack the elevated privileges of the preinstalled components. Removing the firmware-level infection requires cleanup through a firmware update or disabling the malicious component with Android Debug Bridge, which Bitdefender said may be difficult for many users.

  • A Doogee Fire 3 Max owner reported that an official firmware update introduced the infection, while restoring an older firmware removed it until the update was installed again.
  • The malware temporarily disables the Google Play Store before silently installing apps, then re-enables it afterward, a sequence Bitdefender said is intended to avoid Google Play Protect detection.
  • Although the proxy infrastructure accepted registrations from infected devices, Bitdefender did not receive relay targets during testing and therefore could not confirm that traffic was actively being forwarded.
  • Bitdefender found 13 Google Play-distributed apps linked to the same infrastructure and ad-fraud code, but these apps lacked the system privileges of the preinstalled components.

Researchers found harmful software built into some low-cost Android phones before buyers received them, including certain Doogee and Cubot models.   • 02Talos observed techniques over the past 18 months ranging from comments telling an AI to ignore a file to template spraying intended to mislead specific large language models.
  • 03Talos found that these prompt-injection techniques steer an AI verdict in the attacker’s favor about 35 percent of the time and are used across different levels of malware sophistication.
  • 04The MANTLEMAZE family combines AI-analysis deception with abuse of vulnerable drivers to disable endpoint detection and response software from kernel space.
  • 05Talos recommends treating text extracted from a sample strictly as evidence rather than as a system directive and flagging imperative language addressed to analysis systems within binaries.

Cisco Talos reports that malware authors are embedding natural-language instructions in malicious code to mislead AI-assisted analysis systems, a trend it calls “A3: AI-Analysis Evasion.” Over the past 18 months, Talos observed techniques ranging from simple comments that tell an AI to ignore a file to “template spraying” intended to deceive particular large language models. Talos found these methods steered AI verdicts in an attacker’s favor about 35 percent of the time.

The approach is being used across varying levels of malware sophistication and can accompany more direct threats. Talos says the MANTLEMAZE family combines AI-focused deception with abuse of vulnerable drivers to disable endpoint detection and response software from kernel space. Because the misleading instructions must be written in plaintext, Talos recommends that defenders flag imperative language aimed at analysis systems and ensure extracted sample text is treated as evidence rather than as a directive.

The newsletter also emphasizes intentional risk management for legacy systems that cannot be updated without disrupting essential operations. Its example describes isolating outdated check-printing devices onto a separate network with no internet access, reducing both exposure and the chance that attackers would find the devices during internal mapping. The article argues that such compensating controls can help keep critical services running while reducing risk from unsupported software and hardware.

  • The instructions used to mislead AI analysis must appear in plaintext, giving defenders a comparatively stable signal to search for inside suspicious files.
  • Even though the AI-manipulation techniques succeeded only about 35 percent of the time in Talos testing, they are cheap enough to be adopted by malware ranging from simple to sophisticated.
  • AI-focused deception can be paired with more direct technical harm: Talos says MANTLEMAZE also abuses vulnerable drivers to disable endpoint security software from a highly privileged part of the system.
  • When essential legacy systems cannot be updated without disrupting critical business functions, isolating them from the internet and other networks can substantially reduce risk even without fixing the underlying outdated software.

The article warns that some malicious software now includes written instructions meant to mislead AI tools that help examine suspicious files. These tricks can make the AI less likely to recognize a harmful program, while the same programs may also try to disable security protections. Suspected TraderTraitor Group Uses Trojanized Terraform Provider to Deliver Cross-Platform Malware

Zscaler researchers found a suspected North Korean TraderTraitor campaign that trojanized a Terraform provider to silently deploy FLATROOF credential-stealing malware and the ROOFDECK backdoor across macOS, Linux, and Windows. The campaign targets developer workflows and uses disguised font files, public hosting, and resilient Pastebin/Nostr-based C2 discovery to evade detection.
KEY POINTS
  • 01ThreatLabz identified a trojanized Terraform provider named terraform-provider-awsbeta_v1.0.0 that preserves normal Terraform behavior while downloading and launching a Bash loader from a HashiCorp-themed lookalike domain.
  • 02The Bash loader selects encrypted payloads for macOS, Linux, or Windows based on the victim system’s operating system and processor architecture, disguising the payloads as .woff font files.
  • 03The delivered FLATROOF Rust-based backdoor uses platform-specific persistence and can communicate through Telegram, GitHub, or an attacker-controlled HTTP webhook.
  • 04FLATROOF includes Python data-stealing scripts that collect browser credentials, cookies, browsing artifacts, shell history, system information, and, on Windows, data associated with several cryptocurrency wallet extensions.
  • 05The related ROOFDECK backdoor uses a local configuration file, a signed encrypted Pastebin record, and Nostr profile metadata to locate its command-and-control server.

Zscaler ThreatLabz reported a July 2026 campaign linked to TraderTraitor that used a trojanized Terraform provider masquerading as an AWS Terraform plugin. The malicious provider retained normal functionality but executed hidden code when Terraform loaded it, downloading a Bash loader from a HashiCorp-themed lookalike domain. The campaign appears aimed at cryptocurrency and Web3 developers, whose workstations or automated systems may run Terraform providers.

The Bash loader selected macOS, Linux, or Windows payloads based on the operating system and processor type, disguising encrypted executables as .woff font files. It delivered FLATROOF, a Rust-based backdoor with persistence and command channels through Telegram, GitHub, or an attacker-controlled web server, along with Python scripts that collected browser data, shell history, system details, and cryptocurrency wallet-extension data on Windows. The campaign also involved ROOFDECK, a backdoor that can find its command server through local settings, signed encrypted Pastebin records, and Nostr profile metadata, providing multiple ways to maintain remote access.

  • The malicious provider is designed to keep responding normally after launching its hidden code, which may make the compromise less noticeable to affected developers or automated build systems.
  • The loader can decrypt its payload using Python, Node.js, Perl, or OpenSSL, allowing it to operate on systems with different software already installed.
  • ROOFDECK verifies an RSA signature on the encrypted server address retrieved from Pastebin, preventing others from changing the dead-drop record to redirect infected systems without the attackers’ signing key.
  • The campaign uses public services including GitHub, Vercel, Telegram, Pastebin, and Nostr for payload delivery or command-and-control functions, while making malicious files and URLs resemble ordinary developer or web resources.

Researchers found a suspected North Korean campaign that hid harmful software inside a fake Terraform add-on, a tool commonly used by developers to manage cloud services. When a developer or automated work system ran it, the fake add-on could download programs for Mac, Linux, or Windows that steal browser passwords, cookies, cryptocurrency wallet information, and other personal or work data. It could also give the attackers long-term remote control of the computer, putting cryptocurrency and Web3 developers, their employers, and potentially their customers at risk.
