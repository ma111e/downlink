The Technical University of Denmark (DTU) says hackers accessed DTUBasen, its identity and access management system, using compromised credentials and downloaded a large amount of data. DTU cannot determine precisely what data was taken or how many people were affected. The system holds information for nearly 40,000 active users and around 160,000 former users, potentially exposing records dating back more than two decades.

Potentially exposed data for current users includes Danish civil registration numbers, names, home addresses, profile pictures, work email addresses, job titles, office locations, and other employment details. Names, relationships, and telephone numbers for next of kin may also have been exposed where users had supplied them, although certain former-user information is deleted after six months. DTU warned that the data could enable identity fraud and more convincing phishing, and advised potentially affected people to treat unexpected messages, calls, and login requests with caution, change reused passwords, and consider placing a credit alert on an affected CPR number.

  • DTU cannot determine precisely which data was downloaded or how many people were affected, so it is using a public disclosure to reach people it cannot contact directly.
  • Next-of-kin information may have been included for active users, but DTU says former users’ home addresses, profile pictures, and next-of-kin details are automatically deleted after six months.
  • DTU specifically advises people to change passwords reused on other services and to place a credit alert on an affected Danish CPR number, reflecting risks beyond the DTU account itself.

Someone used stolen login details to enter the Technical University of Denmark’s account system and download a large amount of information.   • 03Warlock has used SharePoint web shells to obtain ASP.NET machine keys and execute code within the SharePoint application environment.

In the past two months, the group reportedly targeted at least four organizations, including a water utility, a telecommunications provider, a regional government body, and a university.

The activity indicates that unpatched or otherwise unmitigated SharePoint deployments remain a viable entry point for these attacks.

  • Warlock used the compromised domain's SYSVOL share to distribute ransomware through normal domain replication, allowing it to reach at least 33 hosts in one incident without separately delivering the payload to each machine.

  • In one critical-infrastructure intrusion, a tool to disable security software was pushed to at least 40 hosts in roughly two hours before ransomware deployment.
  • Warlock combined legitimate services and tools, including catbox.moe, wasabisys.com, Velociraptor, and Visual Studio Code tunnels, with malicious payload delivery and remote access activity.
  • The group abused the legitimate but vulnerable K7RKScan.sys driver (CVE-2025-1055), a driver also previously used by DragonForce actors, to disable security software.

Salesforce Sites Phishing: The Attacker Owned Nothing

A phishing campaign abused legitimate subscription-billing email infrastructure, Salesforce Sites hosting, and Wikimedia branding, so SPF, DKIM, and DMARC all appeared valid despite a credential-harvesting lure. The key detection signal was behavioral: a first-time sender impersonating Salesforce in the From local part and requesting login credentials or an Organization ID by reply.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A UK consumer fintech received a malicious email impersonating Salesforce that was delivered through a subscription billing platform’s legitimate transactional email service with valid sender authentication.
  • 02The credential-harvesting page was hosted on a genuine Salesforce Sites organization, while the impersonated Salesforce logo was loaded from Wikimedia Commons.
  • 03The organization’s secure email gateway rewrote body URLs for click protection, causing the original DKIM signature to fail at Microsoft 365 even though the inbound gateway’s ARC records preserved the original passing authentication results.
  • 04The email also asked the recipient to reply with an Organization ID, creating a no-click path that would not trigger URL rewriting, sandboxing, or click-event monitoring.
  • 05The article concludes that behavioral signals, such as a first-time sender using a major SaaS brand in the sender name and requesting account access or identifiers, are important when attackers abuse legitimate third-party infrastructure.

A UK consumer fintech received a malicious email claiming that its Salesforce organization had been temporarily deactivated. The email was sent through a subscription billing platform’s legitimate production transactional mail service and carried valid DKIM, SPF, and DMARC results at the organization’s inbound gateway. A human analyst confirmed the message was malicious and quarantined it.

The phishing page was hosted on a genuine Salesforce Sites organization, and the Salesforce logo was loaded from Wikimedia Commons, leaving the attacker without an obvious malicious domain or image host. The organization’s secure email gateway rewrote URLs for click protection, which caused DKIM to fail later at Microsoft 365 even though ARC records preserved the original authentication passes. The message also offered a no-click route by asking the recipient to reply with an Organization ID, bypassing link rewriting, sandboxing, and click-event monitoring.

The article argues that sender authentication and reputation systems cannot determine whether a legitimate sender is making a deceptive request. It identifies behavioral signals as more useful in this case, including a first-time sender using a major SaaS brand in the sender’s local part while sending from an unrelated domain. It recommends hunting for that sender pattern and using phishing-resistant MFA and conditional access to reduce the impact of stolen credentials.

  • Valid email authentication can confirm that a legitimate vendor’s mail system sent a message without validating the truthfulness of the sender’s local part or the request made in the email.
  • URL rewriting by a recipient’s own email gateway can break downstream DKIM verification, so final-hop authentication failures may reflect defensive processing rather than a forged message.
  • A reply-based request for an Organization ID bypasses link-focused controls because it produces no rewritten URL, sandbox detonation, or click event.
  • A practical hunt proposed by the article is to identify first-time senders whose local part uses a major SaaS brand while their domain has no legitimate business relationship to that brand.
  • Shared platforms such as Salesforce Sites and Wikimedia Commons reduce the usefulness of domain and image reputation because blocking the entire legitimate service is impractical.

A UK consumer finance company received a fake Salesforce account-warning email that used real services from other companies, making it look trustworthy even though criminals did not own the sending or hosting systems. The message tried to steal a worker’s Salesforce login details or persuade them to reply with their organization’s ID, which could help make a later scam more convincing. This matters because common email checks can confirm that a message came through a real service but cannot tell whether its request is dishonest, leaving employees and organizations at risk of account theft.

This is a concrete, high-severity credential-harvesting incident that demonstrates how attackers can abuse legitimate email, hosting, and branding infrastructure while passing standard sender-authentication checks. It is broadly relevant to email-security practitioners because it highlights actionable behavioral and reply-based detection gaps, though it does not describe a named actor, widespread campaign, or urgent patch.
