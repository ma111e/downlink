The Phishing Email That Shipped Its Own Clean Verdicts

A phishing email impersonating Mailgun embedded dozens of forged email-security headers claiming clean authentication and low spam risk, but the only trusted receiving hop showed SPF softfail, DKIM failure, and DMARC failure. Defenders should treat headers added below their trusted mail boundary as attacker-controlled text rather than evidence.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A plain-text email impersonating a Mailgun account notice included roughly 70 sender-written headers that falsely claimed low spam risk, internal authentication, passing authentication checks, and a passing ARC seal.
  • 02The only trusted receiving hop recorded a DKIM body-hash failure, SPF softfail, DMARC failure, and a non-contiguous ARC chain.
  • 03The forged Exchange antispam headers named mail.contoso.com, Microsoft's documentation placeholder domain, while another forged header used the impossible IP address 541.428.412.821.
  • 04A planted ARC seal's signature value base64-decoded to the decimal number 0.49584384000973525 rather than a cryptographic signature.
  • 05IRONSCALES Themis identified a first-time sender and malicious link, and automated detection quarantined both waves of the campaign.

A plain-text phishing email impersonating a Mailgun account notice targeted an employee at a digital advertising company and included roughly 70 pre-written headers claiming it was safe. The sender inserted claims of a low Exchange spam score, internal authentication, passing authentication results, and valid ARC seals. These claims were all placed below the only trusted mail hop and were therefore sender-controlled text rather than receiver-generated verdicts.

The trusted Google receiving hop recorded a DKIM body-hash failure, SPF softfail, DMARC failure, and a non-contiguous ARC chain. Other fabricated details included an Exchange antispam stamp naming Microsoft’s Contoso sample domain, an ARC signature that decoded to a decimal random-number-style float, a false domain-age claim, and an impossible IP address with octets above 255. IRONSCALES Themis identified a first-time sender and malicious link, and automated detection quarantined both waves of the campaign.

  • The attacker did not try to make the email pass authentication checks; instead, the forged headers appear designed to persuade readers or workflows that merely display and trust prewritten verdicts.
  • A simple trust-boundary rule is highly actionable: identify the first Received header added by a server you control, and treat every header below it as sender-controlled text regardless of its official-looking name.
  • Contradictions can be treated as a detection signal rather than ambiguous evidence, because the message claimed an internal, low-risk status while Google's receiving infrastructure recorded authentication failures.
  • Several forged claims were cheaply verifiable: Contoso is a Microsoft sample domain, IPv4 octets cannot exceed 255, the claimed domain age conflicted with WHOIS, and the purported ARC signature decoded to a decimal float.
  • Analyst tools and LLM triage prompts should strip or clearly label headers below the trusted mail edge so forged verdicts are not presented as evidence.

  • Qilin writes per-thread execution logs to %AppData%\Local\Temp\QLOG and packages them into QLOGS.zip after encryption, which attackers can exfiltrate.
  • The malware excludes browser-related directories from encryption, which the report notes may preserve victims' ability to communicate with attackers and negotiate or pay a ransom.

The article examines Qilin, a Russian-speaking criminal group that sells ransomware tools to partners who break into organizations, lock their files, and threaten to publish stolen information unless they are paid. Its victims include organizations in the United States, Canada, Europe, and other regions, with health care especially affected; one attack on U.K. medical-services provider Synnovis disrupted London hospitals and exposed information about roughly 900,000 people. Qilin can spread through company networks and even target the systems that run virtual servers, making outages and data exposure more severe. The group’s continued growth shows that organizations can face both loss of access to essential systems and public release of sensitive data.
