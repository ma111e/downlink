Ransomware recovery CEO charged over secret ransom payments

MonsterCloud owner Zohar Pinhasi has been charged with wire-fraud offenses for allegedly secretly paying ransomware gangs for decryption keys while claiming the company used proprietary recovery technology. Prosecutors say the firm facilitated more than $8 million in ransoms and charged victims over $19 million for remediation services.
KEY POINTS
  • 01MonsterCloud owner Zohar Pinhasi was charged with one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud and two counts of wire fraud over an alleged ransomware-decryption scheme that prosecutors say operated from June 2018 through June 2023.
  • 02Prosecutors allege that MonsterCloud claimed it could recover encrypted customer data using proprietary technology, but instead commonly contacted ransomware operators and paid them for decryption keys.
  • 03The indictment alleges that MonsterCloud charged clients substantially more than the ransoms it paid, including one case involving an approximately $8,200 payment to a ransomware gang and a roughly $150,000 charge to the victim.
  • 04Prosecutors say Pinhasi and co-conspirators facilitated more than $8 million in ransom payments while charging hundreds of U.S. and Canadian companies more than $19 million for recovery and remediation services.
  • 05Pinhasi pleaded not guilty after surrendering and was released on a $2 million bond, according to the U.S. Attorney's Office.

Zohar Pinhasi, owner of Florida-based ransomware remediation company MonsterCloud, was indicted in the Eastern District of New York on charges of conspiracy to commit wire fraud and two counts of wire fraud. Prosecutors allege that from June 2018 through June 2023, he misled customers by claiming MonsterCloud could recover encrypted data using proprietary technology without paying the criminals responsible. Pinhasi surrendered, pleaded not guilty, and was released on a $2 million bond; he faces up to 20 years in prison if convicted.

According to the indictment, MonsterCloud allegedly obtained decryption keys by contacting ransomware operators and paying them, often as its first step rather than only after other recovery efforts failed. Prosecutors say the company then used decrypted files supplied through those payments as proof that it could restore customer data, while charging customers far more than the ransom amounts. The alleged operation facilitated more than \(8 million in ransom payments and charged hundreds of companies in the United States and Canada more than \)19 million for recovery and remediation services.

  • Although some MonsterCloud contracts disclosed that it might communicate with or pay cybercriminals, prosecutors allege the contracts represented this as a fallback after other recovery methods had failed rather than the usual first step.
  • The indictment alleges that decrypted sample files presented as proof of MonsterCloud’s recovery capability were supplied by the ransomware operations after payment, which could make paid decryption appear to be an independent technical solution.
  • A 2019 ProPublica investigation described a test in which researchers posing as ransomware victims received payment offers at attacker-controlled email accounts, which they traced to recovery firms including MonsterCloud.
  • The cited customer charges show that the alleged markup was not uniform: prosecutors describe one case rising from about $8,200 to $150,000 and another from about $236,000 to $380,000.

The owner of MonsterCloud, a company hired to help organizations recover files locked by criminals, has been charged with allegedly misleading customers about how it restored their data. Prosecutors say the company often paid the criminals for the unlock code, then charged victims much more while suggesting it used its own special recovery methods. Hundreds of companies in the United States and Canada may have paid for these services, showing that people hit by digital extortion can face further harm from businesses they trust to help them recover.

Evolution of Web3 in Cloud Supply Chain Attacks

Supply-chain malware is increasingly using blockchain-based Web3 mechanisms to dynamically hide and update command-and-control infrastructure while stealing cloud credentials from developer systems and CI/CD pipelines. Organizations should treat unexpected blockchain traffic from development tools as suspicious and enforce endpoint monitoring and build-pipeline integrity controls.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Threat actors are using blockchain-based Web3 mechanisms to dynamically resolve command-and-control endpoints instead of hard-coding domains or IP addresses in malicious software.
  • 02The ChainDrop npm worm infected more than 400 packages and used installation-time scripts to harvest cloud identity keys, CI/CD tokens, and short-lived OIDC federation keys.
  • 03The PolinRider campaign targeted multiple package ecosystems, including npm, Go modules, and Packagist, and used repository settings, web resources, and IDE workspace automation to run credential-stealing loaders.
  • 04The article describes an evolution from smart-contract storage through transaction input data hiding to zero-value transactions that encode an active command-and-control IP address in the recipient wallet address.
  • 05The article recommends behavioral monitoring for unexpected blockchain activity, process-level inspection of developer and CI/CD systems, and automated controls to detect unauthorized build configuration and package-script changes.

Threat actors are increasingly combining open-source software supply-chain compromises with Web3-based command-and-control infrastructure to steal cloud credentials from developers and CI/CD systems. The article highlights the ChainDrop npm worm and the PolinRider campaign, which targeted packages and developer workflows to collect cloud access keys, service-account credentials, deployment secrets, and short-lived identity tokens. These techniques can provide access to cloud management consoles and APIs and may bypass multifactor authentication when other protections are absent.

Web3 command-and-control has evolved from querying fixed smart contracts for a server address, to placing encrypted instructions in blockchain transaction data, to encoding an IPv4 address in a zero-value transaction recipient address. This makes traditional domain blocking and static indicator blocklists less effective because attackers can update infrastructure through blockchain transactions without changing malicious package code. The article recommends identifying whether blockchain traffic is expected in an organization, monitoring unexpected blockchain connections from developer tools and build runners, and detecting unauthorized changes to package scripts, repository configuration, and workspace automation before builds execute.

  • For organizations that do not use blockchain services, any outbound blockchain activity can be treated as a high-confidence anomaly rather than relying only on known malicious addresses or domains.
  • ChainDrop searches the memory of running build processes, allowing it to capture short-lived cloud credentials that may never be written to disk.
  • The NullReceiver technique can encode a command-and-control IPv4 address in a zero-value blockchain transaction’s recipient address, leaving no domain, payload, smart contract, or transaction data field for filters to inspect.
  • Malicious supply-chain packages can establish persistence through project task hooks and workspace automation, causing payloads to run again when a developer opens a project or begins an AI coding session.

The article describes how attackers hide instructions for malicious software in public blockchain networks, making it harder to shut down their communications. They spread this software through widely used free programming packages, where it can run on developers’ computers and automated software-building systems to steal cloud access keys and other secrets. This affects organizations that rely on open-source code, because one compromised package can give attackers access to many companies’ online systems. The broader risk is that attackers, including North Korea-linked groups, can gain long-term access to valuable business systems while avoiding traditional website and domain blocking.
