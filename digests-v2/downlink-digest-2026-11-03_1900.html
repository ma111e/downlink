Hackers hijack cPGuard autoupdate to deploy backdoor, steal data

Attackers hijacked OpsShield’s cPGuard update channel using compromised credentials, distributing a backdoor and data-stealing script to nearly 3,000 Linux servers. Administrators should update to cPGuard 5.88.25 or later and immediately rotate passwords, database credentials, cloud secrets, and SSH keys.
KEY POINTS
  • 01An attacker used compromised credentials to access OpsShield’s cPGuard update distribution infrastructure on November 2, 2026, and distributed a malicious script through the update channel.
  • 02The compromised cPGuard versions were 5.88.12 through 5.88.19, and OpsShield said it stopped the malicious distribution within 12 hours.
  • 03The malicious script targeted Linux password hashes, root database configuration, root shell history, and root SSH authorization and known-host files.
  • 04OpsShield said the stolen data was uploaded through a compromised account to its licensing server, and its investigation indicated the threat actor retrieved 32 files from each customer.
  • 05OpsShield said 2,946 servers were affected and advised administrators to update to cPGuard 5.88.25 or later and rotate potentially exposed credentials.

Attackers compromised OpsShield’s cPGuard update distribution infrastructure using legitimate credentials and distributed a backdoor and data-stealing script through the software’s automatic update channel. The activity began on November 2, 2026, at 02:06 UTC and affected cPGuard versions 5.88.12 through 5.88.19. OpsShield said it detected the compromise and stopped the malicious distribution within 12 hours, although some servers may not have received or run the script because update checks occur at specific intervals.

The malicious script targeted Linux password hashes, root MariaDB/MySQL configuration, root shell history, and root SSH authorization and known-host files. Stolen information was uploaded to OpsShield’s licensing server through a compromised account, and the company’s investigation indicated that the attacker retrieved 32 files from each customer. OpsShield said 2,946 servers were affected, its billing-system database was also copied, and administrators should update to cPGuard 5.88.25 or later and rotate passwords, database credentials, cloud secrets, and SSH keys.

  • Installing the fixed cPGuard version removes the backdoor and staged files, but it does not protect against credentials that may already have been stolen, so password and key rotation is separately required.
  • Not every server that received an update during the exposure window necessarily ran the malicious script, because cPGuard checks for updates at specific intervals.
  • The attackers used OpsShield’s own licensing server, through a compromised account, to collect and later retrieve the stolen information.
  • OpsShield said it will also remove previously installed support-access keys as an additional precaution, and reported that its billing-system database was copied during the incident.

Someone broke into the system that sends software updates for cPGuard, a tool used to protect Linux web servers, and used it to send harmful code to customers. The code could collect passwords, database settings, and remote-access keys from affected servers; OpsShield says nearly 3,000 servers were affected. This matters because stolen access details can let criminals return later and access websites, databases, or other services run on those servers, even after the harmful update is removed.

This is an active software-update supply-chain compromise affecting 2,946 cPGuard servers, with theft of password hashes, database configuration, shell history, and SSH-related files. Administrators using affected versions need to update immediately and rotate potentially exposed credentials and keys.

Binance says fake job interview led to $6.3 billion crypto heist

Binance says North Korean Lazarus/TraderTraitor hackers stole about $6.34 billion after luring an engineer through a fake job interview into running malware that compromised wallet infrastructure. The exchange has rebuilt affected systems and says roughly $2.85 billion was frozen or intercepted.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Binance said North Korean hackers stole about $6.34 billion in digital assets after compromising its wallet infrastructure.
  • 02The intrusion began when a Binance software engineer was deceived by a fake recruiter and downloaded a coding assignment containing previously unseen malware.
  • 03Binance said the malware replaced the legitimate Homebrew pyenv executable on the engineer’s Mac to run commands from an attacker-controlled server.
  • 04By October 20, the attackers had used the compromised engineer’s computer and an internal AI tool to compromise two additional wallet-infrastructure employees and obtain credentials and a container deployment token.
  • 05Binance said it rebuilt affected systems, replaced compromised devices, revoked stolen credentials, and that about $2.85 billion of stolen assets had been frozen or intercepted as of November 3.

Binance said North Korean hackers stole about $6.34 billion in digital assets after compromising its wallet infrastructure. The FBI attributed the incident to TraderTraitor, a North Korean state-sponsored group also tracked as Lazarus Group, Jade Sleet, and APT38. Binance said the attackers began targeting a software engineer in September through a fake job opportunity on a legitimate job-hunting platform.

The attackers conducted fake HR and CEO interviews, then persuaded the engineer to download a coding assignment from an online code repository. Binance said the assignment installed previously unseen malware that replaced the legitimate pyenv executable in the macOS Homebrew directory and allowed commands from an attacker-controlled server. By October 20, the attackers had compromised two additional wallet-infrastructure employees and obtained credentials and a container deployment token; on October 29, they used these to access the wallet signing service and transfer funds. Binance said it rebuilt affected systems, replaced compromised devices, revoked credentials, and had frozen or intercepted about $2.85 billion in stolen assets as of November 3.

  • The attackers built credibility over multiple stages, including a live video interview and a social-media account with personal photos and videos, before delivering the malicious coding assignment.
  • Binance said its endpoint security tools did not detect the custom malware at the time, and the attackers removed traces of their activity after compromising the engineer’s device.
  • A single compromised engineer’s computer was used alongside an internal AI tool to reach two additional employees working on wallet infrastructure and obtain credentials plus a container deployment token.
  • The attackers reportedly used Ethereum’s EIP-7702 authorization mechanism to keep stealing assets even after Binance suspended withdrawals.

Binance says North Korean hackers stole about $6.34 billion in cryptocurrency after tricking one of its engineers with a fake job interview and a supposedly routine coding assignment that secretly installed harmful software. The hackers then used the employee’s computer to reach systems that approve and move customer funds. This matters because a convincing fake recruiter and job test can become a path to enormous financial losses, affecting an exchange and the people whose digital assets it holds.

This is a major, named threat-actor incident involving the reported theft of about $6.34 billion from Binance wallet infrastructure. It provides concrete details on a fake-interview intrusion path, endpoint evasion, credential theft, and abuse of Ethereum’s EIP-7702 mechanism.

WorkOS says attacker used ARTEX AI to breach internal sales app

WorkOS says an attacker used the ARTEX AI offensive-security framework to breach an internal sales application, exposing business contacts, customer account histories, and communication summaries. The company says production services and WorkOS app data were not accessed, and the incident has been contained.
KEY POINTS
  • 01WorkOS identified unauthorized access on October 30, 2026, to an internal application used by its sales team.
  • 02The attacker accessed business contact details, customer account histories, and summaries or excerpts of sales calls, support discussions, and customer Slack conversations.
  • 03WorkOS said no WorkOS application data or production services were accessed or affected.
  • 04WorkOS said the attacker used an AI-powered offensive-security framework to automate reconnaissance and exploitation attempts, and founder Michael Grinich identified ARTEX AI as the likely tool.
  • 05WorkOS disabled external access to the affected application, engaged an independent incident-response firm, and said it would notify affected customers directly.

WorkOS said it detected unauthorized access on October 30, 2026, to an internal application used by its sales team. The attacker accessed business contact details, customer account histories, and summaries or excerpts from sales calls, support discussions, and customer Slack conversations. WorkOS said that no WorkOS application data or production services were accessed or affected.

The company said the attacker used an AI-powered offensive security framework to automate reconnaissance and exploitation attempts. Founder Michael Grinich said the investigation found web-request user agents including “artex-enrich/1.0” and “norma/0.4,” indicating use of the ARTEX AI penetration-testing suite. WorkOS disabled external access to the affected application, engaged an independent incident-response firm, and said it would directly notify affected customers. It also warned users to watch for phishing or social-engineering attempts using the exposed information.

  • The incident involved an internal sales application rather than WorkOS production services, but the exposed customer relationship and communication information could be used for phishing or social-engineering attempts, according to WorkOS.
  • WorkOS linked the activity to ARTEX AI using web-request user agents including "artex-enrich/1.0" and "norma/0.4."
  • ARTEX AI, originally released as an open-source offensive-security project by Chinese developers, became private after reported attacks on South Korean banks, though forks remain available.
  • The same framework has also been reported in incidents involving the DIVD nonprofit and a CrowdStrike-described campaign attributed to Vapor Panda that targeted 200 organizations.

WorkOS says someone broke into an internal tool used by its sales team and viewed business contact details, customer account information, and summaries of sales, support, and customer chat conversations. The company says its main services and customer application data were not accessed, but it will notify affected customers. The stolen details could help criminals create convincing scam emails or messages, so people connected to WorkOS customers should be alert for suspicious contact. The incident also shows that AI-based tools can speed up the work of finding and trying weaknesses in online systems.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a concrete data breach at WorkOS, a developer infrastructure provider used by thousands of companies, involving exposure of customer contacts, relationship records, and communication summaries. It also provides fresh evidence of ARTEX AI being used to automate reconnaissance and exploitation attempts, although WorkOS says production services and application data were not affected.

Google dismantles massive botnet hidden in Android TV boxes

Google, Japanese law enforcement, and cybersecurity firms disrupted the X.CPE botnet, which was preinstalled in more than 1.3 million Android TV boxes and other IoT devices allegedly tied to Chinese OEM Videostrong. The malware turned devices into residential proxies and enabled ad fraud, data theft, and installation of additional payloads.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Google, Japanese law enforcement, and cybersecurity firms disrupted the X.CPE botnet, which affected more than 1.3 million devices in over 200 countries.
  • 02Google linked the operation to Chinese Android OEM Videostrong Technology and said it cut ties with the company and barred it from making devices with preloaded Google apps.
  • 03Most affected devices were Android TV set-top boxes, though telehealth devices, karaoke machines, and digital signage devices were also found infected.
  • 04Google said the preinstalled software enabled a residential proxy network, allowing criminal affiliates to resell bandwidth, conduct ad fraud, steal data, deliver video content, and install additional software.
  • 05Google said it took legal action to disrupt domains used to control the botnet, which it said effectively rendered the malware benign by severing its communications.

Google, Japanese law enforcement, and cybersecurity firms disrupted the X.CPE botnet, which Google said was installed on more than 1.3 million devices across over 200 countries. Most affected devices were Android TV set-top boxes, although telehealth devices, karaoke machines, and digital signage systems were also found to be infected. Google linked the operation to Chinese Android original equipment manufacturer Videostrong Technology, cut ties with the company, and banned it and future subsidiaries from supplying software to certified Android vendors or selling Android devices.

Google and other investigators alleged that the malicious software was embedded in device firmware before purchase and enabled a residential proxy network by routing third-party traffic through owners’ internet connections. The operation allegedly generated revenue through resold bandwidth, data theft, video delivery, advertising fraud, extra backdoors, and paid app installations; researchers said the software could reinstall itself if users removed it. Google used legal action to disrupt domains controlling the botnet and its affiliates’ malware, saying this severed communications and effectively rendered the malware benign, while noting that the action may also have cut off criminal access to another 10 million compromised devices.

  • The malicious software was embedded in device firmware before sale and could automatically reinstall after a user tried to remove it, limiting the effectiveness of customer-led cleanup.
  • Google said the operation evolved from single-stage droppers into a modular three-stage system since late 2024, indicating it was designed for long-term resilience and use by multiple criminal affiliates.
  • Disrupting the botnet’s control domains was intended to make the malware benign without necessarily removing it from affected devices, and Google did not say whether customers would be notified or devices would be cleaned.
  • Google said the takedown may also have cut off criminal access to an additional 10 million compromised devices beyond the 1.3 million devices identified in the X.CPE campaign.

Google, Japanese police, and security companies shut down parts of a worldwide network of infected Android TV boxes and other internet-connected devices that had affected more than 1.3 million devices in over 200 countries. The harmful software was reportedly placed into devices before customers bought them and could use home internet connections for criminal activity, such as advertising fraud, stealing data, or pretending to be people in the same region. This matters because owners may not know their device was involved, while criminals can use ordinary households and businesses to hide where their activity comes from.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a high-impact disruption of a global, preinstalled-malware operation affecting more than 1.3 million Android TV and other IoT devices across over 200 countries. It is particularly relevant because the malware was embedded in firmware, could reinstall after removal, and enabled criminal use of victims’ internet connections and installation of additional payloads.
