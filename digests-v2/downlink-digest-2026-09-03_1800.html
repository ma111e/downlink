The story behind the intelligence

Cisco Talos warns that AI model guardrails are increasingly blocking legitimate security work, creating a “safety penalty” that slows defenders while attackers use less-restricted tools. Security teams should measure AI refusal rates and plan fallback or private AI options to preserve incident-response capability.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The newsletter emphasizes that producing usable threat intelligence involves messy investigative work, including dead ends, conversations, and evidence-gathering that are usually abstracted away from the final product.
  • 02Talos researcher Azim Khodjibaev’s adversary engagement work includes developing deep- and dark-web personas, directly engaging threat actors, and maintaining relationships with people who have openly threatened him.
  • 03Khodjibaev has maintained eight separate personas at once, some of which interacted with one another, and his engagements have helped Talos identify prolific cybercriminals and support wider disruption efforts.
  • 04Cisco Talos describes an AI “safety penalty,” where built-in guardrails in frontier AI models increasingly block legitimate defensive tasks and can delay incident response.
  • 05Talos recommends that security leaders audit AI refusal rates and evaluate alternatives such as private infrastructure, Model-as-a-Service platforms, or hybrid fallback systems that reroute refused prompts to an unconstrained local model.

This Threat Source newsletter looks behind the scenes at how Cisco Talos produces threat intelligence, emphasizing that the finished assessments hide a messy process of dead ends, conversations, evidence gathering, and careful writing for defenders. It highlights a Beers with Talos episode featuring Azim Khodjibaev, whose adversary engagement work includes building deep- and dark-web personas, directly engaging threat actors, and maintaining relationships with people who may openly threaten him. The article says Khodjibaev once maintained eight separate personas, some interacting with one another, and that his work helped Talos identify prolific cybercriminals and contribute to wider disruption efforts.

The newsletter’s main security focus is Cisco Talos’ warning about an AI “safety penalty,” where built-in guardrails in frontier AI models increasingly block legitimate defensive tasks. Talos says this was evident in July 2026, when Hugging Face’s primary cloud LLM refused to analyze forensic data during a breach, delaying the response. The article argues this creates an asymmetry because defenders can be slowed by refusals while adversaries can use unconstrained models to attack at machine speed.

Talos recommends that security leaders reclaim operational sovereignty by measuring AI refusal rates and ensuring they have the final say over their AI capabilities. Suggested approaches include private infrastructure, Model-as-a-Service platforms, or hybrid fallback systems that reroute refused prompts to an unconstrained local model. The newsletter also summarizes weekly security headlines, links to other Talos content, lists upcoming Talos events, and provides prevalent malware file hashes from Talos telemetry.

  • Threat actors’ behavior is not uniformly professional or highly skilled; Talos’ adversary engagement work shows a mix of technically capable operators, impulsive actors, ego-driven individuals, one-trick ponies, and less-experienced people in loose online collectives.
  • Direct engagement can provoke unusually personal reactions from criminals, including ransomware operators putting “Azim sucks” in their code and accusing the researcher of belonging to the groups he was investigating.
  • AI guardrails can become an operational dependency risk because a vendor policy change could quietly break defensive workflows overnight.
  • Talos frames the AI issue as an asymmetry: defenders may be blocked by cloud AI safety rules during legitimate investigations, while adversaries can use unconstrained models to move faster.

This newsletter explains the messy human work behind cyber threat research, including Talos researcher Azim Khodjibaev using online personas to interact with criminals, and it highlights Talos’ warning that some AI tools are now refusing to help with legitimate security work. Security teams are the ones affected when these tools block tasks during a breach, because delays can cost valuable time. The broader concern is that criminals can use less restricted tools freely while defenders may be slowed down by rules set by AI providers.

Introducing context-aware vulnerability discovery and remediation with Cloudflare Managed Defense and OpenAI Daybreak models

Cloudflare is launching early access to Vulnerability Discovery and Remediation, a Managed Defense service that uses OpenAI Daybreak models to find, validate, prioritize, and propose fixes for vulnerabilities in customer-authorized code. The key value is combining source-code analysis with Cloudflare production traffic, WAF, and security-event context so teams can focus first on exposed, actively targeted issues while retaining control over any patches or rules.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cloudflare announced early access to Vulnerability Discovery and Remediation, an invitation-only service within Cloudflare Managed Defense that helps customers detect and mitigate vulnerabilities in authorized codebases.
  • 02The service uses OpenAI Daybreak models, including GPT-5.6 Cyber, for reconnaissance, hunting, and validation, while customers decide whether proposed code patches or mitigations are implemented.
  • 03Vulnerability Discovery and Remediation combines source code analysis with Cloudflare Web Assets, WAF, Workers Observability, and traffic and security data to prioritize findings based on production exposure and security activity.
  • 04The system can recommend code patches and, when evidence supports it, Cloudflare WAF Custom rules scoped around the method, path, and request details needed to reach vulnerable code.
  • 05Model prompts are sent from Cloudflare Workers through Cloudflare AI Gateway to OpenAI servers, and no model inference runs at Cloudflare's edge or can directly apply patches or rules.

Cloudflare announced early access to Vulnerability Discovery and Remediation, an invitation-only service that is now part of Cloudflare Managed Defense. The service helps selected customers detect and mitigate vulnerabilities in codebases they authorize Cloudflare to access. Cloudflare says the service uses OpenAI Daybreak models, including GPT-5.6 Cyber, for reconnaissance, hunting, and validation, while customers remain responsible for deciding whether proposed patches or mitigations are implemented.

The article emphasizes that vulnerability prioritization is becoming harder because scanners and large language models can surface many findings quickly, while attackers can also use AI to accelerate parts of discovery and exploitation. Cloudflare says its approach adds production context by using Web Assets, WAF data, Workers Observability, and traffic and security snapshots to determine which routes are active, heavily used, or showing recent security activity. Findings can be paired with recommended code patches and, when evidence supports it, Cloudflare WAF Custom rules scoped around the request details needed to reach vulnerable code.

Cloudflare says the harness runs on Cloudflare and sends model prompts from Workers through Cloudflare AI Gateway to OpenAI Daybreak models on OpenAI servers, with no model inference running at Cloudflare’s edge. The service limits investigations to authorized source code and evidence, applies configured redaction controls, logs and checks tool calls, and validates proposed patches or rules outside the model before customer review. During early access, each engagement starts with one authorized application and uses approved read access to relevant Web Assets, WAF controls, and Workers Trace Events Logpush where available.

  • Cloudflare’s prioritization approach explicitly separates proof of a code vulnerability from evidence of real-world exposure, using traffic and security signals only to raise urgency after source-code evidence supports the finding.
  • The service is designed to avoid overbroad temporary defenses: if a route pattern contains only variables and wildcards, Cloudflare says it will not suggest a WAF rule rather than claim a connection the evidence cannot support.
  • Proposed WAF rules are validated against synthetic fixtures representing expected requests instead of being tested against customer traffic before review.
  • The workflow treats source code, logs, and request metadata as evidence to inspect rather than instructions to follow, and tool calls are logged and checked against the investigation’s access policy.
  • Network context can change the practical priority of a finding by showing whether the affected code is deployed on an active route, receives significant traffic, has recent security activity, or already has WAF protections blocking related attacks.

Cloudflare announced early access to a new Managed Defense service that uses OpenAI models to look through customer-approved code, find possible weaknesses, and suggest fixes or temporary protections. It is meant for selected Cloudflare customers who allow Cloudflare to inspect one application and connect the findings to real-world information like which website paths are actually being used and whether suspicious activity is happening there. This matters because teams can be flooded with thousands of possible problems, and attackers can use AI to move faster, so knowing which flaw is live, heavily used, and unprotected can help people fix the most important issue first.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a product announcement for Cloudflare’s early-access Vulnerability Discovery and Remediation service, not a report of active exploitation, a breach, or a critical patch. It may be useful for Cloudflare customers evaluating managed vulnerability prioritization and mitigation, but it is low urgency for most security professionals.

Microsoft Exchange Vulnerability CVE-2026-62911: What Administrators Should Do and How Zscaler Can Help

Microsoft patched CVE-2026-62911, a high-severity Exchange Server authentication bypass that can let a low-privileged attacker take over all mailboxes, but thousands of internet-exposed servers remain unpatched and public exploit code exists. Administrators should patch immediately or remove Exchange from direct internet access, with the article recommending Zscaler’s zero-trust access controls as a way to reduce exposure.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Microsoft’s August 2026 Patch Tuesday included a fix for CVE-2026-62911, a high-severity authentication bypass vulnerability affecting Exchange Server 2016, 2019, and Subscription Edition.
  • 02Shadowserver identified around 22,000 Exchange servers that remained unpatched and exposed to the internet as of September 1, including roughly 6,200 in the United States and 5,100 in Germany.
  • 03Microsoft said successful exploitation of CVE-2026-62911 allows an attacker with basic privileges to take over all mailboxes on the targeted server, including reading and sending email and downloading attachments.
  • 04The Netherlands National Cyber Security Centre confirmed that working exploit code for CVE-2026-62911 is already publicly available.
  • 05The article recommends applying the August 2026 update immediately, and if patching is delayed, ensuring the Exchange server is not reachable from the open internet.

Microsoft’s August 2026 Patch Tuesday included a fix for CVE-2026-62911, a high-severity authentication bypass vulnerability affecting Exchange Server 2016, 2019, and Subscription Edition. Microsoft gave the vulnerability a CVSS score of 8.0. According to Microsoft, successful exploitation allows an attacker with basic privileges to take over all mailboxes on the targeted server, including reading and sending email and downloading attachments.

As of September 1, Shadowserver identified around 22,000 Exchange servers that remained unpatched and exposed to the internet, including roughly 6,200 in the United States and 5,100 in Germany. The Netherlands National Cyber Security Centre confirmed that working exploit code is already publicly available. The article says administrators should apply the August 2026 update immediately, and if patching is not immediately possible, ensure the Exchange server is not reachable from the open internet.

The article argues that the broader issue is internet exposure for private application servers such as on-premises Exchange, because Outlook Web App is commonly reachable from the public internet. It recommends using Zscaler Private Access to keep Exchange accessible to users while making it invisible from the internet, and describes options for managed and unmanaged devices. It also discusses additional Zscaler controls for suspected compromise, lateral movement containment, and exploit protection, and suggests evaluating Microsoft 365 migration as a longer-term way to remove the on-premises Exchange attack surface.

  • The article frames internet exposure, not just missing patches, as the recurring structural problem for on-premises Exchange because Outlook Web App is typically reachable from the public internet.
  • NCSC-NL’s recommendation to make Exchange accessible only internally creates an operational challenge for organizations that still need remote access, which the article positions as a reason to use an access broker rather than direct public exposure.
  • The article argues that patching alone is becoming less reliable for internet-facing systems because public exploit code and frontier AI models can sharply compress the time between disclosure and exploitation.
  • Exchange 2016 and 2019 users face a near-term planning deadline because their Extended Security Update support ends in October 2026, after which they will no longer receive security fixes.
  • The article presents Microsoft 365 migration as a strategic way to remove the on-premises Exchange attack surface, while noting that private applications will still remain necessary for confidentiality, restricted access, and compliance.

Microsoft fixed a serious security flaw in Exchange Server 2016, 2019, and Subscription Edition that could let someone with only basic access take control of all mailboxes on a server, including reading and sending email and downloading attachments. The article says about 22,000 Exchange servers were still unpatched and reachable from the internet as of September 1, including many in the United States and Germany, and working attack code is already public. This matters because organizations that run their own Exchange servers may have very little time to fix problems before attackers can use them, especially as newer AI tools can speed up attacks.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a specific, broadly relevant Exchange Server vulnerability with an available patch, public exploit code, and thousands of exposed unpatched servers. It is important for defenders of on-premises Exchange, though the article says CISA had not reported exploitation in the wild at the time of writing.
