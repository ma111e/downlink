Baker Tilly notifies clients of data breach at third-party provider

Baker Tilly disclosed that a breach at an unnamed third-party service provider exposed client and dependent data, including names, addresses, and Social Security numbers. More than 10,000 Texans were affected, and the firm is offering 24 months of Experian credit and identity monitoring.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Baker Tilly said an unauthorized actor obtained data relating to certain clients from a platform operated by a third-party service provider.
  • 02The company became aware of the potential incident on September 6, 2026, and completed its review of the affected data on October 28, 2026.
  • 03Texas Attorney General data shows that 10,456 Texas residents were affected, with Social Security numbers, full names, addresses, and other information listed as exposed.
  • 04Baker Tilly said it found no evidence of unauthorized activity on its own network and had received no reports of identity theft or fraud linked to the incident.
  • 05The firm is offering affected individuals 24 months of credit and identity monitoring through Experian.

Baker Tilly Advisory Group is notifying certain clients that an unauthorized actor obtained data stored on a platform operated by an unnamed third-party service provider. The firm became aware of the potential incident on September 6, 2026, and completed its review of the affected data on October 28, 2026. Baker Tilly said it contained the incident, engaged external experts, and notified law enforcement.

The company has not disclosed the total number of affected people, the provider involved, or the precise information exposed in its sample notice. However, the Texas Attorney General reports that 10,456 people in Texas were affected and lists Social Security numbers, full names, addresses, and other information among the exposed data types. Baker Tilly said it has no evidence of unauthorized activity on its own network and no reports of identity theft or fraud tied to the exposure, and it is offering 24 months of Experian credit and identity monitoring to affected individuals.

  • The public customer notification leaves the specific data types as a placeholder and does not identify the third-party provider, while the Texas Attorney General portal provides more detail about the exposed information.
  • Baker Tilly said it found no evidence of unauthorized activity on its own network, indicating the disclosed exposure was associated with a platform operated by an outside provider used for client services.
  • Although the total number of affected people was not disclosed, Texas reporting alone lists 10,456 impacted individuals, showing that the incident affected a substantial number of clients or dependents.
  • BleepingComputer found no public ransomware-group claim for the incident at the time the article was published.

Baker Tilly says someone obtained personal information belonging to some of its clients and their dependents from a system run by an outside service provider. The exposed information may include names, home addresses, Social Security numbers, and other details; more than 10,000 people in Texas were affected. This matters because such information can be used for identity theft or fraud, although Baker Tilly says it has not received reports tying either to this incident and is offering affected people two years of monitoring services.

This is a concrete third-party data exposure involving Social Security numbers, names, and addresses, with at least 10,456 affected people in Texas. It is relevant for professionals tracking supply-chain and client-data risks, but the provider, attack method, full scope, and broader operational impact remain undisclosed.

Compromised GitHub accounts push CI secret stealer to tens of thousands of repositories

A threat actor used thousands of compromised GitHub accounts to inject short-lived malicious CI workflows into tens of thousands of repositories, stealing GitHub Actions secrets, cloud credentials, and other runner data. Organizations should immediately investigate the listed branches, pull requests, commits, and workflow indicators and rotate any credentials accessible to affected workflows.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Beginning November 10, a threat actor used stolen GitHub credentials to open probe pull requests in 61,764 repositories and then inject malicious payloads designed to steal CI secrets and cloud credentials.
  • 02The article identified evidence that the payload ran in at least 1,806 repositories, while the attacker deleted workflow runs and logs after collecting credentials.
  • 03Affected repositories may contain a wfx-docs branch, a pull request titled "docs: dependency audit notes," or commits such as "ci: refresh build cache" and "chore: add ci helper."
  • 04The payload can collect GitHub Actions secrets and variables, workflow tokens, cloud credentials, runner environment data, repository contents, caches, and build artifacts.
  • 05The campaign reached Packagist package digitalelvis/neuronai-studio version 3.14.3 and GitHub Go module github.com/onuragtas/docker-env versions v1.0.278 and v1.0.280.

Starting November 10, a threat actor used stolen GitHub credentials to open probe pull requests and push malicious payloads into tens of thousands of repositories. The campaign used compromised accounts to reach 61,764 repositories, including 10,431 repositories that the account did not own but could access through collaborator or organization permissions. The probe pull request was titled docs: dependency audit notes and commonly originated from a wfx-docs branch.

The malicious payload was designed to collect GitHub Actions secrets and variables, workflow tokens, cloud credentials, runner environment data, repository contents, caches, and build artifacts. The attacker generally reverted the changes within one minute and deleted workflow runs and logs, leaving a missing workflow-run number as a possible remaining trace. The article identified evidence that the payload ran in at least 1,806 repositories and advises affected organizations to assume every secret and cloud role available to their workflows was compromised.

  • A "docs: dependency audit notes" pull request was used as an access probe, so its presence indicates the account was compromised even when the malicious workflow did not run.
  • More than 10,000 probe pull requests targeted repositories the compromised account did not own, showing that collaborator and organization access expanded the campaign beyond users' personal repositories.
  • The attacker typically reverted changes within one minute and deleted workflow logs, meaning a missing workflow run number may be the only remaining evidence of execution.
  • The identified Go module versions contain the payload only in GitHub Actions workflow files, so their source code and release binaries were unchanged and normal Go builds do not execute the malicious workflow.

Someone used stolen GitHub account access to add hidden harmful instructions to work projects, affecting tens of thousands of code repositories. In at least 1,806 cases, those instructions ran and sent private project passwords, cloud-service access details, and other sensitive information to the attacker. Anyone who can change or manage a GitHub project that saw the listed suspicious branches, pull requests, or commit messages may be affected, and should treat information available to its automated build system as exposed. The campaign also reached a small number of published software packages, creating risk for people who installed the affected version.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is an active, high-impact supply-chain and CI compromise campaign using stolen GitHub credentials across 61,764 repositories, with confirmed credential exfiltration from at least 1,806. Security teams with GitHub repositories should urgently investigate the listed indicators and rotate secrets and cloud roles exposed to affected workflows.

ARTEX and AI-Powered Red Teaming Tools: What Defenders Need to Know

Unit 42 reports that attackers are using AI-powered red-team frameworks such as ARTEX to autonomously probe exposed identity flows and APIs, rapidly validate credentials, and pursue cloud access at scale. Defenders should reduce public attack surface, protect self-service and write endpoints, remove exposed specifications and secrets, and detect the distinctive high-volume, adaptive behavior of autonomous agents.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Unit 42 observed ARTEX in intrusion attempts against multiple targets and investigated intrusions where attacker infrastructure hosted an ARTEX console or used ARTEX-associated infrastructure and user agents.
  • 02As of October 2026, Unit 42 identified 1,323 internet-facing hosts running CyberStrikeAI and 609 running ARTEX, including 21 hosts running both tools.
  • 03The article states that ARTEX can adapt after failed attempts, target internet-exposed identity workflows and APIs, and use associated credentials and trust relationships to reach cloud environments.
  • 04Observed ARTEX-like activity included brief request bursts followed by 25–31 second pauses, rapid validation of more than 200 client secrets, immediate targeting of routes found in OpenAPI specifications and source maps, and successful retries after HTTP 422 errors.
  • 05Unit 42 recommends removing public OpenAPI specifications, source maps and configuration files containing tokens or client IDs; requiring authentication for every write endpoint; hardening password-reset and registration flows; and hunting for behavior such as canary usernames, new-IP cloud-key use, and write-then-delete storage tests.

Unit 42 reports that attackers are using AI-enabled red-teaming and penetration-testing tools, including ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI, to autonomously conduct offensive operations. The organization observed ARTEX in intrusion attempts against multiple targets, including cases where attacker infrastructure hosted an ARTEX console or used ARTEX-associated infrastructure and user agents. ARTEX is described as an open-source, multi-agent AI penetration-testing framework that can evaluate responses, adapt after failures, and continue pursuing an objective. Unit 42 states that attackers can use it against internet-exposed identity workflows and APIs, then use obtained credentials and trust relationships to reach cloud environments.

As of October 2026, Unit 42 identified 1,323 internet-facing CyberStrikeAI hosts and 609 ARTEX hosts, with 21 running both tools. Observed behavior associated with autonomous tooling included request bursts followed by 25–31 second pauses, rapid credential validation, immediate targeting of paths disclosed in OpenAPI specifications and source maps, retries after HTTP 422 errors, and repeated polling loops. Unit 42 recommends reducing public exposure by removing public attack maps and exposed tokens, authenticating every write endpoint, strengthening password-reset and registration flows, moving secrets into a secrets manager, and enforcing least privilege. It also advises detecting behavior such as canary usernames, cloud-key use from new IP addresses, write-then-delete storage tests, and early probing that precedes later intrusion activity.

  • Unit 42 found that automated ARTEX activity could turn public password-reset pages and exposed APIs into a route to cloud access without exploiting a software vulnerability.
  • The observed request pattern of one to 14 requests followed by 25–31 second pauses may reveal an automated system waiting for an AI model response, providing a behavioral detection opportunity even when infrastructure changes.
  • Current autonomous testing frameworks can be highly conspicuous: Unit 42 reported that Cairn generated hundreds of thousands of high-frequency requests across multiple edge protocols from one source IP address.
  • Generic error responses and silent connection throttling may hinder AI-driven tools because they rely on target responses to decide their next actions, potentially causing them to waste computing resources.
  • Unit 42 observed humans or browser automation still participating in sensitive stages of some operations, including password resets and initial token requests, despite the use of autonomous tooling.

Researchers at Unit 42 say attackers are using tools such as ARTEX, which use artificial intelligence to automatically test public-facing websites and online services for ways to get in. These tools can quickly try password-reset pages, application connections, and exposed digital keys, then change their approach when something fails. Organizations of any size or industry with publicly reachable systems may be affected, because this automation lets attackers test many targets at once. The stakes are higher because attackers can move faster and more persistently, so organizations need to limit what is exposed online and better protect accounts and access keys.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

Unit 42 reports observed ARTEX-associated intrusion attempts and a coordinated campaign against cloud environments, with concrete behavioral indicators and defensive actions. The findings have broad relevance because the tools target public identity workflows and APIs and can rapidly validate credentials and pursue cloud access.
