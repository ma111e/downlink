The DKIM Passed. Nobody Spoofed Anything.

A phishing email sent through a compromised legitimate mailbox passed SPF, DKIM, and ARC after being auto-forwarded through a partner’s Microsoft 365 tenant and re-signed by the recipient’s Google Workspace group. A DKIM pass—especially on the recipient’s own key—does not prove a message is trustworthy; organizations should scrutinize forwarded mail, group posting policies, and misaligned header From domains.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The phishing message originated through authenticated SMTP submission to a Japanese small-business Postfix server using valid credentials for a real mailbox.
  • 02A partner organization’s Microsoft 365 tenant automatically forwarded the message to the European cyber-insurance provider’s Google Workspace administrative group, despite Microsoft having assigned it an SCL:9 spam score.
  • 03The recipient’s Google group redistributed and re-signed the message, causing delivery authentication to show DKIM and SPF passes on the recipient’s own domain while DMARC failed for the visible From domain.
  • 04The email body consisted of “Fix below” and a single redirector link that led to a numeric-prefixed Azure Front Door host with an independent malicious scanner verdict.
  • 05The article recommends reviewing which domain supplied a passing DKIM signature, investigating header From domains that no organization in the delivery path owns, and auditing inbound partner auto-forwards and group posting policies.

A phishing email originated from a compromised real mailbox at a Japanese small business after an attacker authenticated to its Postfix server with valid SMTP credentials. The sender used a display-name impersonation of a communications platform, a subject line the vendor genuinely uses, and a two-word body—“Fix below”—with one link. The message was first sent to a partner organization’s Microsoft 365 tenant, which had already classified it as spam with an SCL:9 score.

The partner tenant automatically forwarded the email to a European cyber-insurance provider’s Google Workspace administrative group, despite the upstream spam classification not being carried forward. The Google group redistributed and re-signed the message, so final delivery to the chief revenue officer showed DKIM and SPF passes tied to the recipient organization’s own infrastructure, along with six valid ARC seals, while DMARC failed for the visible From domain. The article emphasizes that a passing DKIM result can show only that a recipient-side system imported and signed a message, not that the visible sender or message is trustworthy; it recommends examining partner auto-forwards, group posting policies, and cases where the visible From domain is not owned by anyone in the delivery path.

  • A valid DKIM signature from the recipient organization’s own Google Workspace key can indicate that a group imported and redistributed a message, rather than proving that the visible sender is legitimate.
  • The partner tenant’s SCL:9 spam verdict did not protect the final recipient because the automatic forwarding process relayed the email without carrying Microsoft’s spam judgment to the next organization.
  • ARC seals accurately preserved each provider’s observed authentication results, but the six valid seals also carried the authentication of a compromised legitimate mailbox through the delivery chain.
  • The DMARC failure was a meaningful warning because the visible From domain was not owned by any organization in the enumerated delivery path, while the identities that passed authentication belonged to recipient-side or partner infrastructure.
  • A two-word lure and a single link were sufficient to appear credible after the message reached an administrative distribution list through trusted organizational email plumbing.

Someone stole the password for a real small-business email account and used it to send a fake message pretending to come from a well-known communications company. The message was automatically passed through a partner company and then into a European cyber-insurance provider’s internal email group, ultimately reaching its chief revenue officer. Because the message travelled through legitimate systems using real accounts, common email trust checks made it look safe even though it contained a malicious link. This shows that organizations can be exposed not only by fake senders, but also by compromised accounts and automatic forwarding rules at trusted partners.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a concrete phishing case showing how compromised legitimate credentials, partner auto-forwarding, and internal group redistribution can make malicious email appear authenticated. It is useful for email-security professionals because it identifies actionable header and forwarding patterns, but it does not describe a broadly active named campaign or an urgent vulnerability.
