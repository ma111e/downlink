RingCentral data breach exposed info of 1.6 million accounts

ShinyHunters stole and leaked data tied to 1.6 million RingCentral accounts after a July breach reportedly involving social engineering. Exposed records include names, email addresses, phone numbers, and physical addresses.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Have I Been Pwned said leaked RingCentral data contained records for 1.6 million accounts, including names, email addresses, phone numbers, and physical addresses.
  • 02RingCentral disclosed on July 28 that its systems were compromised following what it described as a sophisticated social engineering campaign.
  • 03RingCentral said the incident affected data for a limited portion of customers, that it is communicating with affected customers directly, and that its core platform and services were not disrupted.
  • 04ShinyHunters claimed responsibility for the breach on July 27 and said it had stolen 623GB of data.
  • 05After RingCentral refused to pay a ransom to have the stolen data destroyed, ShinyHunters leaked a compressed archive containing 280GB of files on its dark web leak site.

The ShinyHunters extortion group stole personal information from 1.6 million RingCentral accounts after hacking the company in July, according to Have I Been Pwned. RingCentral is a cloud-based collaboration and communication platform used by more than 600,000 businesses for calling, messaging, and voicemail. The company disclosed the incident on July 28 and said its systems were compromised after a “sophisticated social engineering campaign.”

RingCentral said the incident affected data for a limited portion of customers and that it is communicating with affected customers directly. The company also said customers who are not contacted are not affected, and that the incident did not impact the core RingCentral platform or disrupt services. Have I Been Pwned said the leaked data contained names, email addresses, phone numbers, and physical addresses.

ShinyHunters claimed responsibility on July 27 and said it had stolen 623GB of data, although RingCentral has not attributed the breach to a specific group. After RingCentral refused to pay a ransom to have the stolen data destroyed, the group leaked a compressed archive containing 280GB of files on its dark web leak site. The article notes that ShinyHunters has also claimed or been linked to other data-theft activity involving Salesforce customers, Salesloft Drift, Salesforce Aura, Snowflake customers, third-party integration providers, and Oracle PeopleSoft servers.

  • Have I Been Pwned’s analysis provided confirmation of the exposed account count and data types even though RingCentral had not publicly attributed the incident to ShinyHunters.
  • RingCentral said customers who are not contacted by the company are not affected, making direct notification the stated way for customers to determine their exposure.
  • The incident shows that a data theft can affect customer information even when the company says its core platform continued operating without disruption.
  • The article places the RingCentral incident within a broader ShinyHunters pattern involving Salesforce customers, Snowflake customers, third-party integration providers, and Oracle PeopleSoft servers.

RingCentral, a service used by more than 600,000 businesses for calls, messages, and voicemail, said some of its systems were broken into after a social engineering campaign, and Have I Been Pwned says information from 1.6 million accounts was exposed. The exposed details included names, email addresses, phone numbers, and physical addresses, and RingCentral said it is contacting affected customers directly. Max severity SAP Commerce Cloud flaw now targeted in attacks

Attackers are already targeting CVE-2026-58231, a maximum-severity unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in SAP Commerce Cloud, just three days after SAP patched it. Organizations running exposed SAP Commerce Cloud instances should apply the update immediately and check for signs of exploitation.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Defused reported that CVE-2026-58231, a maximum-severity SAP Commerce Cloud remote code execution vulnerability patched three days earlier, is already being targeted in attacks.
  • 02CVE-2026-58231 is an improper authorization flaw in the core Data Hub Adapter extension for SAP Commerce Cloud that unauthenticated attackers can exploit in low-complexity attacks to execute arbitrary code.
  • 03SAP said successful exploitation of CVE-2026-58231 could compromise internal components and have a high impact on the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the application.
  • 04Shadowserver tracks more than 4,200 IP addresses with a SAP Commerce Cloud fingerprint, mostly in Europe and North America, but the article says it is unclear how many are honeypots or already secured.
  • 05SAP has not yet flagged CVE-2026-58231 as actively exploited in its security advisory, while Defused said exploitation attempts are hitting its honeypots despite no public proof-of-concept being available.

A maximum-severity SAP Commerce Cloud remote code execution vulnerability patched three days ago is already being targeted in attacks, according to threat intelligence company Defused. The flaw is tracked as CVE-2026-58231 and affects the core Data Hub Adapter extension for SAP Commerce Cloud, formerly known as SAP Hybris. SAP Commerce Cloud is a cloud-based e-commerce platform used by online stores owned by high-profile global brands and large retailers.

SAP said the issue allows an unauthenticated attacker to abuse a default authentication client and submit specially crafted input to functions that lack sufficient validation. Successful exploitation could enable arbitrary code execution and compromise internal components, with high impact on the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the application. Defused said exploitation attempts were hitting its honeypots three days after patch day, even though there was no public proof-of-concept and the vulnerability was not known to be exploited.

SAP had not yet flagged CVE-2026-58231 as actively exploited in its security advisory issued Tuesday. Shadowserver tracks more than 4,200 IP addresses with a SAP Commerce Cloud fingerprint, mostly in Europe and North America, but the article says it is unclear how many are honeypots or have already been secured. The article also notes that SAP recently fixed multiple other vulnerabilities affecting Commerce Cloud and that CISA has added 14 SAP vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog since November 2021.

  • The exploitation attempts began within three days of patch availability, despite Defused stating there was no public proof-of-concept and the flaw was not previously known to be exploited.
  • There is a gap between third-party threat intelligence and SAP’s advisory status, as Defused observed exploitation attempts while SAP had not yet marked the vulnerability as actively exploited.
  • The exposed attack surface may be significant because Shadowserver tracks more than 4,200 internet-facing IP addresses with a SAP Commerce Cloud fingerprint, but the article notes that their actual exposure or patch status is unknown.
  • The vulnerability is especially actionable for defenders because SAP’s description says unauthenticated attackers can abuse a default authentication client and submit specially crafted input to vulnerable functions.

A serious flaw in SAP Commerce Cloud, the online shopping platform used by major brands and large retailers, was fixed this week but is already being targeted by attackers. The flaw can let someone with no account or special access run their own code inside affected stores’ systems. More than 4,200 internet-facing systems show signs of using SAP Commerce Cloud, mostly in Europe and North America, though it is unclear how many are still vulnerable. Stealing the Artifact – JFrog Artifactory Vulnerability

NetSPI found a chain of JFrog Artifactory vulnerabilities that let an unauthenticated attacker mint an anonymous JWT, bypass authorization, and retrieve restricted artifacts from affected repositories. JFrog has patched the issues in Artifactory 7.146.8 and several supported maintenance releases.
KEY POINTS
  • 01NetSPI discovered vulnerabilities in JFrog Artifactory that allowed an unauthenticated attacker to bypass authentication and authorization to retrieve an arbitrary artifact from an Artifactory repository.
  • 02At the time of discovery, all versions of Artifactory OSS and Artifactory Enterprise were affected by the vulnerabilities.
  • 03CVE-2026-42018 allowed authentication bypass to the anonymous user and was remediated in Artifactory 7.146.8.
  • 04CVE-2026-69107 covered potential unauthorized artifact access and was remediated in Artifactory versions 7.146.8, 7.133.21, 7.125.14, 7.117.21, 7.111.14, and 7.104.16.
  • 05The attack chain combined four bugs: an anonymous JWT mint authentication bypass, missing authorization in stash results, path traversal in stash export, and URL path confusion.

NetSPI discovered vulnerabilities in JFrog Artifactory that allowed an unauthenticated attacker to bypass authentication and authorization and retrieve an arbitrary artifact from an Artifactory repository. At the time of discovery, all versions of Artifactory OSS and Artifactory Enterprise were affected. The article emphasizes that Artifactory is often a central hub in the software supply chain, storing internal applications, libraries, release packages, infrastructure artifacts, and other valuable files.

The attack chain involved four bugs: Authentication Bypass via Anonymous JWT Mint, Missing Authorization in Stash Results, Path Traversal in Stash Export, and URL Path Confusion. The authentication bypass allowed an attacker to obtain a JWT for the anonymous user by sending a request with a trailing slash to the AWS token endpoint, even when anonymous access was disabled. The stash results issue allowed artifact metadata, including content-addressing hashes, to be placed into an attacker-controlled HTTP session without checking whether the user had permission to read the artifact.

The path traversal issue in stash export allowed the stashed artifact content to be copied to an attacker-influenced file system location with a timestamped directory name. In production-style deployments, the URL path confusion issue helped route external traffic through jf-router to the Tomcat backend and access the copied artifact from the web root. JFrog remediated CVE-2026-42018 in Artifactory 7.146.8 and CVE-2026-69107 in versions 7.146.8, 7.133.21, 7.125.14, 7.117.21, 7.111.14, and 7.104.16.

  • The authentication bypass worked even when the anonymous user was disabled because the control enforcing that setting was scoped to different login and reauthentication endpoints, not the AWS token exchange path used in the attack chain.
  • The deprecated stash search results feature exposed a useful attacker primitive because it could create a fresh HTTP session for a bearer-token request and bind attacker-chosen stash names to artifact metadata without checking whether the user could read the artifact.
  • The stash results bug also acted as an oracle for enumerating repository names and artifact paths, since valid targets returned HTTP 200 while missing artifacts produced an error.
  • In production deployments, stealing the copied artifact required a separate routing confusion issue because external users normally reached Artifactory through jf-router rather than directly reaching the backend Tomcat server.
  • The exploit depended on a small timestamp brute force because Artifactory appended a timestamp to the attacker-controlled stash name when creating the export directory.

NetSPI found several flaws in JFrog Artifactory that could let someone with no login get a hidden software file from a company’s storage system. All versions of Artifactory OSS and Artifactory Enterprise were affected when the flaws were found, and JFrog later fixed them in updated versions. This matters because Artifactory often holds a company’s internal software, release files, and other important building blocks, which can reveal valuable private code, system details, or secret keys if stolen.

This is a specific, high-impact vulnerability analysis affecting JFrog Artifactory, a central software supply chain platform, with an unauthenticated attack chain for retrieving restricted artifacts. It is not described as active exploitation in the wild, but the breadth of affected versions, clear technical detail, and available remediations make it important for security professionals to read.

The Tracking Parameter That Named the Wrong Company

A Microsoft Teams-themed phishing email used multiple nested security-vendor redirects and obfuscation to hide a likely credential-harvesting path, but a stale tracking parameter exposed reuse of the same phishing kit from a prior campaign. The key lesson is to hunt the reusable template artifacts and redirect structure rather than relying on sender authentication or recognizable vendor link wrappers.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A fabricated Microsoft Teams audio-message notification reached a director-level mailbox at a mid-size forensic engineering and investigative consulting firm in late July 2026.
  • 02The message’s button hid a four-layer redirect chain involving multiple vendor link wrappers and ultimately pointed to a payment-themed path on a Brazilian law firm’s long-registered domain.
  • 03A stale recipient-tracking parameter in the final redirect named an employee at an unrelated organization, indicating reused phishing-kit scaffolding rather than involvement by that organization.
  • 04The lure used hidden CSS, zero-width text, and injected hex-like fragments inside Microsoft Teams brand wording to make the message appear normal to humans while disrupting raw-text scanning.
  • 05SPF and composite authentication passed, while DKIM was absent and DMARC returned a best-guess pass, showing that authentication results did not prevent the message from being classified as credential theft.

A fabricated Microsoft Teams audio-message notification reached a director-level mailbox at a mid-size forensic engineering and investigative consulting firm in late July 2026. The message used the subject line “Notification” and presented a single “Listen to Message” button. Themis scored the message at 82% confidence, labeled it as credential theft and VIP recipient, and resolved the report as phishing automatically.

The button’s destination contained a four-layer redirect chain involving a secure-link wrapper, a base64url object, a threat-scanning endpoint, a link-shield redirect, and a final payment-themed path on a Brazilian law firm’s long-registered domain. A stale recipient-tracking parameter in the final redirect named an employee address at an unrelated organization. The article states this did not show that the named organization was breached, attacked, or involved, but instead showed reused phishing-kit scaffolding.

The lure also used hidden CSS, zero-width text, and injected hex-like fragments inside Microsoft Teams brand wording to disrupt raw-text scanning while appearing normal to a human reader. SPF passed, DKIM was absent, DMARC returned a best-guess pass, and composite authentication passed, but the article emphasizes that these results did not establish the message as safe. Over the following week, four more mailboxes at the same firm received the same template, and each was automatically quarantined within seconds of delivery.

  • A recognized email-security vendor hostname inside a link should not be treated as proof that the final destination was vetted, because the article shows the visible wrapper hops received verdicts while the deeper landing page was not independently confirmed as scanned.
  • The stale recipient-tracking parameter is useful as a kit-reuse fingerprint because it shows the same redirect and tracking scaffolding was repurposed from an earlier target without being cleaned.
  • Blocking the apparent sending or landing domains could penalize unrelated legitimate businesses, since the article says both domains belonged to bystanders and the attacker’s durable asset was the reused template.
  • The most durable hunting signals in this case were structural artifacts such as the nesting order, encoding pattern, hidden CSS class, injected fragments, and tracking leftovers rather than any single domain name.
  • Email authentication passing did not establish that the message was safe, because the phishing classification relied on sender behavior and message structure despite SPF and composite authentication passing.

A fake Microsoft Teams audio-message email was sent to a director at a forensic engineering and investigative consulting firm, with a button that hid several layers of forwarding before reaching a page on a Brazilian law firm’s domain. One hidden detail in the link still named an employee at an unrelated company, showing the scam template had been reused and that the named company was not necessarily involved or harmed. This matters because real businesses’ email systems and websites can be borrowed to make scams look trustworthy, so simply trusting familiar-looking links or passed email checks can leave people exposed to password theft.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a concrete phishing case with useful technical detail about nested redirect chains, stale tracking parameters, and obfuscation artifacts that can inform detection and hunting. It is not a breaking high-impact incident or named threat actor operation, so it is a solid but lower-urgency technical read.
