Rackspace shuts down Spot cloud platform after security breach

Rackspace will permanently shut down its Rackspace Spot cloud platform on December 8, 2026, after compromised credentials let attackers access customer Kubernetes clusters, secrets, and storage files. Customers must migrate workloads and rotate potentially exposed tokens, kubeconfigs, and Kubernetes Secrets.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Rackspace will permanently discontinue its Rackspace Spot cloud platform on December 8, 2026, at 5 PM Central Time, after which remaining workloads will be terminated and customer data will no longer be accessible.
  • 02The company decided to wind down Rackspace Spot after attackers used compromised credentials on November 17 to access customer Kubernetes clusters and potentially read secrets and files on storage volumes.
  • 03Rackspace discovered the intrusion on November 18 after a customer report, disabled the platform administration console, and restored service on November 21.
  • 04Rackspace advises all Spot customers to treat API tokens, refresh tokens, kubeconfigs, and Kubernetes Secrets as potentially compromised and to review access logs beginning November 16.
  • 05Rackspace says the affected Spot environment is segmented from its core infrastructure and that it found no evidence of unauthorized access to its core systems or other Rackspace customer environments.

Rackspace will permanently discontinue its Rackspace Spot cloud platform on December 8, 2026, at 5 PM Central Time. All remaining workloads will be terminated at that time, and customer data will no longer be accessible. The company told customers to retrieve data and move their workloads to other platforms before the deadline.

The decision followed a November 17 intrusion in which attackers used compromised credentials to access the Spot platform. They were able to create unauthorized pods in customer Kubernetes clusters, read Kubernetes Secrets, and list nodes and volumes; Rackspace said they may also have read files on storage volumes. Rackspace discovered the incident after a customer report on November 18, disabled the administration console, and restored service on November 21.

Rackspace advises all Spot customers to treat API tokens, refresh tokens, kubeconfigs, and Kubernetes Secrets as potentially compromised, even if they did not receive an earlier breach notice. Customers are also advised to review access logs from November 16 onward and search for activity associated with IP address 23.224.79.106, the 23.224.0.0/15 range, and object prefixes including i1, glm-, and probe. Rackspace says the Spot environment is segmented from its core infrastructure and that it found no evidence of access to core systems or other Rackspace customer environments.

  • Rackspace is telling every Spot customer to treat credentials as potentially exposed, including customers who did not previously receive an individual breach notification.
  • Customers can use the listed IP address, IP range, and Kubernetes object prefixes—23.224.79.106, 23.224.0.0/15, i1, glm-, and probe—to review activity dating back to November 16.
  • Although Rackspace restored Spot service on November 21, it later chose to retire the platform entirely rather than continue operating it.
  • Some customers said Spot’s affordability was a major reason they used it and expressed concern that alternatives would cost more.

Rackspace is closing its Spot cloud service after someone used stolen login details to enter parts of the platform and potentially view customers’ confidential access information and stored files. Organizations that use Spot must move their applications and download their data before December 8, 2026, when the service will be turned off permanently. The incident matters because exposed access details could let outsiders reach customers’ systems, while the shutdown may force them to find more expensive replacement services.

New Inception extortion gang launches large-scale vishing campaign

The new Inception extortion group is running a large-scale phone-based phishing campaign using fake Okta and OpenText authentication sites to steal corporate SSO credentials. Researchers and Okta assess it is likely a rebrand of a ShinyHunters-linked cluster, though Inception denies the connection.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The newly formed Inception extortion group has been linked to a large-scale campaign targeting single sign-on credentials through phone-based social engineering and fake authentication websites.
  • 02Inception appeared publicly on November 17 and claimed seven victims, including AMD, PepsiCo, Cigna Healthcare, AutoZone, and Lucid Motors, although none has publicly confirmed a data breach.
  • 03ReversingLabs identified 21 domains used for single sign-on phishing, 41 domains believed to support the campaign infrastructure, and four additional suspicious domains.
  • 04Okta assessed that Inception is very likely a rebranding of O-UNC-053, a threat cluster previously associated with ShinyHunters, based on consistent infrastructure and tactics.
  • 05ReversingLabs recommends phishing-resistant sign-in methods such as FIDO2 security keys and passkeys, stronger help desk identity checks, and review of relevant logs dating to October 21, 2026.

A newly formed data-extortion group called Inception has been linked to a large campaign aimed at stealing single sign-on credentials from global companies. The group appeared publicly on November 17, claiming seven victims including AMD, PepsiCo, Cigna Healthcare, AutoZone, and Lucid Motors, although none has confirmed a breach. ReversingLabs says it has tracked the group since late October.

Researchers found 21 domains used for single sign-on phishing, 41 believed to support the campaign, and four additional suspicious domains. The infrastructure impersonates many organizations and uses legal-themed websites that imitate Okta and OpenText Access Manager sign-in services. The operation reportedly relies on phone-based social engineering rather than email phishing.

Okta linked Inception to O-UNC-053, a cluster previously associated with ShinyHunters, citing continuing domain registrations, consistent infrastructure, and similar tactics before and after Inception’s leak site launched. Inception denied any affiliation with prior groups. ReversingLabs recommends phishing-resistant sign-in methods, stronger help desk identity checks, and review of DNS, proxy, and identity-provider logs dating back to October 21, 2026.

  • The presence of phishing domains impersonating dozens of companies does not establish that those companies were breached or had necessarily even been targeted.
  • Okta reported that the suspected ShinyHunters-linked cluster continued registering phishing domains after law-enforcement disruption and before the Inception leak site appeared, indicating continuity despite the public brand change.
  • The campaign relies on phone-based deception rather than email, making help desk identity-verification procedures a specific defensive focus.
  • ReversingLabs identified activity dating back to October 21, 2026, giving organizations a defined starting point for reviewing DNS, proxy, and identity-provider logs.

A new group called Inception is using phone calls and fake company sign-in pages to trick workers into giving away credentials that unlock workplace accounts. It claims several large companies as victims, but none has confirmed a breach, while researchers found fake sign-in websites impersonating many well-known brands. If successful, this kind of scam can give criminals access to company data and let them pressure organizations for money.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This reports an active, large-scale credential-theft campaign tied to a named extortion group and potentially linked to the ShinyHunters-associated O-UNC-053 cluster. Its phone-based tactics and broad impersonation of corporate authentication services make it relevant to organizations using SSO, although the claimed victim breaches remain unconfirmed.

Colorado says cyberattack took down myColorado app, warns 150,000 had data stolen

Colorado’s myColorado app was taken offline after a cyberattack discovered November 18, with state officials warning that data linked to nearly 150,000 of its 2.1 million users may have been stolen. Residents must use physical IDs and agency websites while the state investigates and restores the service.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Colorado’s Governor’s Office of Information Technology said it discovered a cyberattack involving the myColorado app on the evening of November 18 and shut the app down.
  • 02The state said data connected to nearly 150,000 people may have been stolen, though investigators did not specify what information was involved.
  • 03myColorado has 2.1 million users and provides digital identification, document storage, and access to state government services.
  • 04Colorado advised residents traveling for Thanksgiving to carry physical identification, while state services remain available through agency websites.
  • 05The Gentlemen ransomware group claimed responsibility for the incident on Sunday.

Colorado’s Governor’s Office of Information Technology said it discovered a cyberattack involving the myColorado app on the evening of November 18. The state shut down the app, which remained unavailable as of Tuesday afternoon, and began an investigation. myColorado is used by Colorado residents for digital identification, document storage, and access to state government services. The state said the app has 2.1 million users.

Colorado warned that the incident likely involved the theft of data tied to nearly 150,000 people, but investigators did not disclose what data may have been taken. Residents were advised to bring physical IDs when traveling for Thanksgiving, while state services remained available through individual agency websites. The Gentlemen ransomware group claimed the attack on Sunday. The article also notes that public entities in New York, Mississippi, California, and West Virginia were dealing with other cyber incidents or ransomware-related disruptions.

  • Although myColorado was unavailable, Colorado said residents could still access state services through individual agency websites, providing an alternative route for essential services.
  • The potential data exposure affected nearly 150,000 people, while the app has 2.1 million users, meaning the reported potential exposure was smaller than the app’s total user base.
  • The outage had an immediate practical effect on travel, prompting the state to advise residents to carry physical identification for Thanksgiving trips.
  • The group that claimed the incident, The Gentlemen, also claimed 398 attacks in the prior quarter, according to Halcyon researchers, and the article describes other recent disruptions affecting public institutions in several U.S. states.

Colorado shut down its myColorado app after discovering that criminals had broken into it and may have taken information connected to nearly 150,000 people. The app is used by 2.1 million residents for digital identification, storing documents, and reaching state services, so people may need physical IDs and must use agency websites instead for now. The incident shows how a disruption to a government app can affect everyday needs for large numbers of residents while putting personal information at risk.

California privacy agency director Kemp to leave after two-year tenure

California Privacy Protection Agency Executive Director Tom Kemp will depart after nearly two years, during which the agency launched its consumer data-deletion and opt-out platform used by more than 600,000 Californians.
KEY POINTS
  • 01California Privacy Protection Agency Executive Director Tom Kemp announced that he will leave the agency after nearly two years leading it.
  • 02Kemp highlighted the Delete Request and Opt-Out Platform, which launched in January and is used by more than 600,000 Californians to block data sharing and request deletions from data brokers.
  • 03According to Kemp, each user of the platform has prompted more than 200 data deletions by data brokers on average.
  • 04Penalties for companies that violate consumers’ privacy choices through the platform took effect in August.
  • 05During Kemp’s tenure, the agency issued its largest fine, promoted two privacy-expansion laws, and adopted regulations for cybersecurity audits, risk assessments, and automated decision-making technology.

Tom Kemp, executive director of the California Privacy Protection Agency, announced that he will leave after nearly two years leading the agency. He will start a new position in January but did not disclose the role. Kemp said he is proud of the agency’s progress in making privacy rights easier for Californians to exercise.

During his tenure, the agency launched the Delete Request and Opt-Out Platform, which allows consumers to block data sharing and request deletion of their information from data brokers. More than 600,000 Californians use the platform, and Kemp said each user has prompted more than 200 data deletions on average. The platform debuted in January, while penalties for companies that disregard consumer privacy choices took effect in August.

The agency also supported two laws expanding consumer privacy, opposed federal preemption of California privacy laws, and issued its largest-ever fine. It adopted regulations addressing cybersecurity audits, risk assessments, and automated decision-making technology that uses personal data for significant decisions, including employment-related decisions. These actions expanded the agency’s role in enforcing privacy rules and overseeing how organizations use personal information.

  • With more than 600,000 users each prompting more than 200 data deletions on average, the platform has resulted in more than 120 million deletion requests from data brokers.
  • The platform was available from January, but companies did not face penalties for ignoring consumer privacy choices until August.
  • The agency’s work under Kemp extended beyond data-broker deletion requests to rules requiring cybersecurity audits, risk assessments, and oversight of software that makes significant decisions about people.
  • CalPrivacy’s enforcement activity included its largest-ever fine, requiring Federal Express to change practices and pay close to $3 million over California Consumer Privacy Act violations.

Tom Kemp, the head of California’s privacy agency, is leaving after nearly two years in the role. During his tenure, the agency launched a service that lets Californians ask many companies that trade personal information to stop sharing it or delete it with one request. More than 600,000 people use the service, and each has prompted over 200 data deletions on average. The work matters because it gives residents more control over personal information used by data-selling companies and strengthens California’s rules for companies using automated decision-making systems.
