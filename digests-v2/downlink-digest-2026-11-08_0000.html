The Buyer Who Told Sales to Expect a Password Prompt

A fake construction-firm buyer targeted a sales representative with a link-free email that preconditioned them to enter Microsoft credentials when a purported WeTransfer specification file prompted for a password. The case shows how attackers exploit sales teams’ responsiveness and trust in customer conversations to bypass traditional link and attachment detection.
KEY POINTS
  • 01An attacker posing as a project manager at a real construction firm targeted a sales engineer at a U.S. electronic-connector manufacturer using a fake buyer persona.
  • 02After the sales representative offered calls twice, the attacker declined calls and instructed the representative to sign in to Microsoft and enter a password when prompted to view a purported WeTransfer specification file.
  • 03The phishing email contained no links, attachments, or QR codes; its only inline object was a signature image.
  • 04The sender used the lookalike domain flemingconstructiongruop.com, registered 25 days before the reply, and SPF, DKIM, and DMARC all passed because the attacker controlled the domain.
  • 05A colleague reported the message, a human analyst confirmed it was malicious, and the affected mailbox was quarantined.

An attacker impersonated a project manager at a real construction firm to target a sales engineer at a U.S. electronic-connector manufacturer. After the sales representative initiated contact and twice proposed a call, the fake buyer declined calls and claimed to have sent technical specifications through WeTransfer. The attacker instructed the representative to sign in to Microsoft and enter a password when prompted, attempting to make an unexpected credential request appear legitimate before it occurred.

The email contained no links, attachments, or QR codes, leaving little for URL or file-scanning systems to inspect. The sender used the recently registered lookalike domain flemingconstructiongruop[.]com, where SPF, DKIM, and DMARC passed because the attacker controlled the domain. A colleague reported the message, a human analyst confirmed it was malicious, and the affected mailbox was quarantined; the article highlights sales teams as targets because they are expected to respond quickly to unfamiliar prospective customers.

  • The attacker’s main tactic was to normalize an unexpected password prompt before it appeared, making the instruction itself the credential-theft mechanism rather than a visible malicious link or file.
  • By posing as a buyer rather than a supplier, the attacker targeted sales personnel whose role requires them to respond quickly to unfamiliar contacts and accommodate prospective customers.
  • Passing SPF, DKIM, and DMARC did not establish legitimacy because the attacker controlled the newly registered lookalike domain used to send the email.
  • The decisive detection came from a colleague’s report and human analyst review, illustrating that link-free and attachment-free messages can evade checks designed to inspect those items.

Someone pretending to be a project manager at a real construction company contacted a sales representative at a U.S. connector maker, then told the representative to expect a Microsoft sign-in and password request to view supposed project files. The message contained no harmful link or attachment; it tried to make a later password prompt seem normal so the attacker could steal the representative’s account details. This matters because sales staff are expected to quickly respond to unfamiliar potential customers, and a stolen work account can give criminals a way into an organization.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a concrete, analyst-confirmed credential-harvesting case with a notable link-free tactic that preconditions a sales target to trust a future password prompt. It is useful for email-security and awareness teams, but it does not report broad active exploitation, a major breach, or an urgent product vulnerability.
