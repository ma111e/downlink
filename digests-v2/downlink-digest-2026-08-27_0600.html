Detection Isn’t Proof: The 72-Hour-Evidence Problem

Organizations often can detect security incidents but cannot quickly prove what happened across data systems within GDPR’s 72-hour breach notification window. The key takeaway is that audit-ready evidence must be built continuously through classification, lineage, and policy records before an incident occurs, not assembled after the clock starts.
KEY POINTS
  • 01GDPR requires breach notification within 72 hours, but many organizations struggle to produce comprehensive and auditable evidence before that deadline.
  • 02Research conducted by Cybersecurity Insiders involving more than 1,000 security practitioners found that only 12% of organizations can quickly produce a comprehensive and auditable chain of custody.
  • 03Only 9% of organizations can reliably recognize sensitive data after it has been modified, such as being renamed, reformatted, summarized by an AI tool, or pasted into a new document.
  • 04The article says organizations that meet regulatory deadlines usually build classification, lineage, and policy-event records into daily operations before an incident occurs.
  • 05Netskope One Data Security, including Netskope One DSPM, Netskope One DLP, Netskope One Data Lineage, and Netskope One DataSec Command Center, is described as maintaining classification and lineage across environments where sensitive data is stored, used, copied, and transformed.

The article argues that discovering a potential breach starts a 72-hour GDPR notification clock, but many organizations cannot quickly produce a comprehensive and auditable evidence trail. It cites research conducted by Cybersecurity Insiders, involving more than 1,000 security practitioners at organizations with 2,500 or more employees. According to the article, only 12% of organizations can quickly produce a comprehensive and auditable chain of custody when regulators, auditors, or legal teams ask for it.

The article says the main gap is not simply detecting incidents, but proving what happened before and after an alert across every system the data touched. Almost 50% of organizations require significant manual effort across multiple systems to produce that evidence or struggle to produce it at all, and 27% cannot reconstruct a sensitive data path before the 72-hour window closes. The evidence problem becomes harder when data changes form, because only 9% of organizations can reliably recognize sensitive data after it has been renamed, reformatted, summarized by an AI tool, or pasted into a new document.

The article says organizations that meet regulatory deadlines usually build classification, lineage, and policy-event records into daily operations before an incident occurs. It presents Netskope One Data Security, including Netskope One DSPM, Netskope One DLP, Netskope One Data Lineage, and Netskope One DataSec Command Center, as tools intended to maintain classification and lineage across environments where sensitive data is stored, used, copied, and transformed. The article concludes that the outcome of the 72-hour deadline depends on whether the evidence trail already exists or must be built while the clock is running.

  • The article distinguishes detection maturity from evidence readiness, arguing that organizations can be good at spotting incidents while still being unable to prove what happened in a regulator-ready way.
  • The weakest point in auditability appears to be transformed data, because confidence drops sharply when sensitive content is renamed, reformatted, summarized by AI, or pasted into new locations.
  • Organizations that meet regulatory deadlines are described as having built evidence trails before incidents begin, making evidence a byproduct of routine data governance rather than an emergency deliverable.
  • The article frames AI-generated summaries and rewritten content as a practical breakdown point for conventional audit trails, since sensitive information can move even when the original file remains untouched.

The article says many large organizations can spot possible data breaches, but cannot quickly show exactly where sensitive data came from, who used it, where it went, or how it changed. This matters because GDPR gives organizations 72 hours to report certain breaches, and many teams need heavy manual work or cannot rebuild the full trail in time. The people affected include security teams, legal and compliance teams, regulators, auditors, and people whose personal data may be involved.
