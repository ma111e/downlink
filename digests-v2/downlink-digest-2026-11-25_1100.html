Credential Remediation Is Slow When You Don’t Know What Will Break

Credential remediation stalls when teams cannot determine what will break if an exposed key is revoked, leaving many valid secrets active for years. Reduce risk safely by prioritizing credentials by impact and exposure, then replacing, vaulting, deploying, monitoring, and only finally revoking the old credential.
KEY POINTS
  • 01GitGuardian’s State of Secrets Sprawl 2026 found that 64% of valid secrets discovered in 2022 were still working in January 2026.
  • 02Credential remediation is delayed because the teams finding exposed credentials often cannot determine which services will fail if a credential is revoked.
  • 03GitGuardian research says prioritizing solely by automatic validity checks misses 46% of critical secrets, including custom API tokens and SSH keys that cannot be verified automatically.
  • 04The article recommends issuing a replacement credential, storing it in a vault, updating every known consumer, deploying and monitoring the changes, and only then revoking the original credential.
  • 05A remediation ticket should not be closed until responders have verifiable confirmation that the old credential no longer authenticates and an audit trail of the work performed.

Credential remediation is often slow because teams do not know which applications or services will fail if an exposed credential is revoked. GitGuardian’s State of Secrets Sprawl 2026 found that 64% of valid secrets discovered in 2022 were still working in January 2026. Security, development, identity, platform, and endpoint teams may each hold only part of the information needed to replace a credential safely, which can leave remediation requests stuck in backlogs.

The article says teams should prioritize leaked credentials by their potential impact and exposure, rather than relying on validity checks alone, which GitGuardian says miss 46% of critical secrets. Safe remediation starts by issuing a replacement, storing it in a vault, changing every known system to use the vaulted replacement, deploying and monitoring the change, and only then revoking the old credential. Tickets should remain open until responders can verify that the old credential no longer works and preserve an audit trail of what was changed.

  • A credential’s risk score should be updated as new information arrives, rather than treated as a fixed assessment made when the exposure is first discovered.
  • Closing an incident while the old credential remains valid can indicate that one team rotated only its own copy without accounting for other active uses.
  • Routing remediation through existing engineering ticketing tools can keep the work in normal development workflows while allowing security responders to track progress centrally.
  • Repeated exposure of vaulted credentials in unmanaged local files can reveal a broader prevention problem, not just isolated remediation cases.

The article explains why companies often leave leaked passwords and access keys active for a long time: security staff may not know which apps or services will stop working if they disable them. GitGuardian reports that 64% of working secrets it found in 2022 still worked in January 2026, showing how long this problem can last. Businesses, developers, and security teams are affected because a stolen key can let outsiders reach important systems, while disabling it too early can interrupt customer-facing services. The safer approach is to create and test a replacement first, move every system to use it, and then disable the exposed key.

Hackers Exploit Two GeoServer Flaws as Researchers Warn of Unauthenticated RCE Chains

Attackers are actively probing two newly patched GeoServer flaws, including an unauthenticated file-read/SSRF issue and an authorization bypass. Organizations should urgently upgrade to GeoServer 2.27.7, 2.28.6, or 3.0.2 because these bugs can be chained with other flaws for unauthenticated remote code execution.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Threat actors are actively exploiting CVE-2026-120705 and CVE-2026-129243, two recently patched GeoServer vulnerabilities.
  • 02CVE-2026-120705 allows unauthenticated attackers to read local files accessible to the GeoServer process or retrieve attacker-selected HTTP or HTTPS URLs through a Web Feature Service request.
  • 03CVE-2026-129243 can allow unauthenticated attackers to invoke OWS operations that should be protected by GeoServer service-security rules.
  • 04Researchers described unauthenticated remote-code-execution chains that combine the file-read issue with predictable administrator-session signing keys and JDBC configuration or deserialization flaws.
  • 05Users are advised to update to GeoServer versions 2.27.7, 2.28.6, or 3.0.2 as soon as possible.

Threat actors are actively exploiting two newly patched GeoServer flaws: CVE-2026-120705, an unauthenticated file-read and attacker-controlled URL-fetch issue, and CVE-2026-129243, an authorization bypass affecting protected OWS operations. WatchTowr said attackers used error-based probes involving stored queries to check whether servers were vulnerable. GeoServer advisories were published on November 24, 2026, while patches had been released in October and early November.

Researchers described ways to combine these and related flaws into unauthenticated remote code execution. One chain uses file disclosure to obtain an administrator password representation and the predictable “remember me” signing key, then forges an administrator cookie and abuses a JDBC configuration to run code from an uploaded native library. A separate five-step chain includes a stored-query path disclosure, a service-rule bypass using a prefixed WFS name, XML injection, administrator-session forgery, and JDBC deserialization. Users are advised to update promptly to GeoServer versions 2.27.7, 2.28.6, or 3.0.2.

  • The disclosed attack chain does not require cracking an administrator password: an attacker can use a leaked password representation and GeoServer's predictable “remember me” signing key to forge an administrator login cookie.
  • Changing the signing key in GeoServer's security configuration alone may not stop this chain if the same file-disclosure weakness also exposes the replacement key.
  • A prefixed service name such as “x/WFS” can reportedly bypass the administrator-only restriction on stored queries, enabling access to files containing the signing key and administrator password digest.
  • Active exploit probes have used stored queries to trigger errors and determine whether a GeoServer instance is vulnerable.

Hackers are actively testing weaknesses in GeoServer, software organizations use to provide map and location-related data. The flaws can let an outsider read sensitive files or bypass restrictions without logging in, and researchers showed that several of the weaknesses together could let someone take over the server. Organizations using GeoServer should install the latest updates quickly, because a successful break-in could expose stored information and allow attackers to run commands on affected systems.

Drupal Fail2Install Exploit Details Emerge as Critical RCE Flaw Faces Active Exploitation

CISA has added the actively exploited Drupal core flaw CVE-2026-132165 (Fail2Install) to its KEV catalog after researchers showed that resource exhaustion can reopen the installer on existing sites and enable unauthenticated remote code execution. Organizations should update Drupal to the fixed releases immediately.
KEY POINTS
  • 01CISA added Drupal core vulnerability CVE-2026-132165, also called Fail2Install, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and required U.S. federal agencies to apply fixes by November 27, 2026.
  • 02Fail2Install is a critical unauthenticated remote code execution flaw with a CVSS score of 9.8 that can allow an attacker to take over an existing Drupal site or run arbitrary code through the Drupal installer.
  • 03Searchlight Cyber found that exhausting file handles, database connections, or TCP source ports can cause Drupal to incorrectly conclude that an existing site has not been installed and reopen its installation process.
  • 04The research describes using Drupal's SQLite installer option to create a database file with a .php extension and write PHP code into it, potentially leading to arbitrary PHP execution through an Apache path-handling weakness.
  • 05Drupal fixed the issue in versions 11.4.13, 11.3.22, and 10.6.22, and the Drupal Security Team said the flaw had been exploited on live sites as early as November 19, 2026.

CISA added CVE-2026-132165, also known as Fail2Install, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog after evidence that it was exploited against live Drupal sites. The critical Drupal core flaw has a CVSS score of 9.8 and can enable an unauthenticated attacker to take over an existing site or execute arbitrary code through the Drupal installer. U.S. federal agencies were directed to apply fixes by November 27, 2026.

Searchlight Cyber found that attackers can exhaust system resources, including file handles, database connections, or TCP source ports, to make Drupal incorrectly believe an existing site has not been installed. A race condition can then allow the installer to proceed, where an attacker can create an administrator-controlled account and use an SQLite database path ending in .php to write PHP code into a file. Drupal addressed the issue in versions 11.4.13, 11.3.22, and 10.6.22.

  • The flaw turns ordinary service-capacity failures, such as too many open files or database connections, into a way to bypass Drupal’s normal safeguard that prevents reinstallation of an existing website.
  • The exploit path relies on a timing condition in which Drupal can see two failed checks that suggest no installation exists, followed by a successful database check that lets installation continue.
  • Drupal’s installer permits an attacker to choose an SQLite database path ending in .php under the web-accessible directory, allowing submitted site-name content to be written into that PHP-named file.
  • Searchlight Cyber reported the issue across four common setups combining Apache or PHP-FPM with either PostgreSQL or MySQL, indicating that the behavior is not limited to one database choice or web-serving configuration.

A serious flaw in Drupal, software used to run websites, is being actively used against real sites. Attackers can overwhelm parts of a server so Drupal mistakenly treats an existing website as new, then use its setup page to take control of the site or run their own code. The U.S. cybersecurity agency CISA has flagged the issue as urgent, and Drupal users should install the available updates because a compromised site could be fully controlled by an outsider.

  • 02CyCognito reports that its AI agents found significant issues after customers’ regular manual pentests, including an attack path that enabled access to every customer’s files at a Fortune 500 services company.
  • 03The article argues that most AI pentesting is still limited to a short, preselected list of assets, leaving unknown or overlooked parts of an organization’s internet-facing systems untested.
  • 04AI pentesting can be expensive when agents must first learn each asset from scratch, with cited per-application prices typically ranging from $5,000 to $15,000.
  • 05The article states that compliance requirements for qualified, independent human professionals may prevent AI-only pentests from replacing manual pentesting immediately.

AI pentesting agents can now match or exceed experienced human pentesters in some real-world testing tasks, according to the article. It cites a Stanford and Carnegie Mellon study in which one agent outperformed nine of 10 professional testers on a network of about 8,000 hosts, finding nine valid vulnerabilities. CyCognito also says its agents found serious issues that customers’ regular manual pentests had missed. The article argues that AI can reason through multi-step paths, connect weaknesses across applications, and continue testing without the time limits of a traditional engagement.

The article’s main concern is coverage: most AI pentesting is still aimed at a short list of assets that organizations already know about. CyCognito promotes its approach of mapping the external attack surface, running continuous deterministic testing, and using its Target Graph to prioritize assets and attack paths for deeper AI testing.   • The article’s central limitation is not whether AI can find and combine weaknesses, but that organizations still commonly decide in advance which systems receive deep testing.
  • The examples show that serious risks can emerge when small issues in separate applications are combined, even when no individual team sees the complete path.
  • Using continuously collected information about internet-facing systems before deep testing can let AI agents focus their effort on trying to prove real access rather than first identifying what each system is.
  • Human-led testing may remain necessary in the near term for compliance reasons even where AI testing can technically perform comparable work.

The article says AI tools can now test company systems for weaknesses as well as, and sometimes better than, experienced human security testers. In examples from large companies, these tools found ways an outsider could access customer files, pretend to be a customer, or enter systems used for live operations. This matters because companies often test only the systems they already know about, while overlooked internet-facing systems may provide an easier route for criminals.
