Over 16,000 Supabase databases expose PII, passwords, auth tokens

UpGuard found more than 16,000 misconfigured Supabase databases exposing readable data, including PII, passwords, and authentication tokens, primarily because of weak or missing row-level security and misuse of public keys. The exposures affected organizations worldwide and may be worsened by AI-assisted app development without adequate security review.
KEY POINTS
  • 01UpGuard researchers found more than 16,000 misconfigured Supabase databases with publicly readable tables exposing personally identifiable information, passwords, or authentication tokens.
  • 02The researchers analyzed roughly 300,000 domains that appeared to use Supabase and used accessible table schemas to infer the kinds of data exposed.
  • 03More than half of the exposed databases contained personally identifiable information, while smaller subsets included passwords and authentication tokens.
  • 04Examples included a U.S. valet service exposing over 100,000 customer records and a Canadian immigration service exposing nearly 5,000 user records, including 884 plaintext passwords.
  • 05UpGuard attributed the exposures to poor application security settings, including missing or ineffective row-level security policies and misuse of public keys.

UpGuard researchers found more than 16,000 misconfigured Supabase databases with readable tables exposing personally identifiable information, passwords, or authentication tokens. The researchers examined around 300,000 domains showing signs of Supabase use and checked for accessible user-related tables and schemas. More than half of the exposed databases contained personally identifiable information, while a smaller subset included passwords and authentication tokens.

The reported cases included a U.S. valet service with more than 100,000 exposed customer records, a Canadian immigration service with nearly 5,000 user records and 884 plaintext passwords, and an India-based adult creator platform with identity data, payment accounts, and over 100,000 private messages. UpGuard also cited an OTP service in the Philippines exposing user and SMS data and an African government consulate exposing records for 25,000 people, including emergency housing locations. The researchers attributed the exposures to poor application security configuration, including missing or ineffective row-level security policies and misuse of public keys, and encouraged Supabase users to review the platform’s security documentation.

  • The exposure was not confined to one industry: the article cites affected services ranging from valet parking and immigration assistance to an adult creator platform, an OTP service, and a government consulate.
  • UpGuard’s review found that even when a database did not expose a table named “users,” another accessible table could still reveal data through its schema.
  • Although AI-assisted development accounts for more than 60% of newly created Supabase databases, UpGuard said its scans did not prove that every exposed site was created with an AI coding agent.
  • The reported configuration problems involved access controls rather than a Supabase platform flaw, including ineffective row-level restrictions and inappropriate use of public keys.

Researchers found more than 16,000 online databases built with Supabase that were set up so strangers could read private information. The exposed records included names and other personal details, passwords, login access codes, messages, payment-related information, and data from organizations in the United States, Canada, India, the Philippines, and Africa. This matters because people could have their privacy, accounts, and potentially their money put at risk when organizations do not properly restrict who can view their data.

This is a broad, concrete finding affecting more than 16,000 publicly exposed Supabase databases and involving sensitive data such as PII, passwords, and authentication tokens. It is particularly relevant to organizations using Supabase because the reported cause is insecure configuration rather than an isolated incident.

Times Car confirms data breach affecting 6.6 million user accounts

Japanese car-sharing provider Times Car confirmed that a cyberattack exposed personal data from roughly 6.6 million current and former members, including driver’s license and identity-verification documents. Credit card data was not affected, and the company says passwords were stored in a non-reversible form.

  • 01Times Car confirmed that unauthorized access to its systems resulted in the theft of data affecting approximately 6.6 million current and former members, including members of its Times Business Service corporate program.
  • 02The exposed information includes names, addresses, birth dates, telephone numbers, email addresses, driver’s license information, identity-verification document images, account passwords, and linked service IDs.
  • 03Times Car said the affected passwords were stored in a form that cannot be restored, while credit card information was not affected.
  • 04The company said it has no evidence that the stolen data has been distributed online and is conducting a forensic investigation with an external expert.
  • 05Times Car will notify affected customers in stages and has warned members to be cautious of emails, SMS messages, and phone calls claiming to come from the company.

Japanese car-sharing service Times Car confirmed that a cyberattack exposed data tied to approximately 6.6 million current and former members, including participants in its Times Business Service corporate account program. The company said a third party accessed its systems at the beginning of September and that it blocked the unauthorized access on September 26. It initially investigated whether personal information had been accessed before confirming the data theft in a subsequent update.

Exposed information includes names, addresses, birth dates, phone numbers, email addresses, driver’s license details, identity-verification document images, account passwords, and linked service IDs. Times Car said passwords were stored in a form that cannot be restored, while credit card information was not affected, and it has no evidence that the stolen data has been distributed online. The company is conducting a forensic investigation with an external expert, will notify affected customers in stages, and has warned members to be wary of purported Times Car emails, text messages, and phone calls.

  • The incident extends beyond active consumer users to former members and current and former participants in the Times Business Service corporate program, broadening the population that may need to watch for impersonation attempts.
  • The combination of contact details, driver’s license information, and identity-document images makes Times Car’s warning about fraudulent emails, text messages, and phone calls particularly relevant.
  • Credit card information was not affected, but the exposed data categories include account passwords and linked service IDs, so the company emphasized avoiding requests to enter passwords or card details.
  • Times Car said it has no evidence that the stolen data has been distributed online, while also stating that customer notifications will be delivered in stages.

Japanese car-sharing service Times Car says someone gained unauthorized access to its systems and took information connected to about 6.6 million current and former customers, including people in its business account program. The exposed details may include names, addresses, birthdays, phone numbers, email addresses, driver’s license details and images of identification documents. Credit card information was not affected, but customers could face convincing fake emails, texts, or phone calls using their personal details, so the company has warned them to be cautious.

Hackers Use NeedyMantis to Maintain Long-Term Access in Breached Networks

Microsoft says the NeedyMantis malware family is being used in targeted post-compromise intrusions to maintain long-term access through DLL sideloading and encrypted payloads. The activity, including use by the suspected China-linked Storm-3069 group, has targeted organizations across telecommunications, academia, healthcare, intergovernmental bodies, and government contracting.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Microsoft identified NeedyMantis as a malware family used to maintain long-term access in a small number of targeted intrusions affecting telecommunications organizations, universities, medical nonprofits, intergovernmental organizations, and government contractors.
  • 02NeedyMantis commonly uses DLL sideloading, packaging a legitimate application with a malicious DLL and encrypted archive so that the application loads and runs the malware.
  • 03Microsoft tracks one user of NeedyMantis as Storm-3069, assesses its activity appears to originate in China, and has not attributed it to a Chinese nation-state actor.
  • 04Microsoft has not confirmed that NeedyMantis was distributed through the compromised DAEMON Tools Lite installers, although it found the malware while investigating indicators related to that supply-chain incident.
  • 05Microsoft published file hashes, a command-and-control domain, file paths, and hunting queries to help defenders identify NeedyMantis activity.

Microsoft reported that the NeedyMantis malware family has been used to maintain long-term access in a small number of targeted intrusions affecting telecommunications organizations, universities, medical nonprofits, intergovernmental organizations, and government contractors. The activity dates to at least October 2025. Microsoft found NeedyMantis while following indicators from Kaspersky’s investigation of the DAEMON Tools supply-chain compromise, though it has not seen NeedyMantis delivered through the tampered installers.

NeedyMantis is deployed alongside legitimate software such as Poedit, curl, Vim, or TightVNC, using a malicious file that the legitimate program loads. The malicious component extracts encrypted stages, contacts a command-and-control server over HTTPS, switches to a WebSocket connection, and can receive additional modules from operators. Microsoft tracks one user of the malware as Storm-3069, assesses that its activity appears to originate in China, and says more than one group may use NeedyMantis. Microsoft published hashes, file paths, a command-and-control domain, and hunting queries, while noting that the queries cover only the previous seven days and that a normal Poedit WinSparkle.dll file must be checked against the published hash.

  • Microsoft’s published hunting queries look back only seven days, even though the related NeedyMantis files were first seen in October 2025 and May 2026, so unchanged queries would not identify activity from those earlier periods.
  • A WinSparkle.dll file in Poedit’s normal installation path is not sufficient evidence of infection because WinSparkle.dll is a legitimate Poedit component; the file must be compared with Microsoft’s published hash.
  • Microsoft found NeedyMantis while investigating indicators tied to the DAEMON Tools supply-chain compromise, but it has not observed the malware being delivered through the tampered DAEMON Tools installers.
  • Microsoft has observed NeedyMantis outside Storm-3069 activity and says multiple groups may use it, so the malware alone does not establish that a single actor was responsible.

Microsoft says hackers have used a program called NeedyMantis to keep access to organizations after breaking into their computer networks. The activity has affected a small number of telecommunications companies, universities, medical nonprofits, intergovernmental organizations, and government contractors, and may be linked to groups operating from China. This matters because the program can hide behind legitimate software and let attackers remain in a network for a long time, potentially giving them continued access to information and systems.

IAM for AI agents: A Practical Enterprise Framework

Enterprise IAM for AI agents must go beyond provisioning and static roles by assigning distinct, owned, short-lived identities and enforcing task-scoped permissions. Organizations also need runtime observability across applications and infrastructure to verify what agents actually did and rapidly revoke authority when behavior exceeds intent.
KEY POINTS
  • 01AI agents should be treated as non-human identities with a human owner, defined purpose, scoped authorization, expiration, and continuous monitoring.
  • 02Traditional IAM systems show configured access and authentication events but generally do not show what an autonomous agent did inside an application.
  • 03Agent identities should use distinct identities and favor federated workload identity with short-lived, automatically rotated credentials instead of embedded secrets.
  • 04Task-scoped grants, tool allowlists, data boundaries, and approval requirements for high-consequence actions can constrain agent behavior.
  • 05Organizations need application- and infrastructure-level telemetry to compare an agent’s intended task with its actual execution and support rapid revocation when they diverge.

AI agents act across enterprise systems with delegated authority, so the article argues they should be managed as non-human identities with a named human owner, defined purpose, limited authorization, expiration, and continuous monitoring. Traditional identity and access management systems primarily show configured access and sign-in events, rather than what an autonomous agent actually did after entering an application. This creates an intent-to-execution gap, including identities, credentials, application-local accounts, and authentication paths that central identity systems may not report.

The article recommends distinct agent identities, federated workload identity, and short-lived automatically rotated credentials rather than shared accounts, borrowed user credentials, or embedded secrets. Agent authority should be constrained through task-scoped grants, tool allowlists, data boundaries, and human approval or a second authorization path for high-consequence actions. Organizations also need application- and infrastructure-level telemetry to compare an agent’s intended task with its actual behavior, generate audit evidence, and revoke delegated authority when behavior diverges from approved scope.

  • An agent can appear well governed in an identity provider because its sign-ins look normal, while application-level records reveal sensitive data queries, exports, or entitlement changes that the identity provider never records.
  • The article distinguishes three separate things that must be evaluated: an agent’s approved intent, the permissions it was granted, and the actions it actually executed; only execution shows what occurred.
  • Agent identities often escape standard governance because they can be created by deployment pipelines, infrastructure automation, or other workloads rather than human-resources-driven processes.
  • For most enterprises, the article recommends extending existing identity governance for ownership and approvals, building in-application controls where needed, and adding observability for discovering identities and verifying behavior.
  • Revocation speed and the quality of evidence are presented as critical evaluation criteria that are often overlooked when organizations focus mainly on provisioning features and connector counts.

The article explains how companies should control AI agents, which are software helpers that can sign in to business systems, use tools, and act for people. These agents can access customer records, supplier information, or systems that manage company infrastructure, so broad or long-lasting access can let them do more than they were meant to do. Businesses need to give every agent a clear human owner, limited and temporary access, and records showing the actions it actually took. This matters because normal sign-in records may not reveal an agent’s real actions after it enters a company system.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is an evergreen best-practice guide on governing AI-agent identities, permissions, and runtime monitoring rather than reporting a specific incident, vulnerability, or active threat. It is useful background for teams deploying enterprise AI agents but is not time-sensitive.

KEY POINTS
  • 01Apple released security updates for older iOS, iPadOS, and macOS versions to address CVE-2026-86950 in CoreGraphics.
  • 02CVE-2026-86950 is an out-of-bounds write that could enable arbitrary code execution when a device processes a maliciously crafted file.
  • 03Apple said the flaw may have been exploited in an extremely sophisticated attack against specific targeted individuals on iOS versions before iOS 27.
  • 04The issue was fixed through improved bounds checking and was discovered and reported by Meta Product Security.
  • 05Apple addressed the flaw in iOS and iPadOS 26.7.1, macOS Tahoe 26.7.1, and macOS Sequoia 15.8.1.

Apple released security updates for older versions of iOS, iPadOS, and macOS to fix CVE-2026-86950, a CoreGraphics flaw. The issue is an out-of-bounds write that could allow arbitrary code execution when a device processes a maliciously crafted file. Apple said it fixed the problem through improved bounds checking and credited Meta Product Security with reporting it.

Apple is aware of a report that the issue may have been used in an extremely sophisticated attack against specifically targeted individuals using iOS versions before iOS 27. The company did not disclose how many people were targeted, whether any exploitation attempts succeeded, or when exploitation was first observed. Fixes are available in iOS and iPadOS 26.7.1, macOS Tahoe 26.7.1, and macOS Sequoia 15.8.1 for the listed supported devices and systems.

  • Apple disclosed only that the suspected real-world use involved iOS versions before iOS 27, even though it issued fixes for iOS, iPadOS, and two macOS release lines.
  • The company did not disclose how many people were targeted, whether any attempts succeeded, or when exploitation first occurred, leaving the known scope of the activity unclear.
  • The flaw can be triggered by processing a specially crafted file, making timely installation of the listed operating-system updates the direct mitigation described in the article.
  • Meta Product Security, rather than Apple, discovered and reported the CoreGraphics issue.

Apple released updates for older iPhones, iPads, and Macs to fix a flaw that could let someone take control of a device by getting it to open a specially made file. Apple says the problem may have been used in highly sophisticated attacks against a small number of specifically chosen people, though it did not say who or how many were affected. Updating affected devices helps protect users from this risk.
