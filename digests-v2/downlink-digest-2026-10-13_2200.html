OpenAI engineer arrested for allegedly planting spy device in office

A former OpenAI engineer was arrested after allegedly hiding encrypted audio-recording devices in executive conference rooms at the company’s San Francisco offices to capture confidential discussions. OpenAI said it fired the employee, referred the case to the FBI, and found no evidence that model weights, source code, or customer data were accessed or exfiltrated.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The U.S. Department of Justice announced that Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old Saudi national who had worked at OpenAI since July 2025, was arrested on October 9 and charged with illegally intercepting oral communications.
  • 02OpenAI security personnel found a concealed recording device plugged into a USB port behind a television in an executive conference room on September 30.
  • 03The DOJ alleges that the device was installed around September 28, held encrypted data, and was concealed in toilet paper.
  • 04OpenAI said it terminated Alfozan and referred the incident to the FBI after using its AI models to help identify the employee allegedly responsible.
  • 05OpenAI stated that it had no indication that model weights, source code, or customer data had been accessed or removed.

The U.S. Department of Justice said Abdulrahman Alfozan, a 35-year-old Saudi national who had worked at OpenAI since July 2025, was arrested on October 9 and charged with illegally intercepting oral communications. Prosecutors allege that he placed a hidden audio-recording device in an executive conference room at OpenAI’s San Francisco offices. The room was regularly used for confidential discussions involving proprietary research, product plans, technical capabilities, business relationships, and government engagements.

OpenAI security personnel found the first device on September 30 in a USB port behind a television, allegedly concealed in toilet paper and containing encrypted data. The complaint alleges that surveillance footage showed Alfozan near the room and that searches and chatbot prompts associated with him or his spouse involved office details and methods for building, concealing, connecting, and encrypting recorders. OpenAI said it used its own AI models to investigate the device, fired the employee, referred the matter to the FBI, and had no indication that model weights, source code, or customer data were accessed or removed.

  • The alleged surveillance activity targeted an executive conference room used for discussions of proprietary research, product plans, business relationships, and government engagements, showing why meeting-room physical security can protect highly sensitive information.
  • The complaint links the alleged device activity to searches for office layouts and meeting schedules, as well as chatbot prompts about building, concealing, connecting, and encrypting a recorder.
  • OpenAI said it used its own AI models to examine the device and determine its origin, then identified the employee it says had placed it.
  • Although the incident involved alleged secret recording, OpenAI said it had found no indication that model weights, source code, or customer data were accessed or removed.

A former OpenAI engineer was arrested after allegedly hiding small audio-recording devices in executive meeting rooms at the company’s San Francisco office. Prosecutors say the devices were meant to secretly capture discussions about OpenAI’s research, products, business relationships, and government work. OpenAI fired the employee and contacted the FBI, and said it has not found signs that its software, model details, or customer information were taken. The case shows that sensitive company information can be at risk from someone with physical access to an office, not only from outside hackers.

The Noise Inside the Network: How an Unsophisticated Attack Exposed a Third-Party Risk

A European bank was compromised through a misconfigured third-party gateway and stolen credentials, enabling months of noisy network reconnaissance and password guessing. Cortex XDR stopped an attempted WSUS-based escalation, underscoring that MFA, prompt patching, and strong third-party access controls can prevent major incidents.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A European bank detected unauthorized VPN-based access attempts that had occurred over several months and engaged Unit 42 to investigate the intrusion.
  • 02The intrusion began through a third-party-managed external gateway appliance that was not configured to enforce strict authentication, using compromised credentials.
  • 03After entering the network, the attacker conducted broad scans, tested passwords against administrator accounts, and accessed internal documentation and data shares.
  • 04The attacker attempted to exploit a Windows Server Update Services remote-code-execution vulnerability on the bank’s central configuration management server.
  • 05Cortex XDR detected and contained the unauthorized code-execution attempt, and Unit 42’s subsequent forensic review found no additional attacker presence or persistent backdoors.

A European bank detected unauthorized VPN-based access attempts that had taken place over several months and brought in Unit 42 to assess the intrusion. The attacker entered through a third-party-managed external gateway appliance that did not enforce strict authentication, using compromised credentials. Once inside, the intruder performed broad internal host and network scanning across many protocols and systems.

The attacker also ran password-guessing campaigns against administrator accounts, browsed internal documentation, and enumerated operational and network shares. They later attempted to exploit a Windows Server Update Services remote-code-execution vulnerability on the bank’s central software-distribution server, but Cortex XDR detected and contained the activity before it could escalate. Unit 42 reviewed endpoint and server telemetry and found no additional attacker presence or persistent backdoors, while the bank rotated compromised credentials and reviewed exposed documentation.

The incident demonstrated that an opportunistic, noisy attacker can still create serious risk by exploiting basic weaknesses such as missing multifactor authentication and delayed patching. Unit 42 emphasized strict identity controls, timely patching, endpoint detection, rapid containment, and thorough threat hunting as key defenses. Following the incident, the bank expanded its Cortex XSIAM and Cortex XDR coverage and broadened its incident-response retainer service.

  • The attacker’s activity was highly visible rather than stealthy, yet it continued over months, showing that persistent but unsophisticated behavior can still create significant risk when basic controls are weak.
  • A third-party-managed gateway became the entry point because strict authentication was not enforced, highlighting that external providers can expose an organization’s internal environment to avoidable access risks.
  • Stopping the attempted code execution on the central software-distribution server prevented the attacker from turning initial access into broader control and long-term presence across the bank’s network.
  • The bank’s combination of quick containment, credential rotation, exposed-document review, and broad forensic hunting enabled it to confirm that no persistent backdoors remained.

A European bank discovered that someone had used stolen login details to enter its systems through an outside company’s access gateway, then spent months searching its internal network and trying passwords. The intruder also tried to take control of the server that distributes software updates, which could have given them broader, lasting access. Security software stopped that attempt, and investigators found no remaining hidden access, but the case shows that simple gaps such as weak login protections or delayed updates can put an organization and its information at serious risk.

This is a concrete incident-response case involving months of unauthorized access at a European bank and an attempted WSUS-based escalation. It offers useful lessons on third-party access, credential protection, detection, and containment, but provides no named threat actor, technical indicators, or broadly actionable vulnerability details.
