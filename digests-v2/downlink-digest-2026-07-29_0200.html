Coordinated “cyberattack” on Minnesota water utilities: What you need to know

Defenders should urgently remove PLCs from direct internet exposure, segment OT networks, monitor CISA IOCs, and harden Rockwell, Schneider Electric, and Siemens control environments.

A coordinated cyberattack disrupted water and wastewater utility operations across more than 30 Minnesota communities between July 26 and July 27, 2026. Four cities publicly disclosed attacks: Braham, Plymouth, South St. Paul, and Maple Plain. Officials said drinking water quality was not affected and no boil-water advisories were issued, while the Minnesota Department of Health worked directly with targeted water systems to protect public health.

Federal and state officials have not publicly attributed the Minnesota attacks, but the article says the operational pattern is consistent with the CyberAv3ngers threat ecosystem, which the U.S. government has attributed to Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Cyber-Electronic Command. The timing closely followed CISA’s July 22, 2026 update to Advisory AA26-097A, which warned that Iranian-affiliated actors had been compromising internet-connected PLCs across U.S. water, energy, and government sectors. The article notes that CyberAv3ngers has evolved from propaganda claims to default-password PLC compromises, custom IoT and OT malware, and exploitation of internet-facing industrial controllers.

The July 22 CISA update expanded observed targeting from Rockwell Automation devices to include Schneider Electric and Siemens PLCs, documented PLC project file exfiltration, and added detection guidance for manipulation of reusable code modules embedded in PLC programs. The article highlights CVE-2021-22681, a critical authentication bypass in Rockwell Automation Logix controllers that has no available vendor patch, making architectural controls the only remediation path. Recommended actions include disconnecting PLCs from direct internet exposure, setting physical mode switches to Run, ingesting CISA indicators, segmenting IT and OT networks, auditing cellular OT connections, and backing up PLC logic and configurations offline.

  • The article emphasizes that CVE-2021-22681 cannot be resolved through normal patch management because Rockwell Automation says there is no software patch, making architectural controls such as segmentation, engineering workstation isolation, CIP Security, and physical mode switch hardening the only remediation path.
  • The Plymouth incident highlights cellular-connected water infrastructure as a practical attack path, since the city disconnected cellular-connected equipment at water towers and wastewater lift stations to stop the attack and prevent retargeting.
  • CISA’s updated advisory describes attackers using the same vendor engineering tools that legitimate operators use, including Studio 5000 Logix Designer, EcoStruxure Control Expert, and TIA Portal, to pull PLC project files from victim environments.
  • The advisory’s discussion of malicious Add-On Instructions shows that attackers can preserve normal-looking downstream logic while changing reusable code modules that affect safety shutdowns and alarms, making malicious changes harder for operators to notice.
  • The article notes that a large share of internet-exposed Rockwell Automation or Allen-Bradley devices identified by Censys were in the United States and many were on cellular carrier networks, matching the kind of field-deployed infrastructure described in the Minnesota incidents.
