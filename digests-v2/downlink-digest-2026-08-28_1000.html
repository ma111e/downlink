The Redirect Was Real HubSpot. The Hiding Was Broken.

A phishing email passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC by using hijacked dormant domains and Amazon SES, then sent victims through HubSpot’s legitimate sales-engagement redirect service instead of a lookalike domain. The key takeaway is that trusted senders and reputable first-hop links are not enough to prove an email is safe; defenders need behavioral and structural detection beyond authentication and basic URL reputation.
KEY POINTS

  • 02The message passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, relayed through Amazon SES from a dormant Australian small-business domain whose DNS had been repurposed.

A controls engineer at a mid-size industrial automation company received a document-share notification claiming that the recipient’s own employer had shared a contract with them. The message passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, relayed through Amazon SES from a dormant Australian small-business domain whose DNS had been repurposed. The lure gave the recipient twenty-four hours before the link expired and presented a single button labeled “View document here.”

The button did not point to a lookalike domain or compromised website, but to a per-account tracking subdomain on hs-sales-engage[.]com, HubSpot’s genuine sales-engagement redirect service. The article says the link scan returned clean with no screenshot captured because it only reflected the reputable first hop, not whatever may have sat behind the redirect. Themis scored the message at 90% confidence and labeled it credential theft based on content, community resolutions of similar incidents, and sender reputation compared against known phishing behavior.

The message also contained a broken hiding attempt: a .hidden-block { display: none; } rule appeared as plain body text instead of inside a stylesheet, so unrelated third-party correspondence rendered visibly after dozens of blank lines. The article highlights that authentication checks worked as designed but did not address the sending-domain hijack or the redirector-fronted link. One mailbox was affected, and the message was mitigated roughly five seconds after receipt with no user action required.

  • Clean SPF, DKIM, and DMARC results did not indicate that the message was safe, because the attacker used a legitimate relay and a repurposed domain while the authentication checks only validated the sending domain.
  • A reputable first-hop URL can hide risk rather than remove it, because the HubSpot tracking link itself served no malicious content and the article says the abuse likely existed one redirect further downstream.
  • The broken CSS hiding attempt created a detection opportunity, since the orphaned style rule, dozens of blank lines, and unrelated foreign-language correspondence were structurally anomalous before any link needed to be resolved.
  • The mismatch between the promised contract filename and the actual attached PDF was a behavioral signal that the lure did not correspond to a real shared document.
  • The article emphasizes that sender behavior, message structure, and context flagged the email where authentication and basic link reputation did not.

A controls engineer received a fake document-sharing email that claimed to come from their own employer and invited them to click a “View document here” button. The email looked trustworthy to normal checks because it was sent through real services and used a real HubSpot link-tracking system, while the unrelated small businesses whose domains were used were also bystanders. It matters because scams like this can steal login details even when the email appears legitimate, and the article says those stolen credentials are involved in a large share of real-world breaches.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a specific, concrete phishing case showing abuse of legitimate Amazon SES and HubSpot redirect infrastructure while passing SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. It is broadly relevant to security professionals because it highlights how trusted first-hop links and clean authentication can fail to indicate safety, though the reported impact was limited to one mailbox and no losses were described.
