The Future of Age Verification: Your Face Never Leaves Your Device

Incode is promoting on-device age estimation that verifies a user’s age locally on their phone, tablet, or laptop so facial images are not transmitted or stored. The company argues this privacy-by-architecture approach helps platforms meet expanding age-verification laws while reducing biometric data exposure and fraud risk.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Incode says more than 30 age assurance laws are now in force worldwide, including the UK Online Safety Act, Australia’s under-16 rules, Brazil’s Digital ECA, and age verification mandates in about half of U.S. states.
  • 02Incode’s On-Device Age Estimation runs facial age estimation and passive liveness detection on the user’s phone, tablet, or laptop so the face is not transmitted or stored.
  • 03The system sends only the age-threshold outcome onward, while a server-side layer analyzes session metadata to detect injection attacks, tampering, and other session integrity issues without facial or biometric information.
  • 04Incode says it compressed its facial age estimation and passive liveness models to roughly one-tenth of their original size using knowledge distillation so they can run in ordinary browsers or apps without special hardware.
  • 05Incode announced a $100 million commitment to privacy-preserving identity infrastructure alongside its acquisition of Identiq, whose technology is intended to let organizations share fraud signals without exposing customer data to a third party.

More than 30 age assurance laws are now in force worldwide, and the article says facial age estimation has become one of the most accessible ways for platforms to comply because it does not require a government ID or database lookup. Incode says users in regulated markets choose facial age estimation eight out of ten times over other age assurance methods. The article argues that the main concern is what happens to the face data collected during these checks, especially when traditional systems send the face to a server for analysis.

Incode’s On-Device Age Estimation runs facial age estimation and passive liveness detection directly on a user’s phone, tablet, or laptop, so the face is not transmitted or stored. The system sends onward only whether the user meets the platform’s required age threshold, while server-side analysis of session metadata is used to detect injection attacks, tampering, and other session integrity issues. Incode says it compressed the models to roughly one-tenth of their original size using knowledge distillation so they can run in an ordinary browser or app without special hardware.

The article connects the product to Incode’s $100 million commitment to privacy-preserving identity infrastructure and its acquisition of Identiq, which focuses on privacy-enhancing technology for anti-fraud collaboration. It says Identiq’s technology is intended to let organizations share fraud signals without exposing customer data to a third party or creating central data lakes. Incode presents these efforts as part of a broader approach in which privacy and fraud prevention are handled together as age checks become law around the world.

  • The article frames privacy policies as insufficient for biometric age checks because they only assign responsibility after misuse or a breach, while on-device processing is presented as removing the face from the data flow entirely.
  • Incode’s design still relies on server-side session metadata because the article says a purely local age check could be easier to cheat through injected camera feeds or manipulated devices.
  • The article positions facial age estimation as a practical compliance option for users without government ID because it does not require documents or database lookup.
  • Incode links rising age-verification regulation with increasing AI-assisted fraud, arguing that age checks must solve both privacy concerns and spoofing resistance at the same time.
  • The acquisition of Identiq expands the article’s privacy theme beyond age checks into shared fraud intelligence, emphasizing collaboration without creating centralized pools of customer data.

The article says more countries are requiring websites and apps to check users’ ages, and Incode is offering a way to do this by analyzing a person’s face only on their own phone, tablet, or laptop instead of sending or storing the face elsewhere. This affects people who need to prove their age online and the platforms that must follow these new laws. It matters because many people worry about companies collecting face data, and the article argues that keeping the face on the user’s device lowers the chance it could be stolen or misused while still helping block fake checks.

The Squarespace Phish With No Brand Text to Match

A Squarespace phishing email bypassed brand-text detection by hiding the brand name with invisible Unicode characters and placing it only inside an image, while passing SPF, DKIM, and DMARC through a hijacked aged domain. It was ultimately caught through behavioral signals such as malicious link reputation, sender anomalies, and OCR/image-based detection.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A credential-harvesting email impersonating Squarespace reached a K-12 school district and used a fake billing notice to warn that a domain would be deactivated in two days.
  • 02The word “Squarespace” did not appear as machine-readable text in the message because the logo and wordmark were delivered as an image and the subject and sender display name were padded with invisible U+206E characters.
  • 03The message passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC because it was sent from the hijacked 24-year-old domain oldanopetroli[.]com through Aliyun DirectMail infrastructure.
  • 04The payment link led to a tokenized subdomain on compromised studiointech[.]net, which was flagged malicious.
  • 05IRONSCALES Themis flagged the incident at 90% confidence as Credential Theft and Image-Based Attack and automatically resolved it as phishing before anyone in the district acted on it.

A credential-harvesting email impersonating Squarespace reached a K-12 school district and appeared to be a billing notice about an expiring subscription. It warned that a domain would be deactivated in two days and offered a “Payment link” button. The article says the message mattered because it looked like a familiar brand while leaving automated brand detection with no normal text to match.

The attackers hid the readable brand name by putting the Squarespace wordmark and logo in an image and padding the subject line and sender display name with invisible U+206E characters. The email also passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC because it was sent from the hijacked 24-year-old domain oldanopetroli[.]com through Aliyun DirectMail infrastructure. IRONSCALES Themis flagged the incident at 90% confidence as Credential Theft and Image-Based Attack, relying on signals such as malicious link reputation, sender anomalies, OCR, and community correlation.

  • Brand-impersonation detection that depends on finding readable brand text can fail when the brand appears only in an image and is fragmented in headers with invisible Unicode characters.
  • Passing SPF, DKIM, and DMARC did not indicate legitimacy in this case because the attacker used a hijacked, aged domain that was authorized to send through a bulk mail service.
  • OCR, link reputation, sender behavior, and community signals were more useful than standard content scanning for catching this message.
  • The European-style amount and date format were a practical clue that the lure did not fit the targeted US school district.

A fake Squarespace billing email reached a K-12 school district and tried to trick people into clicking a payment link to give up credentials. It looked real because the attackers hid the readable brand name from automated checks, put the logo in an image, and sent it from a real older domain they had taken over. This matters because even messages that appear properly sent and familiar can be scams, especially for organizations like schools with limited security teams.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a specific phishing incident with broadly relevant evasion techniques, including invisible Unicode obfuscation, OCR-only branding, and fully authenticated sending through a hijacked aged domain. It is not a major breach or critical patch, but it provides actionable lessons for email security professionals about why content-only brand detection and SPF/DKIM/DMARC trust can fail.
