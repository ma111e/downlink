Microsoft’s X account hacked in crypto pump-and-dump scheme

Microsoft’s official X account was hijacked to promote a fraudulent Clippy-branded crypto token in an apparent pump-and-dump scheme. Microsoft secured the account, removed the posts, disavowed any connection to the token, and said it is investigating and pursuing legal action.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Unknown attackers hijacked Microsoft’s official X account and used it to follow and repost content promoting a Clippy-branded cryptocurrency token.
  • 02Microsoft said it secured the account, removed the unauthorized posts, and is investigating how the access occurred.
  • 03Microsoft stated that it has not authorized, sponsored, or endorsed any cryptocurrency token associated with Clippy, Microsoft, or $MSFT, and said it will pursue legal action.
  • 04Microsoft India’s X account was also hijacked in June 2024 to promote a Roaring Kitty-themed cryptocurrency scam that used a site designed to steal crypto assets from connected wallets.
  • 05The article notes that the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission’s X account was compromised in a SIM-swapping attack that posted a false Bitcoin ETF approval announcement and temporarily increased Bitcoin’s price.

Unknown attackers hijacked Microsoft’s official X account, @Microsoft, and used it in an apparent pump-and-dump scheme promoting a Clippy-branded cryptocurrency token. The account, which has more than 13 million followers, followed and reposted content from a now-suspended account impersonating Microsoft’s Clippy virtual assistant. Another account continued promoting the \(Clippy token and claimed it had a liquidity pool paired directly with \)MSFT. Microsoft confirmed that unauthorized access occurred, secured the account, removed the posts, and said it is investigating.

Microsoft said it has not authorized, sponsored, endorsed, or permitted any cryptocurrency token connected to Clippy, Microsoft, or \(MSFT, and it plans to pursue legal action to remove the token and related materials. The incident follows a June 2024 compromise of Microsoft India’s X account, which attackers used to promote a Roaring Kitty-themed crypto scam and direct people to a site that allegedly stole assets from connected cryptocurrency wallets. The article also notes broader misuse of compromised X accounts, including a SIM-swapping compromise of the SEC’s account that posted a false Bitcoin ETF approval announcement and temporarily raised Bitcoin’s price. ScamSniffer reported that the MS Drainer wallet-stealing operation took roughly \)59 million from 63,000 people through a Twitter advertising campaign between March and November 2023.

  • Microsoft India’s 2024 account hijack shows that compromised official accounts can be used not only to promote tokens but also to direct people to sites that steal cryptocurrency from connected wallets.

Android 17 Advanced Protection Locks Accessibility Services to Verified Accessibility Tools

Android 17’s Advanced Protection will restrict accessibility-service access to verified Accessibility Tools, blocking a commonly abused route for banking trojans and spyware. The update also adds protections such as intrusion logging, USB restrictions, WebGPU disabling, and failed-authentication lockdown.
KEY POINTS
  • 01In Android 17, enabling Advanced Protection restricts AccessibilityService access to verified applications categorized as Accessibility Tools.
  • 02Google said the restriction closes a major attack path while preserving essential assistive technology.
  • 03Malicious apps can abuse accessibility services to read sensitive data, initiate fraudulent banking transfers, log keystrokes, display fake login screens, and grant themselves additional permissions.
  • 04Android 17 Advanced Protection also includes intrusion logging, USB Protection, the option to disable WebGPU, Failed Authentication Lock, and a view showing apps that checked Advanced Protection status.
  • 05Users who already use Advanced Protection will receive a notification when the new capabilities reach their devices, and must manually enable Intrusion Logging in Advanced Protection settings.

Google announced that Android 17 will restrict access to the AccessibilityService API when Advanced Protection is enabled. Only verified applications categorized as Accessibility Tools will be allowed to use the service. Google said this closes a major attack route while preserving important assistive technology for users who need it.

Accessibility services can run in the background, observe screen activity, and interact with other apps on a user’s behalf. Criminal apps have abused these capabilities after persuading users to enable them, allowing fraudulent banking transfers, keystroke logging, fake login screens, sensitive-data theft, and additional permission grants. Android 17 also adds Intrusion Logging, USB Protection, an option to disable WebGPU, Failed Authentication Lock, and a page that shows which installed apps checked Advanced Protection status.

  • The protection is activated by enabling Advanced Protection rather than requiring users to assess every accessibility app individually.
  • The restriction is designed to preserve verified disability-assistance software while blocking unverified apps from using the same powerful phone-control capability.
  • Intrusion Logging is not automatically enabled with the new protections; users must manually turn it on in the Advanced Protection settings to use the forensic logging capability.

Google says Android 17 will add a setting that lets only approved apps designed to help people with disabilities use powerful phone-control features when Advanced Protection is turned on. This matters because criminals have abused those features to read private information, fake login screens, record typing, and move money through banking apps after tricking people into granting access. People who use Advanced Protection will also receive added safeguards against physical access and sophisticated spying, while legitimate assistive apps should continue to work.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

Google’s Android 17 Advanced Protection update targets a widely abused route for banking trojans and spyware by limiting accessibility-service access to verified assistive apps. It is broadly relevant to Android security professionals, though the article does not report active exploitation or an urgent patch.

Residential Proxy Risk: The Employee Earns Pennies, The Buyer Gets The Router

Silent Push found that Peer2Profit bandwidth-sharing clients can turn home or corporate-connected devices into AstroProxy exit nodes, allowing buyers to route abuse through trusted IP addresses. Researchers also bypassed AstroProxy’s internal-IP filter via DNS resolution to access a Portuguese router’s management interface, highlighting potential exposure of internal network resources.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Silent Push found that a residential IP enrolled as a Peer2Profit bandwidth-sharing node appeared in AstroProxy’s proxy inventory within about 10 minutes.
  • 02Peer2Profit pays users $0.28 per gigabyte for residential bandwidth, while AstroProxy sells residential proxy traffic for $7.60 per gigabyte.
  • 03During a 72-hour crawl, Silent Push observed 117,224 unique IP addresses across AstroProxy pools, including 60,247 residential addresses and an average of 1,071 new residential addresses per hour.
  • 04Silent Push bypassed AstroProxy’s filter on direct internal IP requests by using a domain name that resolved to a MEO router-management address, then accessed the interface and downloaded a file.
  • 05Silent Push said it has not handled an incident in which a bandwidth-sharing client was confirmed as the entry point, although it demonstrated internal-network access through the proxy arrangement.

Silent Push researchers reported that Peer2Profit, a bandwidth-sharing program, can enroll users’ residential connections into AstroProxy’s commercial proxy network. In a controlled test, a clean residential IP enrolled in Peer2Profit appeared in AstroProxy’s proxy data within roughly 10 minutes. Peer2Profit pays residential users \(0.28 per gigabyte, while AstroProxy sells residential traffic for \)7.60 per gigabyte; Silent Push calculated that the operation retains more than 97% of the resale value.

Over 72 hours, Silent Push counted 117,224 unique IP addresses across three AstroProxy pools, including 60,247 residential addresses, with an average of 1,071 new residential addresses added each hour. Researchers also bypassed AstroProxy’s direct internal-address filter by using a domain name that resolved to a MEO router-management address, allowing them to reach the interface and download a file without credentials. The article says an employee-installed bandwidth-sharing client could make a corporate IP address available as a proxy exit node, causing abuse traffic to appear to originate from that organization and potentially exposing internal resources.

  • The economic incentive is highly uneven: Peer2Profit pays residential users $0.28 per gigabyte while AstroProxy sells that traffic for $7.60 per gigabyte, which Silent Push calculates leaves the operation with more than 97% of the resale value.
  • Bandwidth-sharing clients may evade ordinary endpoint defenses because users can install them deliberately and the associated servers and APIs do not appear in standard threat-intelligence or reputation feeds.
  • Rapid residential-proxy churn limits the usefulness of reputation-based blocking, because Silent Push observed an average of 1,071 new residential addresses entering AstroProxy’s pool each hour.
  • A filter that blocks direct requests to internal network addresses can be bypassed when it permits a domain name that resolves to an internal address, as Silent Push demonstrated against a MEO router interface.
  • The article identifies proxy-pool enumeration, rather than endpoint tooling, as a way to determine whether an organization’s network address is currently being sold as proxy bandwidth.

The article describes apps that pay people a few cents to let strangers use their internet connection, then resell that access through a service called AstroProxy. Silent Push showed that someone paying for this access could use a participating home connection in Portugal to reach the home router’s settings page and download a file without knowing the owner’s password. Employees who install such apps on work or personal devices can make their employer’s internet address appear to be the source of fraud or other harmful activity. This can damage the organization’s reputation, get its internet address blocked, and potentially expose devices inside its network.

The article reports a concrete Silent Push finding that commercial residential proxy access could be used to reach a router management interface through a DNS-based filter bypass.
