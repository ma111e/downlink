A DMARC Pass With No SPF, and a CTA Full of Hidden Letters

A phishing email impersonating Fifth Third Bank passed DMARC despite SPF returning None because an aligned DKIM signature was enough to authenticate it. The key takeaway is that a DMARC pass is not a trust verdict, especially when attackers abuse legitimate domains and hide malicious CTA text with HTML obfuscation.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A sales representative at a medical device manufacturer received a document notification impersonating Fifth Third Bank that was labeled credential theft and automatically resolved as phishing.
  • 02The email passed DKIM, DMARC, and Microsoft composite authentication even though SPF returned None for the Amazon SES sending host, because one DKIM signature aligned with the From domain.
  • 03The sending domain belonged to a real media business in Botswana, and the landing page was hosted on an unrelated Malaysian corporate domain, making both businesses bystanders rather than attacker-owned infrastructure.
  • 04The call-to-action text looked normal to a human reader but contained seven hidden ASCII letters inside inline HTML elements styled to near-zero width, causing scanners that read raw text to see a garbled phrase.
  • 05The email hotlinked four real Fifth Third Bank pages, including the bank’s phishing-awareness page, while using a blank subject line and no attachment.

A sales representative at a medical device manufacturer received a document notification impersonating Fifth Third Bank. The message was assessed as a high-severity credential-harvesting, brand-impersonation, scanner-evasion, and authentication-abuse attack. It used a blank subject line, no attachment, real Fifth Third Bank footer links, and a call-to-action that led through Outlook Safe Links to a path on an unrelated Malaysian corporate domain.

The message passed DKIM, DMARC, and Microsoft composite authentication even though SPF returned None for the Amazon SES sending host. The article explains that DMARC passed because one DKIM signature aligned with the From domain, which belonged to a real Botswana media business, while the landing page domain also belonged to an unrelated legitimate business. The call-to-action text rendered normally to a human reader, but its HTML hid seven ordinary ASCII letters in near-zero-width inline elements, making scanners read the anchor text as garbled content. The article’s defensive takeaway is to treat DMARC pass as an alignment fact rather than a trust verdict and to compare rendered message text against the underlying content.

  • A DMARC pass should be treated as an alignment fact rather than a trust verdict, because the article shows that DKIM alone can produce a clean pass even when SPF returns None for the sending host.
  • An aligned DKIM signature on an aged domain paired with SPF None is a suspicious pattern because it suggests someone could verify the domain with a sending platform without maintaining consistent control of the whole DNS zone.
  • The mismatch between rendered button text and raw extracted text is itself a stronger indicator than the hidden words, since legitimate bulk senders do not normally hide individual letters inside visible words with near-zero-width styling.
  • Blocklisting an entire compromised or abused third-party domain can punish uninvolved businesses, so the article recommends focusing on the specific URL path and sender pattern instead.
  • URL reputation and authentication checks were insufficient in this case because the email used genuine bank links, a security-product link wrapper, legitimate third-party domains, and a full composite authentication pass.

A sales representative at a medical device manufacturer received a fake Fifth Third Bank document message that looked technically trustworthy, even though it led to a password-stealing page on an unrelated Malaysian business’s website. The message also used a real Botswana media company’s domain to help it look legitimate, so those two real businesses were bystanders rather than the attackers. This matters because criminals can borrow trusted services, real company domains, and even real bank links to make scams look safe, while hiding small tricks in the message that people and simple scanners may miss.

The Anatomy of a Pentest: Where Does AI Change the Game?

AI does not change the core phases of pentesting, but it should be used selectively where reasoning matters most: exploitation and evidence-based reporting. Broad discovery, recon, and baseline testing are better handled by deterministic automation so organizations can afford to test far more assets more often.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The article argues that the structure of pentesting has not changed, but AI is becoming a new executor for parts of the existing process.
  • 02Discovery, recon, and baseline security testing are described as mechanical tasks that deterministic automation can already handle at scale without model-based reasoning.
  • 03The article says using large language models across every phase can become costly because model use is metered by tokens, which can reduce coverage across large attack surfaces.
  • 04AI is presented as most valuable during exploitation, where a tester must form hypotheses, send requests, interpret responses, and adapt to prove that a weakness is real.
  • 05The article emphasizes that real exploitation must stay within approved scope and avoid destructive actions because it runs against live production systems.

The article examines how AI changes pentesting by looking at the process phase by phase. It argues that the pentest itself has not changed, only who or what performs each part of it. The five phases used are discovery and scoping, recon and enumeration, testing for security weaknesses, exploitation, and reporting with validation and learning.

A central point is that AI should not be applied equally across every phase because model usage is metered by tokens and can become expensive at scale. The article says discovery, recon, and baseline testing are largely mechanical tasks that deterministic automation and existing tools can handle broadly and cheaply. It contrasts this with model-driven orchestration, where repeated model calls around standard scanner output can produce the same results at much higher cost.

The article identifies exploitation as the phase where AI reasoning can change what is possible because it requires forming hypotheses, sending requests, interpreting responses, and adapting to prove a weakness is real. It gives the example of a leaked production signing key that could be used to forge a valid token and bypass authorization. It also stresses that real exploitation must remain within approved scope and avoid corrupting data or disrupting availability, while reporting should be grounded in reproduced evidence rather than inferred findings.

  • The article frames AI pentesting economics as a coverage problem: using models for routine steps can make testing so expensive that organizations again test only a small set of assets.
  • The article identifies discovery and scoping as the point where the coverage gap begins, because unknown or forgotten assets are excluded before any testing starts.
  • The article argues that model-based orchestration of standard recon tools can produce the same map as deterministic workflows while costing one to two orders of magnitude more per asset.
  • The article suggests that evidence-backed exploitation can make remediation more immediately actionable because it points to the exact failed link, such as a leaked signing key, unauthenticated endpoint, or missing authorization check.

The article explains how AI changes security testing by looking at each step of the process and arguing that AI should not be used for everything. It says companies often test only a few important systems once or twice a year, while many internet-facing systems change every day and may be forgotten or unknown. The main point is that routine mapping and basic checks are cheaper with normal automation, while AI is most useful when it can reason through a real weakness and show clear proof. This matters because large organizations may have tens of thousands, or even millions, of exposed systems, and the cost of testing decides how many of them actually get checked.
