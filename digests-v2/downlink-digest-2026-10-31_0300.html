A Shared ChatGPT Link Could Run Attacker Code in Your Session and Read Private Chats

Researchers found that a malicious shared ChatGPT link could escape its Preview sandbox and execute code in a logged-in victim’s session, potentially exposing private chats and allowing account actions. OpenAI fixed the P1 flaw within eight days of disclosure, and users do not need to take action.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Parameter researchers found that a malicious ChatGPT shared-conversation link could execute attacker code within a signed-in visitor's ChatGPT session and access private conversations.
  • 02The attack used ChatGPT Preview together with an interactive graph that loaded MathLive, whose message handler accepted HTML-containing messages without confirming which browser frame sent them.
  • 03Parameter reported the issue through OpenAI's bug bounty program on October 18, and OpenAI fixed it eight days later after rating it P1, its most serious severity category.
  • 04OpenAI's fixes caused both ChatGPT web frontend versions to reject the messages used by the attack by October 26.
  • 05Parameter said it found no real-world exploitation and conducted its private-chat proof of concept only between separate accounts.

Researchers at Parameter found that a malicious public ChatGPT shared-conversation link could execute attacker-controlled code within the signed-in session of someone who opened it. The code could list recent conversations, open a private chat that had never been shared with the attacker, and send a small portion of it to a researcher-controlled server. Parameter said the attack could also perform actions with the same access as the victim’s ChatGPT account. The researchers reported no real-world exploitation and performed their proof of concept only between separate accounts.

The attack combined ChatGPT’s Preview feature with an interactive graph that loaded the MathLive library into the main ChatGPT page. Although Preview was isolated in a sandbox, a MathLive listener accepted messages from any sender and allowed raw HTML in keyboard-layout data, enabling the Preview to inject HTML into the trusted page. The injected HTML created a frame treated as part of chatgpt.com, while a script from YouTube supplied the final code because ChatGPT’s content security policy allowed YouTube scripts. Parameter reported the issue through OpenAI’s bug bounty program on October 18; OpenAI rated it P1 and fixed it across two frontend versions by October 26, with no action required from users.

  • The attack did not require a victim to install software, join a workspace, or authenticate through the attacker; its later version could trigger merely by opening a shared chat link.
  • Although ChatGPT's Preview feature was isolated on a separate address, a separate MathLive message listener in the main ChatGPT page created the path out of that isolation.
  • The proof of concept called ChatGPT's signed-in APIs directly, so its access extended beyond the contents visible in the shared conversation.
  • ChatGPT's policy blocked code written directly into the page but permitted scripts from YouTube, which the researchers used as a workaround to load the final code.
  • Blocking the YouTube-based script source alone would not have addressed the underlying issue, because the core problem was that an untrusted Preview could inject HTML into the trusted main page.

Security researchers found that a harmful shared ChatGPT conversation link could run code as anyone who opened it while signed in, letting the sender view private chats and potentially act through that person’s account. The link could be sent through normal channels such as email or social posts, and in one version simply opening it was enough. OpenAI fixed the problem after the researchers reported it, so ChatGPT users do not need to install or reset anything. The case shows that even an ordinary-looking shared link can put private information at risk when a website’s safety barriers fail.

This is a high-severity, concrete finding in ChatGPT: a malicious shared link could execute code in a logged-in user’s session and access private chats or act through the account. OpenAI has already fixed the P1 issue and no real-world exploitation was reported, reducing immediate urgency but leaving broad relevance for web-application and AI-platform security professionals.

Cloudflare Reports 18.2 Million Network-Layer DDoS Attacks in Q3, Up 38%

Cloudflare mitigated 18.2 million network-layer DDoS attacks in Q3 2026, a 38% quarterly rise driven largely by DDoS-for-hire services and short, low-bandwidth attacks. While attacks above 1 Tbps fell after botnet disruption efforts, new botnets and a sharp increase in DNS amplification attacks show the threat remains active.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cloudflare autonomously mitigated 18.2 million network-layer DDoS attacks and 14.4 trillion HTTP DDoS requests during the third quarter of 2026.
  • 02The 18.2 million network-layer attacks represent 197,400 attacks per day, or roughly one every 0.44 seconds, and were up from 13.2 million in Q2 2026.
  • 03Cloudflare linked the increase in network-layer attacks to DDoS-for-hire booter and stresser services, while short attacks under 10 minutes and 1 Gbps rose 208%.
  • 04Hyper-volumetric attacks above 1 Tbps declined 69% quarter over quarter, falling from 805 in Q2 to 251 in Q3, following law-enforcement action against AISURU, KimWolf, JackSkid, and Mossad.
  • 05Media, production and publishing; information technology and services; gambling and casinos; computer software; and telecommunications were the most targeted industries, while the United States, China, Indonesia, the United Kingdom, and Türkiye were the most attacked countries.

Cloudflare said it autonomously mitigated 18.2 million network-layer DDoS attacks and 14.4 trillion HTTP DDoS requests in the third quarter of 2026. That equates to about 197,400 network-layer attacks each day, or one every 0.44 seconds. The 18.2 million attacks were up from 13.2 million in Q2 and 10 million in Q1, with DDoS-for-hire booter and stresser services linked to hundreds of thousands of attacks. Cloudflare reported roughly 8,226 network-layer attacks per hour.

Short attacks lasting under 10 minutes and below 1 Gbps increased 208%, while attacks exceeding 1 Tbps declined 69%, from 805 in Q2 to 251 in Q3. Cloudflare said the reduction in the largest attacks likely followed law-enforcement actions against AISURU, KimWolf, JackSkid, and Mossad, although newer variants such as Dysphoria and Evooo1Bot remain active. CLDAP floods declined, but DNS amplification attacks rose 215% and accounted for 3.43% of network-layer attacks. The most targeted sectors included media and publishing, IT services, gambling, software, and telecommunications, while the United States, China, Indonesia, the United Kingdom, and Türkiye were the most attacked countries.

  • The increase in DDoS activity was driven not only by massive floods but also by a 208% surge in short, low-bandwidth attacks lasting under 10 minutes and below 1 Gbps.
  • Law-enforcement action against several botnets coincided with a 69% decline in attacks exceeding 1 Tbps, but Cloudflare said newly emerging variants such as Dysphoria and Evooo1Bot mean the threat has not been eliminated.
  • Attack methods shifted during the quarter: CLDAP floods declined while DNS amplification attacks increased 215% and reached 3.43% of all network-layer attacks.
  • Canada and Israel entered the 10 most attacked countries, with attacks on Canadian retail, telecommunications, and marketing organizations contributing to Canada’s ranking.

Cloudflare says it blocked 18.2 million attempts in July through September 2026 to overwhelm online services and make them unavailable, up 38% from the prior quarter. Media and publishing, technology, gambling, software, and telecommunications organizations were among the most affected, with the United States, China, Indonesia, the United Kingdom, and Türkiye seeing the most activity. Although extremely large attacks declined after law-enforcement action against several networks of compromised devices, new versions continue to appear and smaller, frequent attacks are rising, meaning organizations and the people who rely on their online services can still face disruptions.
