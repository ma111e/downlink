The Risk of AI-Powered Antidetect Browsers in Online Fraud and Influence Operations

AI-enabled antidetect browsers such as Kameleo, AdsPower, and MultiLogin let operators mask device identities, automate thousands of accounts, and evade platform defenses, enabling scalable fraud, account takeover, scraping, and coordinated influence campaigns. Their combination of fingerprint spoofing, residential proxies, cookie injection, account warming, and LLM-driven automation lowers the barrier for sophisticated abuse.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Antidetect browsers such as Kameleo, AdsPower, and MultiLogin create isolated browser profiles with fabricated device fingerprints, allowing operators to manage many accounts while attempting to evade platform detection.
  • 02The article documents criminal uses including account takeover through stolen session cookies, card testing with stolen payment credentials, fraudulent loan applications, and bulk creation of accounts for advertising fraud.
  • 03AdsPower provides automation features, including an RPA marketplace, account warming, and a Model Context Protocol server that can connect large language model workflows to browser profiles for automated content distribution.
  • 04Kameleo supports WebRTC suppression and cookie injection, which can insert an already authenticated session into a browser profile without a new login event.
  • 05The article argues that the combination of identity spoofing, residential proxies, automated account activity, scraping, and AI-generated content lowers the barrier for large-scale fraud and coordinated inauthentic influence operations.

Check Point Research examines Kameleo, AdsPower, and MultiLogin, commercially available antidetect browsers that create separate browser profiles with spoofed device characteristics. The article says these tools can let a small team manage thousands of accounts across social media, e-commerce, and financial platforms while attempting to avoid systems that identify coordinated or fraudulent activity. Documented criminal uses include account takeover, payment-card testing, fraudulent loan applications, advertising fraud, phishing, and malware distribution.

The tools combine fabricated browser fingerprints, residential proxies, WebRTC leak suppression, isolated cookies and storage, and automated account-warming activity to make accounts appear unrelated and legitimate. The article highlights cookie injection as a way to place stolen pre-authenticated session data into a browser profile without generating a new login event, and describes AI integrations that can generate and distribute content through automated workflows. It concludes that this infrastructure lowers the cost and technical barrier for large-scale fraud and coordinated influence operations, creating challenges for social platforms, financial services, companies, and their users.

  • Cookie injection can enable a stolen, already-authenticated session to be placed into a new browser profile without a visible login event, potentially bypassing checks that depend on detecting unusual sign-ins or multi-factor authentication prompts.
  • Account warming is designed to build weeks of apparently normal activity before abuse begins, meaning accounts may look legitimate during the period when platforms would otherwise assess their trustworthiness.
  • The article describes an end-to-end automation path in which AI-generated content can be routed through pre-warmed profiles and distributed using ready-made workflows, reducing the need for an operator to actively run the campaign.
  • Scraping capabilities can turn access to a closed social-media group into targeting data, enabling artificial accounts to tailor messages to members' stated interests, locations, and engagement patterns.
  • The tools' commercial support, multilingual availability, large profile limits, proxy integrations, and cryptocurrency payment options indicate an operational ecosystem extending beyond isolated custom-built criminal tooling.

The article describes browser tools that let one person run many online accounts while making each account look like it belongs to a different real person using a different device and location. Criminals can use them to take over banking or cryptocurrency accounts, test stolen card details, create fake accounts, spread scams, and influence conversations on social media. Social networks, financial services, businesses, and ordinary users are affected because the fake accounts can appear trustworthy and may evade existing checks. Adding AI-written content and automation makes these campaigns cheaper and easier to run at a scale once associated with large, well-funded groups.

There's only so much code: the rise in vulnerability numbers

AI-assisted vulnerability discovery is driving a sharp rise in disclosed flaws in legacy software, but most are not being exploited in the wild. Defenders should prioritize vulnerabilities that are demonstrably exploitable in their environments while strengthening layered mitigations and patching processes.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Frontier AI models, including Mythos, are rapidly finding previously undiscovered weaknesses in decades-old, widely used software, contributing to 1,415 Microsoft vulnerability advisories in October after 975 in September.
  • 02The article reports that only three of Microsoft’s 975 September vulnerability advisories had detected in-the-wild exploitation, while Known Exploited Vulnerabilities increased 37% year over year compared with a 105% increase in published CVEs.
  • 03Open-source projects are being flooded with vulnerability reports, placing pressure on maintainers and challenging the traditional 90-day coordinated disclosure model.
  • 04The article suggests AI-assisted discovery may eventually level off as the backlog of historical code is reviewed and new software is checked with AI before release.
  • 05The article recommends that organizations prioritize flaws with high exploitability in their own environments, patch promptly, use interim mitigations such as LiveProtect, and deploy layered behavioral and intrusion-prevention protections.

Frontier AI models such as Mythos are rapidly finding weaknesses in decades-old, widely used software, contributing to a sharp increase in vulnerability disclosures. Microsoft issued 975 vulnerability advisories in September and 1,415 in October, compared with a pre-June monthly average of about 100. The article says this surge is overwhelming some open-source maintainers and increasing pressure on the traditional 90-day coordinated disclosure model.

The disclosure increase has not been matched by a proportional increase in known exploitation. Only three of Microsoft’s 975 September advisories had detected in-the-wild exploitation, while Known Exploited Vulnerabilities grew 37% year over year compared with a 105% increase in published CVEs. The article argues that many AI-discovered issues may be impractical for attackers, although AI could also help attackers analyze vendor patches more quickly.

The article suggests that the current discovery spike may eventually decline after old code is reviewed and fixed, especially if developers use AI models to evaluate new code before release. It recommends that organizations patch promptly but focus on weaknesses that offer realistic paths into their own environments. It also advises layered protections such as interim mitigations including LiveProtect, custom IPS or Snort triggers, behavioral anomaly detection, and AI-based checks in software build chains.

  • The increase in reported vulnerabilities does not translate directly into active attacks: only three of Microsoft’s 975 September advisories had detected in-the-wild exploitation, despite the much larger disclosure volume.
  • The article notes that Known Exploited Vulnerabilities measure distinct flaws known to be abused, rather than the number of attacks or successful compromises, so attackers can repeatedly use a limited set of useful weaknesses against many organizations.
  • AI may reduce the time attackers need to understand vendor fixes, because attackers have historically used patches to identify the underlying weakness and AI can remove human analysis bottlenecks.
  • The traditional 90-day private disclosure period is under pressure because some researchers believe a flaw found by one AI system may effectively be discoverable by others immediately.
  • For developers, checking new code with current AI models is presented as comparatively inexpensive, while reviewing the accumulated backlog of old code is costly and therefore better approached in manageable chunks.

New AI tools are finding large numbers of security weaknesses in old, widely used software, helping drive Microsoft’s monthly security notices from roughly 100 to 1,415. This affects companies, open-source software projects, and anyone who relies on their tools, because maintainers can be overwhelmed by the work of fixing issues quickly. Most newly reported flaws are not known to be used by criminals, but attackers can use AI to study fixes faster, so organizations still need to prioritize the most dangerous problems and use several layers of protection while updates are installed.

Its figures on Microsoft advisory volume and limited known exploitation are useful context, but it does not identify an immediately actionable critical flaw or active campaign.

Rust 1.100 Will Add Dependency Cooldowns to Cargo

Rust 1.100 will add an opt-in Cargo dependency cooldown that blocks newly published crate versions for a configurable period, helping limit exposure to malicious supply-chain releases. The protection is not enabled by default, so teams must configure a minimum publish age themselves.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Rust 1.100, scheduled for November 12, will add stable support for Cargo’s configurable minimum publish age, although the setting remains disabled by default.
  • 02With a configured minimum publish age, Cargo excludes dependency releases that are newer than the specified waiting period and fails resolution if no eligible version satisfies the project’s requirements.
  • 03Compromised releases of arrayref, internment, and append-only-vec were available on crates.io for 86, 90, and 107 minutes, respectively, before removal in August.
  • 04Cargo preserves dependency versions already recorded in Cargo.lock even when they are younger than the configured minimum age.
  • 05The stabilized implementation does not apply the publish-age check to cargo install, Git dependencies, local path dependencies, or registries without publication timestamp metadata.

Rust 1.100, scheduled for November 12, will add stable support for Cargo’s configurable minimum publish age, allowing teams to delay adoption of newly released dependencies. The feature is opt-in: Cargo’s default minimum publish age remains zero, so teams must configure it themselves. A seven-day delay can be set in .cargo/config.toml with registry.global-min-publish-age = "7 days".

When resolving dependencies, Cargo skips releases newer than the configured age and fails if no older eligible version meets the project’s requirements. This behavior is intended to prevent attackers from making a malicious release the only available option by yanking safe versions, a tactic used against arrayref during an August compromise involving arrayref, internment, append-only-vec, and the malicious proc-macro1 crate. The cooldown does not apply to cargo install, Git dependencies, local path dependencies, or registries without publication-time metadata, and developers can temporarily override it for urgent updates.

  • Upgrading to Rust 1.100 alone does not add a waiting period for dependencies, because Cargo’s minimum publish age defaults to zero and teams must explicitly configure it.
  • Cargo is designed to fail rather than accept a too-new release when no older eligible version is available, preventing an attacker from making a malicious update the only resolvable option by yanking safe versions.
  • Enabling the cooldown does not remove recently published dependencies that are already listed in Cargo.lock, so existing lockfiles can retain versions younger than the configured threshold.
  • A temporary override for an urgent update disables the age check not only for the selected package but also for all transitive dependencies needed during that resolution.

Rust 1.100 will let software teams tell Cargo, Rust’s tool for downloading code packages, to wait a set amount of time before using newly released packages. This can give maintainers and security researchers time to spot and remove harmful updates, such as the compromised Rust packages briefly available in August, before they spread to more developers’ computers and automated build systems. The setting is optional and starts turned off, so teams must enable it themselves; it can reduce risk but cannot guarantee that an older harmful package will be caught.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

Cargo’s upcoming opt-in minimum publish-age control is broadly relevant to Rust teams managing third-party dependencies and directly addresses recently observed malicious crate releases. It is not an immediate patch or active-incident alert, but it provides a concrete new supply-chain protection teams can plan to enable.

KEY POINTS
  • 01ClearSky identified renewed activity in Israel by the Russian influence campaign known as Doppelgänger, or “The Double.”
  • 02Doppelgänger creates fake copies of recognized news sites, using similar designs, logos, and layouts to appear legitimate.
  • 03The campaign spreads false and misleading messages based partly on real publications through fake social-media accounts, mainly on X.
  • 04ClearSky documented at least 76 fake articles published between May and October 2026 on walla[.]ist, a website impersonating Walla.
  • 05The campaign aims to advance Russian interests, weaken trust in Israeli institutions, deepen divisions in Israeli society, and pollute AI engines.

ClearSky identified renewed activity in Israel by Doppelgänger, a Russian influence campaign also known as “The Double.” The campaign creates fake copies of established news websites, using similar designs, logos, and layouts to make them appear legitimate. It spreads false and misleading messages that incorporate parts of real publications. These messages are distributed primarily on X through a network of fake accounts.

ClearSky documented at least 76 fake articles on walla[.]ist, a website impersonating Walla, between May and October 2026. The campaign seeks to advance Russian interests, weaken trust in the government and public institutions, deepen divisions in Israeli society, and pollute AI engines. ClearSky states that the resurgence of activity since August 2026 may be tied to an effort to influence Israeli public opinion before the October 27 election. The linked report covers the infrastructure, scale, messages, and accounts identified by the researchers.

  • The campaign does not rely solely on fabricated stories; it combines misleading messages with portions of genuine publications, which can make impersonated content appear more credible.
  • ClearSky describes the operation as a sustained effort to shape public attitudes over time rather than a one-time misinformation event.
  • The campaign’s stated goal includes polluting AI engines, extending its potential influence beyond social-media audiences.
  • The renewed activity since August 2026 may be connected to efforts to influence Israeli public opinion ahead of the October 27 election, according to ClearSky.

ClearSky says a Russian campaign has returned in Israel, creating fake copies of well-known news websites and using fake X accounts to spread misleading stories. From May to October 2026, researchers found at least 76 fake articles on a site pretending to be the Walla news outlet. Israelis are affected because the campaign seeks to reduce trust in government and public institutions, increase social divisions, and possibly shape public opinion before the October 27 election.
