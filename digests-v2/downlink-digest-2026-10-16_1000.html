AI Decision Models and Their Impact on AI SOC Platforms

KEY POINTS
  • 01AI decision models return structured choices or probabilities rather than generated text, allowing SOC teams to automate alert handling with confidence thresholds.
  • 02The article argues that calibration does not ensure a correct decision when a model receives incomplete or misleading alert data, such as an EDR report that an infection was mitigated.
  • 03Intezer reports that 51% of machines with active infections found through live memory scans had already been marked as mitigated by their EDR.
  • 04The article says effective triage should collect forensic evidence such as process trees, memory contents, file detonation results, and network activity before making a verdict.
  • 05Organizational context, including normal user and asset behavior and prior case outcomes, must be continuously learned and refreshed because it is specific to each environment and changes over time.

AI decision models such as TypeSafe AI’s Jev, OpenAI’s Decisions API, and Cribl’s cribl-decision provide structured choices, scores, or yes/no answers with probabilities instead of generating free-form text. The article argues that these models could make it inexpensive to assess every security alert, automate actions using confidence thresholds, and retain auditable records of each decision. However, it cautions that a correctly formatted and calibrated answer can still be wrong when the model receives incomplete or misleading alert information.

The article cites Intezer’s AI SOC Report 2026, which analyzed 25 million alerts triaged during 2025 and found that 51% of machines with active infections identified through live memory scans had already been marked as mitigated by endpoint detection and response software. It argues that investigations should gather evidence such as process trees, command lines, memory contents, sandbox behavior, network activity, and information about normal behavior in the organization before making a verdict. The author also says organizational knowledge must be continuously updated and that automated decisions should be checked against real outcomes to ensure a model’s stated confidence remains accurate in that environment.

  • A decision model can be well calibrated yet make the wrong operational choice when the alert lacks critical context; for example, an alert marked as quarantined may omit evidence of an active memory-resident infection.
  • Intezer's 2025 data found that 51% of machines with active infections identified through live memory scans had already been reported as mitigated by their EDR, showing that a remediation status alone may be unreliable.
  • Low-severity alerts can still contain meaningful risk: nearly 1% of real incidents in the reported data traced back to the lowest severity levels, which the article estimates as roughly 54 real threats per year in an environment receiving 450,000 alerts.
  • Identity-alert context is highly organization-specific: only about 2% of impossible-travel alerts were confirmed compromises in the article's data, while VPN use accounted for around 30% of those alerts.
  • The article argues that organizational knowledge must be continuously updated rather than loaded once, because normal user behavior, assets, and tools change over time.

The article says new AI systems can quickly sort security warnings into likely harmless or likely dangerous, making it affordable for companies to review every warning instead of only the most serious ones. But these systems can still make confident wrong choices if they only see a short alert message and not detailed evidence from the affected computer or knowledge of what is normal at that company. This matters to businesses and their employees because threats can remain active even after security software says they were removed, while harmless alerts can overwhelm staff. The author argues that AI should be given real evidence and continuously updated company knowledge before it is trusted to close or escalate security cases.

Continuous accountability for Verified bots

Cloudflare is introducing continuous trust assessments for Verified Bots, measuring whether they honor robots.txt directives and reduce traffic after HTTP 429 responses. The system will build behavior-based trust bands so website owners can make access decisions based on sustained bot conduct, not identity alone.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cloudflare is introducing a system to assess whether Verified Bots continue to behave responsibly after their identities have been verified.
  • 02The system evaluates whether bots consistently follow website preferences expressed in robots.txt files, focusing on repeated patterns rather than isolated violations.
  • 03It also measures whether bots reduce or pause their request rate after receiving HTTP 429 Too Many Requests responses.
  • 04Cloudflare plans to translate these observations into explainable trust bands that show how consistently a bot respects site preferences and what behaviors informed the assessment.
  • 05Cloudflare is using the system internally and plans to make it available to all Cloudflare customers, who will be able to select a trust threshold for handling Verified Bot traffic.

Cloudflare announced a system for continuously evaluating whether Verified Bots behave responsibly after their identities have been confirmed. The company distinguishes identity from trust: a bot may be operated by a known entity but can still disregard website preferences, create excessive load, or evade controls. The system is intended to ensure trust is earned through sustained behavior rather than assumed from verification alone.

The initial evaluation considers whether bots follow robots.txt instructions and whether they adapt after receiving HTTP 429 Too Many Requests responses. Cloudflare will focus on repeated patterns instead of isolated violations, and will translate observations into explainable trust bands showing how consistently a bot respects preferences and what behaviors informed the assessment. Cloudflare is already using the system internally, plans to publish scores on Radar, and intends to make it available to all customers so they can select trust thresholds for handling Verified Bot traffic.

  • Verified status is not treated as permanent: Cloudflare distinguishes identity verification from trust, which must be continually earned through sustained behavior.
  • A robots.txt file is presented as a minimum restriction rather than permission for everything else, meaning bots may still be expected to stop when websites communicate that continued access is unwelcome through other signals.
  • HTTP 429 responses are treated as direct feedback that a bot should reduce its rate or pause, even if the bot is otherwise visiting allowed website paths.
  • Cloudflare emphasizes repeated behavior over isolated mistakes, allowing one-off violations or misconfigurations to carry less weight than an ongoing pattern of disregarding site preferences.
  • The planned trust bands are intended to be explainable, showing both a range of behavioral trust and the observations that led to it rather than assigning an unchanging label to a bot or operator.

Cloudflare is building a way to judge whether identified automated website visitors continue to behave responsibly over time, rather than trusting them simply because their operator is known. It checks whether these bots follow a website’s published rules and slow down when the site says it is receiving too many requests. This matters to website owners because even known bots can ignore limits, overload sites, or bypass requests not to collect content; Cloudflare plans to give customers a way to use this history when deciding how to handle them.
