AI tools are enabling non-technical employees to build workplace applications quickly using natural-language prompts. While these applications can automate workflows and reduce pressure on professional developers, unsanctioned apps can become shadow AI assets when they bypass IT and security oversight. The article says these apps may contain untested code, dangerous misconfigurations, risky open-source components, insecure access to critical systems, and poorly protected sensitive data. They may also lack patching, monitoring, logging, backups, disaster-recovery coverage, and appropriate identity and access controls.

Tenable argues that blanket bans on AI-aided development may drive employees to conceal their work, so it advocates structured governance instead. Its five-tier model includes executive strategy, governance boards and technical councils, departmental AI Functional Leaders, enablement teams, and employee support communities. AI Functional Leaders oversee departmental requests for skills, connectors, and data access, helping identify unvetted external AI components before applications reach production. Tenable also ties AI-tool access to mandatory security and responsible-use training, supplemented by webinars, courses, tool-specific instruction, and regular peer sessions.

  • The governance challenge is not limited to unauthorized apps: even approved citizen-developed apps can overwhelm IT and security teams because of the volume requiring review, deployment, and ongoing maintenance.
  • Citizen coders may use their legitimate business-application access to create local data lakes for AI-powered apps, creating both escalating AI-token costs and dangerous data sprawl.
  • Tenable gives departmental AI Functional Leaders direct responsibility for AI skill approvals, operational queues, and adoption, enabling them to identify unvetted external AI components before an app reaches production.
  • Tenable makes access to AI tools conditional on mandatory security and responsible-use training, then reinforces it with webinars, tool-specific instruction, and peer-support sessions.

The article explains that AI tools now let employees with little or no programming experience create workplace apps quickly, which can save time and automate routine work. If these apps are built and used without review by the company’s technology and security teams, they may expose sensitive information, connect unsafely to important company systems, or contain mistakes that are never fixed. Tenable says companies should guide this activity with approved tools, clear rules, training, and departmental leaders rather than banning it, because workers may otherwise hide what they build. The stakes are higher costs, scattered copies of company data, and security or legal problems affecting the whole organization.
