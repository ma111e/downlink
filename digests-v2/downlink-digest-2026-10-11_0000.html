Nippon Columbia malware incident exposes 8.6 million karaoke fan records

A malware infection at Daiichi Kosho contractor Nippon Columbia may have exposed personal data for 8.63 million karaoke customers and 93,000 employees in Japan. No theft or public leak has been confirmed, and the affected records reportedly did not include passwords or loyalty-point misuse evidence.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Daiichi Kosho disclosed that a malware infection at contractor Nippon Columbia Group may have exposed personal information for 8,631,000 customers and 93,000 employees.
  • 02The potentially exposed records include full names, genders, dates of birth, email addresses, and telephone numbers.
  • 03Daiichi Kosho said it has not confirmed data theft or leaks, and Nippon Columbia Group is investigating the incident’s cause, scope, and whether information was leaked online.
  • 04The company said passwords were not included in the exposed data and that it found no evidence of unauthorized loyalty-point use.
  • 05Customers of BIG ECHO, MEGA BIG, Karaoke CLUB DAM, Banana Club, B-GARAGE, and DK Dining may be affected.

Daiichi Kosho, a major Japanese karaoke operator, disclosed that a malware infection at contractor Nippon Columbia Group may have exposed personal information it handles on Daiichi Kosho’s behalf. Nippon Columbia notified Daiichi Kosho on October 5 after finding malware on an employee computer and isolated the affected system the following day. Daiichi Kosho says its own systems were not breached.

The potentially affected data covers 8,631,000 customers and 93,000 employees, including names, genders, dates of birth, email addresses, and telephone numbers. Customers of BIG ECHO, MEGA BIG, Karaoke CLUB DAM, Banana Club, B-GARAGE, and DK Dining may be impacted. Daiichi Kosho has not confirmed that data was stolen or leaked, but advises people to be cautious about unsolicited emails, text messages, or calls seeking payments or sensitive information.

The company said the data does not include passwords and that it has found no evidence of unauthorized loyalty-point use. Nippon Columbia Group reset passwords and other authentication credentials while investigating the cause and scope of the incident and whether data was leaked online. A later Daiichi Kosho update did not add information about whether a leak had occurred.

  • The incident shows that Daiichi Kosho’s customers could be affected through a contractor even though Daiichi Kosho says its own systems were not breached.
  • Because the potentially exposed information includes names, birth dates, email addresses, and phone numbers, Daiichi Kosho specifically advises customers to be cautious about unsolicited emails, text messages, and calls requesting payments or sensitive information.
  • The company reported that passwords were not included and that there is no evidence of unauthorized loyalty-point use, limiting two specific forms of immediate account-related impact.
  • The possible exposure spans customers across multiple Daiichi Kosho brands, including BIG ECHO, MEGA BIG, Karaoke CLUB DAM, Banana Club, B-GARAGE, and DK Dining.

A harmful computer program was found on an employee’s computer at Nippon Columbia, a company that handles customer information for Japanese karaoke operator Daiichi Kosho. The incident may involve personal details of about 8.63 million customers and 93,000 employees, including names, birthdays, email addresses, and phone numbers. Although no stolen or publicly released data has been confirmed and passwords were not included, affected people could face convincing scam emails, texts, or calls that use their personal information.

The DKIM Passed. Nobody Spoofed Anything.

A phishing email sent through a compromised legitimate mailbox passed SPF, DKIM, and ARC after being auto-forwarded through a partner’s Microsoft 365 tenant and re-signed by the recipient’s Google Workspace group. A DKIM pass—especially on the recipient’s own key—does not prove a message is trustworthy; organizations should scrutinize forwarded mail, group posting policies, and misaligned header From domains.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The phishing message originated through authenticated SMTP submission to a Japanese small-business Postfix server using valid credentials for a real mailbox.
  • 02A partner organization’s Microsoft 365 tenant automatically forwarded the message to the European cyber-insurance provider’s Google Workspace administrative group, despite Microsoft having assigned it an SCL:9 spam score.
  • 03The recipient’s Google group redistributed and re-signed the message, causing delivery authentication to show DKIM and SPF passes on the recipient’s own domain while DMARC failed for the visible From domain.
  • 04The email body consisted of “Fix below” and a single redirector link that led to a numeric-prefixed Azure Front Door host with an independent malicious scanner verdict.
  • 05The article recommends reviewing which domain supplied a passing DKIM signature, investigating header From domains that no organization in the delivery path owns, and auditing inbound partner auto-forwards and group posting policies.

A phishing email originated from a compromised real mailbox at a Japanese small business after an attacker authenticated to its Postfix server with valid SMTP credentials. The sender used a display-name impersonation of a communications platform, a subject line the vendor genuinely uses, and a two-word body—“Fix below”—with one link. The message was first sent to a partner organization’s Microsoft 365 tenant, which had already classified it as spam with an SCL:9 score.

The partner tenant automatically forwarded the email to a European cyber-insurance provider’s Google Workspace administrative group, despite the upstream spam classification not being carried forward. The Google group redistributed and re-signed the message, so final delivery to the chief revenue officer showed DKIM and SPF passes tied to the recipient organization’s own infrastructure, along with six valid ARC seals, while DMARC failed for the visible From domain. The article emphasizes that a passing DKIM result can show only that a recipient-side system imported and signed a message, not that the visible sender or message is trustworthy; it recommends examining partner auto-forwards, group posting policies, and cases where the visible From domain is not owned by anyone in the delivery path.

  • A valid DKIM signature from the recipient organization’s own Google Workspace key can indicate that a group imported and redistributed a message, rather than proving that the visible sender is legitimate.
  • The partner tenant’s SCL:9 spam verdict did not protect the final recipient because the automatic forwarding process relayed the email without carrying Microsoft’s spam judgment to the next organization.
  • ARC seals accurately preserved each provider’s observed authentication results, but the six valid seals also carried the authentication of a compromised legitimate mailbox through the delivery chain.
  • The DMARC failure was a meaningful warning because the visible From domain was not owned by any organization in the enumerated delivery path, while the identities that passed authentication belonged to recipient-side or partner infrastructure.
  • A two-word lure and a single link were sufficient to appear credible after the message reached an administrative distribution list through trusted organizational email plumbing.

Someone stole the password for a real small-business email account and used it to send a fake message pretending to come from a well-known communications company. The message was automatically passed through a partner company and then into a European cyber-insurance provider’s internal email group, ultimately reaching its chief revenue officer. Because the message travelled through legitimate systems using real accounts, common email trust checks made it look safe even though it contained a malicious link. This shows that organizations can be exposed not only by fake senders, but also by compromised accounts and automatic forwarding rules at trusted partners.

It is useful for email-security professionals because it identifies actionable header and forwarding patterns, but it does not describe a broadly active named campaign or an urgent vulnerability.

Emerging Threat: (CVE-2026-107406) NetScaler ADC and Gateway Remote Code Execution via SAML

CVE-2026-107406 is a critical NetScaler ADC and Gateway SAML memory overflow that can enable unauthenticated remote code execution or denial of service on internet-facing appliances. Organizations should urgently identify SAML-configured instances and upgrade to Citrix’s fixed builds, as no workaround is available.
KEY POINTS
  • 01CVE-2026-107406 is a critical memory overflow flaw in NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway that can cause remote code execution or denial of service without requiring privileges or user interaction.
  • 02The issue affects appliances configured as SAML service providers or identity providers, although certain newer build ranges are affected only when configured as identity providers.
  • 03Citrix released fixed builds including NetScaler ADC and Gateway 14.1-73.46 or later and 13.1-64.29 or later, with separate fixed versions for FIPS and NDcPP branches.
  • 04There is no Citrix workaround other than upgrading or removing SAML authentication where it is not required, and end-of-life appliances require migration to supported versions.
  • 05External scans cannot reliably determine whether a NetScaler appliance is vulnerable because build numbers and SAML configuration are generally not visible externally.

CVE-2026-107406 is a critical memory overflow vulnerability in NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway that can enable remote code execution or denial of service. It has a CVSS v4.0 score of 9.5, can be reached over a network, and requires neither privileges nor user interaction. Citrix reported no awareness of unmitigated exploitation at disclosure, and the article states that no public proof of concept had been identified at publication.

The issue applies only to appliances configured as SAML service providers or identity providers, with newer listed build ranges affected only in the identity-provider role. NetScaler systems commonly sit at an organization’s internet-facing access point and broker authentication for applications behind them, making control of an appliance significant. External scans can identify a NetScaler device but generally cannot determine its build number or whether its SAML configuration makes it affected.

Citrix released fixed builds for all affected supported lines, including 14.1-73.46 or later and 13.1-64.29 or later, with separate targets for FIPS and NDcPP variants. There is no workaround other than upgrading or removing unnecessary SAML authentication, while end-of-life appliances require migration to a supported release. The article recommends inventorying internet-facing appliances, checking their SAML settings and exact builds, prioritizing devices with authentication login panels, and watching for unexplained crashes, restarts, or abnormal requests.

  • An internet-facing NetScaler AAA login panel does not prove that SAML is enabled, but it identifies appliances running the authentication layer where SAML configurations reside and should be reviewed first.
  • External exposure data is unusually limited for this issue: every observed asset was identified only at the fingerprint level, with no high-confidence version detections, so scanning cannot separate patched appliances from affected ones.
  • For newer builds in the listed 14.1 and 13.1 ranges, only the SAML identity-provider role is affected, whereas older builds are affected in either SAML role.
  • The 13.1 FIPS and NDcPP branch uses a different build numbering scheme, making its required fixed version easy to misread during remediation.

The article warns about CVE-2026-107406, a serious flaw in NetScaler ADC and Gateway devices that organizations use to provide remote access to applications. Devices using SAML, a sign-in system that lets services verify users’ identities, may let an outsider take control of the device or cause it to stop working. This matters because these devices often sit at an organization’s internet-facing entrance and handle access to many internal applications, so a compromise could affect far more than one server. Organizations need to check which devices use this sign-in setup and install Citrix’s updated software, because there is no simple temporary fix.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a critical patch advisory for internet-facing NetScaler ADC and Gateway appliances, where a SAML configuration can allow unauthenticated remote code execution or denial of service. Although no exploitation or public proof of concept was known at publication, affected organizations need to verify configuration and upgrade because no workaround is available.
