Cleverbridge warns hackers stole credit card numbers, CVVs in data breach

E-commerce platform Cleverbridge says attackers stole customers’ payment card numbers, expiration dates, and CVV/CVC codes after unauthorized activity was detected on November 4, 2026. The company is notifying affected customers, offering credit monitoring, and advises them to watch for fraudulent charges and contact their card issuers.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Cleverbridge detected unauthorized activity on November 4, 2026, temporarily took systems offline, and later determined that a third party copied payment card data stored on its platform.
  • 02A breach notification shared with BleepingComputer states that the stolen information included payment card numbers, expiration dates, and CVV/CVC security codes.
  • 03Cleverbridge says no other personal data, such as names, contact information, or billing details, were copied.
  • 04The company began notifying affected customers on November 20 and is working with TransUnion’s Cyberscout to provide credit monitoring and fraud assistance.
  • 05Cleverbridge said the incident was not ransomware and that its systems were not encrypted by the attackers.

Cleverbridge, an e-commerce platform used by software and SaaS companies to process purchases and subscriptions, says attackers copied payment card data from its platform. The company detected unauthorized activity on November 4, 2026, temporarily took systems offline, and later restored them that day. It began notifying affected customers on November 20.

A notification shared with BleepingComputer says the stolen information includes card numbers, expiration dates, and CVV/CVC security codes, while names, contact information, and billing details were not copied. Cleverbridge has not disclosed how attackers accessed its systems, how long they remained there, how many customers were affected, or which client companies’ customers were impacted. The company says the incident was not ransomware and that attackers did not encrypt its systems.

Cleverbridge has notified data protection authorities, law enforcement, payment card networks, and payment partners. It is working with TransUnion’s Cyberscout to provide affected customers with credit monitoring and fraud assistance. Customers are advised to review card statements for unauthorized transactions and contact their card issuers, which may decide to replace compromised cards.

  • The inclusion of CVV/CVC codes is especially consequential because Cleverbridge notes these codes are used to verify payment card transactions.
  • The impact may extend across customers of multiple software and SaaS companies that use Cleverbridge, but the company has not identified which client businesses’ customers were affected.
  • Customers should not rely only on the absence of other stolen personal details, because Cleverbridge specifically advises reviewing card statements and contacting card issuers, which may choose to replace compromised cards.
  • Important details remain undisclosed, including how attackers entered the platform, how long they had access, how many customers were affected, and whether the card data was encrypted.

Cleverbridge, a company that handles online sales and subscriptions for software businesses, says someone copied customers’ payment card information from its platform. The stolen details include card numbers, expiration dates, and the security code on the card, although Cleverbridge says names, contact details, and billing information were not copied. People who used affected cards could face unauthorized charges, so the company is notifying customers, offering monitoring help, and advising them to check statements and contact their banks.

Hackers exploit GL.iNet cloud flaw to infect routers, KVM devices

Attackers exploited a flaw in GL.iNet’s GoodCloud remote-management service to install persistent malware and reverse shells on routers and KVM devices, potentially exposing connected systems. GL.iNet has blocked the attack path and urges customers to update firmware, scan devices, preserve evidence, reset compromised systems, and rotate credentials.
KEY POINTS
  • 01GL.iNet confirmed that an unauthorized party exploited a vulnerability in its cloud remote-management services to access customer routers and KVM devices and install malicious software.
  • 02GL.iNet says it blocked the attack path and fixed the cloud vulnerability, but those measures do not remove malware from devices already infected.
  • 03Researchers and users reported persistent backdoors and root-level reverse shells on affected devices, including outbound connections to 170.39.226[.]219.
  • 04A malicious ni.sh script tailored for GL.iNet devices downloads payloads, creates persistence through startup scripts and scheduled tasks, and can hide connections by replacing the legitimate netstat utility.
  • 05GL.iNet recommends installing the latest security firmware, using its linked detection and cleanup tool, preserving evidence before resets, avoiding restoration of old configurations, and rotating exposed credentials or access keys.

GL.iNet confirmed that an unauthorized party exploited a vulnerability in its GoodCloud remote-management services to access customers’ routers and KVM devices and install malicious software. The company says it has fixed the cloud vulnerability and blocked the access path, preventing new infections through that route, but malware remains on devices that were already compromised. GL.iNet is investigating the incident, including whether computers connected to affected KVM devices were also compromised, and plans to notify identified customers through their cloud-account email addresses.

Users and researchers reported custom malware, persistent backdoors, and root-level remote command access on affected devices. A malicious ni.sh installer tailored to GL.iNet models downloads payloads, creates persistence through startup scripts and scheduled tasks, can install remote-access SSH keys, and replaces the legitimate netstat utility to conceal connections to a malicious server. RemoteToHome Consulting reported suspicious activity on 152 of 183 GL.iNet routers it analyzed, while some recovered malicious files had timestamps from December 2025, though the article says it is unclear whether those earlier infections were related.

GL.iNet recommends that customers install the latest security firmware and use the linked community-developed detection and cleanup tool, which checks for known files, processes, modified utilities, and persistence changes. The tool cannot identify every infection, and the developers warn that a firmware reinstall without retaining existing settings may be necessary because malicious configuration files can survive an ordinary update. Customers who suspect compromise are advised to preserve evidence before resetting, avoid restoring potentially infected configurations, rotate exposed credentials and access keys, and assess computers connected to affected KVM devices.

  • A normal firmware upgrade may not fully remove the infection because malicious files stored in device configuration directories can survive if existing settings are retained.
  • The malware can replace the legitimate netstat utility with a script that hides connections to a malicious server, making routine network checks less reliable.
  • In one analysis of 183 managed GL.iNet routers, 152 showed suspicious activity, including 24 with root-level reverse shells and 47 with an older persistent backdoor.
  • Because compromised KVM devices may provide access to the computers they control, GL.iNet advises that connected computers also be examined for compromise.
  • Some recovered malicious files had timestamps from December 2025, indicating that certain infections may have existed for months, although their connection to the November incident is unconfirmed.

Hackers used a weakness in GL.iNet’s online device-management service to place unwanted software on some customers’ internet routers and remote computer-control devices. The software could let attackers control those devices from afar, remain active after restarts, and possibly reach computers connected through the affected remote-control hardware. GL.iNet says it has closed the entry point, but affected customers still need to update their devices, check for infection, and change passwords or access keys that may have been exposed.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This reports active exploitation of GL.iNet’s GoodCloud service to install persistent malware and root-level reverse shells on customer routers and KVM devices. It is immediately actionable for affected owners because the cloud entry point was closed but already infected devices may retain malware and potentially expose connected systems.

Signal to block Siri, Gemini AI from capturing chats on iOS, Android

Signal is adding protections on iOS and Android to prevent Siri and Gemini AI assistants from reading or capturing private chats, extending similar defenses previously introduced for Microsoft Recall. The safeguards are automatic where supported, though iOS screenshot blocking may also prevent users from taking their own Signal screenshots.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Signal announced protections intended to block Siri on iOS 27 and AI assistants such as Gemini from capturing Signal conversations on supported Android devices.
  • 02Signal said the measures are designed to prevent operating-system AI features, including Siri’s Onscreen Awareness and Google’s Gemini Intelligence, from accessing chat content displayed on screen.
  • 03On iOS, users can check Settings > App Security to confirm that Block Siri AI is enabled, while Android enables Screen Security automatically when Signal detects an AI assistant attempting to access the screen.
  • 04Signal warned that the iOS protection also blocks users from taking screenshots of their own Signal conversations.
  • 05Signal said it currently cannot provide screenshot-blocking protection on macOS and recommends macOS 27 users add Signal to the Excluded Apps list in System Settings > Siri.

Signal announced new protections intended to stop Siri on iOS 27 and AI assistants such as Google Gemini on supported Android devices from capturing private conversations. Signal said the measures address operating-system features such as Siri’s Onscreen Awareness and Gemini Intelligence, which can access chat content displayed on screen. The company previously introduced a protection in May 2025 to block Microsoft Recall screenshots on Windows 11.

Signal said its engineers reverse engineered how AI systems access app data and designed protections to block verified extraction paths. On iOS, users can check Settings > App Security to ensure Block Siri AI is enabled, while Android automatically enables Screen Security when Signal detects an AI assistant attempting to access the screen. Signal warned that iOS protection also prevents users from taking their own Signal screenshots, and said it cannot currently provide screenshot-blocking protection on macOS; it recommends that macOS 27 users add Signal to Siri’s Excluded Apps list.

  • Signal’s protections are aimed at keeping chat content outside the reach of operating-system AI features, because those features can access what appears on screen rather than only data shared directly by the app.
  • The iOS safeguard has a practical privacy-versus-convenience tradeoff: blocking Siri’s access also prevents users from taking legitimate screenshots of Signal chats.
  • Signal says macOS currently has no equivalent screenshot-blocking protection, so macOS 27 users must manually exclude Signal from Siri in system settings.
  • Signal is urging Apple, Google, and Microsoft to document all ways AI systems can access screens, files, notifications, and app interfaces, and to provide developers with a clear opt-out mechanism.

Signal is adding safeguards meant to stop Apple’s Siri and Google’s Gemini from reading or recording messages shown in Signal chats on supported iPhones and Android phones. The protections turn on automatically when possible, but iPhone users may also be unable to take screenshots of their own Signal conversations. This matters because phone and computer assistants can be built deeply into the operating system, potentially giving them access to private conversations that Signal cannot otherwise control.

Researchers Demonstrate Ghostscript Sandbox-Escape RCE via Crafted File Uploads in Popular Apps

A patched Ghostscript use-after-free flaw (CVE-2026-125396) lets crafted uploaded documents bypass the -dSAFER sandbox and execute commands on servers through common rendering paths in apps such as WordPress, Roundcube, and ImageMagick. Organizations should update Ghostscript to 10.08.0 or later, including copies embedded in containers and application bundles.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Ghostscript flaw CVE-2026-125396 allowed crafted files to bypass its -dSAFER protections and run commands on the server processing them.
  • 02The issue was a use-after-free bug in Ghostscript's font-loading code, and Artifex fixed it in Ghostscript version 10.08.0 on September 8.
  • 03pwn.ai demonstrated that upload, preview, and print features in about ten applications could route crafted PostScript or EPS content to Ghostscript, including through image and document libraries.
  • 04Some tested paths required no login, including anonymous jobs sent to a shared Debian 13 print queue, Roundcube email attachments processed when opened, and public WordPress plugin upload forms.
  • 05Updating only the host operating system may not fix the exposure if an application or container includes its own older Ghostscript copy.

Researchers at pwn.ai demonstrated that CVE-2026-125396, a now-patched Ghostscript flaw, can let a crafted document escape Ghostscript’s -dSAFER security sandbox and run commands on the processing server. The vulnerability is a use-after-free in Ghostscript’s font-loading code. Artifex fixed the issue in Ghostscript 10.08.0 on September 8, and the article states there are no reports of exploitation in attacks.

The issue can be reached indirectly when applications use libraries such as ImageMagick, Pillow, libvips, or Qt plugins to render uploaded documents or images, including content named with .png, .jpg, or .pdf extensions. pwn.ai tested about ten applications and found reachable paths through web uploads, email attachments, and printing; some required no login, including a shared Debian 13 print queue, Roundcube attachments, and public WordPress plugin upload forms. Updating the host operating system alone may not be sufficient if an application or container image contains its own older Ghostscript copy, while controls such as authenticated print queues, restricted accepted file types, and AppArmor can reduce exposure until the renderer is updated.

  • A file does not need to be identified as PostScript or EPS to reach Ghostscript, because libraries may send that content to the renderer even when the file is named with .png, .jpg, or .pdf extensions.
  • Network-facing features turned a CVE classified as a local flaw into reachable server-side command execution paths through email attachments, print jobs, and web uploads.
  • Updating WordPress alone is insufficient where Ghostscript remains outdated, because the WordPress and Ghostscript fixes address separate links in the same attack chain.
  • Updating an operating system package may leave systems exposed when an application or container image carries its own older Ghostscript copy.
  • In one test, an AppArmor profile prevented the compromised Ghostscript process from starting a shell, showing that system-level restrictions can reduce impact before the renderer is updated.

Researchers found and helped fix a flaw in Ghostscript, a widely used program that turns uploaded documents and images into other formats. A specially made file could make a server run commands when services such as WordPress upload forms, Roundcube email, shared print queues, or image-processing tools try to preview or convert it. This matters because even ordinary features like creating a thumbnail can expose a server, so organizations need to update Ghostscript everywhere it is installed, including inside separate application packages or containers.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a high-impact patched remote command-execution path in widely used Ghostscript that can be reached through common upload, email, print, and image-processing workflows. There is no reported exploitation in the wild, but organizations may remain exposed through bundled or containerized older Ghostscript copies.

OpenRouter Drops 3 Providers After Researchers Catch Months of Prompt Theft at AI Hosting Firms

Dragos found that attackers and unscrupulous AI inference providers had been copying customer prompts, including Zero Data Retention requests, for months, exposing credentials and sensitive data. OpenRouter removed three providers and 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility while notifying potentially affected users.
KEY POINTS
  • 01Dragos seeded roughly two million AI prompts with unique links and credentials between July and November and found evidence that data from three AI inference providers had been copied for months.
  • 02Some copied requests were marked Zero Data Retention, a setting intended to ensure that prompts are not stored.
  • 03OpenRouter said it removed three providers from its platform, removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility, and notified potentially affected customers.
  • 04Researchers observed attackers using stolen planted AWS keys to list S3 buckets, search AWS Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, and attempt to create Bedrock API keys and IAM users.
  • 05Phala, Together AI, and Novita each publicly disclosed separate incidents involving exposure, storage, or unintended forwarding of customer AI requests.

Dragos said attackers copied data from inside three AI inference providers for months before researchers alerted them, including prompts marked Zero Data Retention. Its researchers seeded about two million prompts with unique web links and credentials between July and November, then monitored for use of those planted secrets. OpenRouter said its own systems were not breached, but it removed three providers from its platform, removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility, and notified potentially affected customers.

In the largest incident, planted AWS credentials sent to one provider in late October were used within hours to list S3 buckets, search AWS Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, and attempt to create Bedrock API keys and IAM users. Researchers said requests rejected before a model ran were also copied, suggesting the traffic was captured when it first arrived at the provider. Other findings involved low-priced marketplace sellers that enabled request logging, undisclosed routing to another marketplace, and a Chinese AI lab whose systems fetched secret links from more than half of seeded prompts sent to its models, including many marked with no-data-collection or Zero Data Retention settings.

Phala reported that an attacker controlled an inference server and could view requests being processed, while Together AI said an unauthorized party downloaded stored prompt samples after a credential was committed to its public GitHub repository. Novita said a routing configuration issue caused some MiniMax requests to be forwarded to MiniMax’s official API beyond what was intended. Dragos warned that privacy labels do not protect prompts if an attacker is already inside a provider or if requests are passed to additional companies, and OpenRouter said it is adapting the researchers’ testing approach for ongoing checks.

  • The fact that stolen prompts included requests rejected before any model ran indicates that at least one attacker was copying data at the provider's initial intake point rather than from the model-processing system.
  • A marketplace seller could collect customer prompts without breaking into a system simply by offering low-priced models while request logging was enabled.
  • Route tracing of about 2,000 paths found several hundred unidentified systems, and one seller was adding about 1,000 hidden tokens to every prompt.
  • A secret link from one prompt was posted in a Feishu chat 24 days later and opened from multiple devices, showing that a full cached prompt had been accessed by people after the original request.
  • OpenRouter said it is adapting the researchers' planted-secret testing approach for continuous checks and is reviewing network paths where requests or responses could be forwarded to third parties.

Researchers found that attackers and some AI service sellers were able to copy customer requests for months, including requests marked as not to be stored. Those requests can contain passwords, access keys, private code, and other sensitive information, and the stolen keys were used to try to access cloud accounts and data. OpenRouter removed three AI providers and changed which services can use its stricter privacy label, but the incidents show that a privacy setting cannot protect information if the company handling it has already been compromised or passes it to another company.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a high-impact supply-chain data exposure finding affecting AI inference providers and customers whose prompts may have contained cloud credentials, passwords, and private repositories. The reported misuse of stolen AWS keys and the removal of providers from OpenRouter make it broadly relevant to organizations using third-party AI routing or hosting services.

The Invoice That Told You Not to Use LinkedIn's Address

A fake LinkedIn invoice used the company’s real address but explicitly told accounts payable not to use it, directing payment instead to an ACH account whose “LinkedIn Corporation” name was not verified. The key lesson is to independently verify vendor bank details through trusted channels before paying any unexpected invoice.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A fake LinkedIn Billing invoice for $3,990 was sent to the accounting director of a global commercial real estate firm and requested payment by ACH transfer.
  • 02The invoice displayed LinkedIn’s real Sunnyvale, California address but twice instructed the recipient not to remit payment to that address.
  • 03The payment details named a real U.S. bank and listed the account name as “LinkedIn Corporation,” but the article states that ACH credits are generally routed by account and routing number without verifying the account holder’s name.
  • 04The sender used an unrelated Thai company domain and a display name matching the target firm’s chief executive, while the subject line contained the firm’s name inside unfinished-looking curly braces.
  • 05The accounting director reported the email as phishing, and a human analyst classified it as malicious and quarantined four copies in the reviewing mailbox.

A fake LinkedIn Billing invoice for USD 3,990 was sent to the accounting director of a global commercial real estate firm. It used LinkedIn’s real Sunnyvale, California headquarters address and tax ID, but twice instructed the recipient not to remit payment to that address. Instead, it directed payment to an ACH account at a U.S. bank with the account name “LinkedIn Corporation.”

The article highlights that an ACH account name is generally free text and is not normally verified against the actual account holder when a domestic credit is sent. The invoice therefore replaced a detail the recipient could independently check with bank instructions that could not be confirmed from the document itself. Other warning signs included an unrelated Thai sender domain, a display name matching the target company’s chief executive, an unfinished-looking subject template, no PO number, and dates showing the invoice was 107 days overdue.

The accounting director reported the email as phishing, after which a human analyst classified it as malicious and four copies were quarantined. The article states that it does not show whether anyone paid the invoice. It advises accounts-payable staff to confirm unexpected vendor payment details through a trusted contact method, such as a prior contract or the vendor’s billing portal, rather than using information in the invoice.

  • The invoice turned a common verification step against the recipient by printing LinkedIn’s real headquarters address and then instructing the recipient not to use it for payment.
  • An ACH account name such as “LinkedIn Corporation” is not proof of account ownership because domestic ACH credits are generally routed using the routing and account numbers rather than matching the stated name to the holder.
  • The fraud required no malicious link or attachment; its effective payload was the bank-payment instruction itself.
  • The repeated phrase telling recipients not to remit to the printed address is a useful detection clue because the article says legitimate remittance notices rarely instruct payers to distrust an address printed nearby.

A fake LinkedIn bill for $3,990 was sent to the accounting director of a global real estate firm, using LinkedIn’s real address but telling the recipient not to send payment there. Instead, it directed money to a bank account labeled “LinkedIn Corporation,” even though that label does not prove LinkedIn owns the account. The accounting director reported the message before payment was made, but similar scams can trick businesses into sending money to criminals, so invoices and bank details should be confirmed directly with the vendor through a trusted contact method.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

The article documents a specific invoice-fraud incident with a notable payment-redirection tactic: using a real vendor address while instructing recipients not to use it. It is broadly relevant to email-security and accounts-payable teams, but it does not identify an active campaign, named threat actor, or urgent technical remediation.

Top Ransomware Gangs & AI Pentesting Tools

Top ransomware affiliates have not broadly adopted autonomous AI pentesting frameworks because their proven access-broker model and manual Windows/ESXi tradecraft are stealthier, more reliable, and more cost-effective. AI is more likely being used quietly for operator assistance, while smaller crews absorb the expense and OPSEC risks of agentic tooling.
KEY POINTS
  • 01The article says established ransomware affiliates such as Akira, Qilin, and Rhysida have not been widely reported using autonomous AI offensive frameworks such as ARTEX, Cairn, or PentAGI.
  • 02Smaller or newer extortion groups, including WhiteArmor, Settra, and n0n, have been observed experimenting with AI tools, sometimes with poor operational security.
  • 03Top ransomware affiliates often purchase pre-vetted corporate access from initial access brokers and focus on internal actions such as moving through Windows domains, stealing data, and targeting VMware ESXi systems.
  • 04The article argues that cloud-based AI services can create logging, account-suspension, attribution, and cost risks, while locally hosting capable models requires expensive hardware and operational effort.
  • 05SOCRadar reported cases involving INC/Lynx and TheGentlemen affiliates using AI-based frameworks, with reported OpenRouter costs of $4,500 and $23,900 respectively.

The article examines why leading ransomware-as-a-service affiliates, including Akira, Qilin, and Rhysida, have not been widely reported using autonomous AI offensive frameworks such as ARTEX, Cairn, CyberStrikeAI, and PentAGI. Although reporting from Google, CrowdStrike, Microsoft, and the FBI has highlighted operational use of such tools, the author observes that their adoption appears concentrated among smaller or newer groups. WhiteArmor, Settra, and n0n are cited as examples of groups experimenting with AI-assisted operations. The article argues that this differs from the established ransomware ecosystem’s existing operational model.

Top affiliates often buy vetted corporate access from initial access brokers rather than performing external reconnaissance and initial intrusion themselves. Their work instead focuses on rapid internal activity, including Active Directory enumeration, lateral movement, privilege escalation, data theft, and targeting VMware ESXi environments. The article says current agentic tools are more focused on web exploitation and initial access, while introducing reliability, stealth, cost, logging, account-suspension, and attribution risks. SOCRadar nevertheless reported individual cases involving INC/Lynx and TheGentlemen affiliates using AI-based frameworks, with reported OpenRouter costs of \(4,500 and \)23,900, respectively.

  • The article highlights a mismatch between what current autonomous AI tools are strongest at—external reconnaissance and web application entry—and what top ransomware affiliates primarily need after they have already bought access: rapid movement within Windows and VMware ESXi environments.
  • AI use by ransomware operators may be difficult for defenders to observe when it is limited to local tasks such as writing scripts, obscuring code, creating phishing messages, or sorting stolen documents, rather than directing an autonomous intrusion.
  • The reported costs of AI-driven operations were substantial: SOCRadar observed one INC/Lynx affiliate spending about $4,500 through OpenRouter and an INC/TheGentlemen affiliate spending about $23,900.
  • The article presents the criminal ecosystem’s division of labor as a reason autonomous tools have limited appeal to established affiliates, because initial access brokers already supply corporate access that agentic tools might otherwise try to obtain.

The article argues that the biggest ransomware groups are not widely using AI tools to run break-ins automatically, even though smaller criminal groups are experimenting with them. Major groups often buy pre-existing access to company networks and rely on experienced people and proven tools to steal data and lock systems, including business servers. Automated AI tools can be expensive, unreliable, noisy, and may expose the criminals themselves, so they are not yet a clear advantage. This matters because businesses still face serious ransomware risks from established methods, while AI may gradually make attacks easier for smaller and less skilled groups.

OPEN SOURCE ↗

This is a trend and opinion analysis rather than reporting a single urgent incident or patch. It offers useful context on limited AI-framework adoption by ransomware affiliates and cites a few concrete cases, but it does not require immediate action.
