One RFQ Number, Two Real Suppliers, 17 Minutes Apart

Two legitimate aviation suppliers sent near-identical RFQ phishing emails from authenticated Microsoft 365 accounts, revealing a cross-sender campaign only when analysts correlated the shared reference number, template, and landing-page path. Organizations should detect vendor-email compromise by clustering message content and destinations across senders rather than relying solely on sender authentication or reputation.
KEY POINTS
  • 01A UK charter airline received two nearly identical RFQ emails numbered 68453 from two different aviation suppliers 17 minutes apart.
  • 02Both messages were sent from within the suppliers’ Microsoft 365 tenants, passed authentication at the airline’s gateway, and came from first-time senders.
  • 03Each email used the same template and a bold PDF-named link, while both links resolved to the same aviation-themed path on an unrelated web-design site.
  • 04Eight mailboxes across two airline domains received the messages, including a fleet support engineer and an engineering director.
  • 05A human analyst resolved both emails as malicious, and the copies were removed, although the record did not establish whether anyone clicked.

A UK charter airline received two nearly identical request-for-quotation emails, both using RFQ number 68453, from two different aviation suppliers 17 minutes apart. The messages originated from the suppliers’ own Microsoft 365 tenants, passed authentication at the airline’s gateway, and came from first-time senders. Eight mailboxes across two airline domains received the pair, including a fleet support engineer and an engineering director. A human analyst ultimately resolved both messages as malicious and the copies were removed.

Each email used the same one-line template and a bold, PDF-named link, View_RFQ68453_.pdf, rather than including an attachment. The subject line, Request for Quotation 68453_, and link text used a supplier-specific suffix, while both links led to the same /aerosupply/technic/aviation.html path on an unrelated web-design site. Considered separately, each message looked like an unusual but authenticated vendor email; considered together, the reused reference number, shared template, and identical destination path exposed a coordinated pattern. The article’s practical recommendation is to correlate inbound vendor messages by reference number, template shape, and destination path across senders, because sender authentication alone cannot establish that two legitimate accounts are being used independently.

  • Email authentication confirmed that each message came from the supplier account it claimed to use, but it did not establish that the supplier account was being used legitimately.
  • The campaign became apparent only by correlating content across unrelated senders: the reused RFQ number, matching template, and identical destination path provided the stable links between the emails.
  • The supplier-specific suffix added to the shared RFQ number and PDF-style link made each copy appear tailored while preserving a detectable template pattern.
  • Two first-time supplier emails could appear unremarkable when reviewed separately, but their arrival within 17 minutes and shared content created a materially different signal.

A UK charter airline received two nearly identical requests for quotation from two real aviation suppliers only 17 minutes apart, but both messages used the same fake PDF-style link to an unrelated website. The emails reached engineering employees who would be likely to open an aircraft-related quote request, and the suppliers’ real email accounts appear to have been used. This matters because an email can come from a legitimate company and still be dangerous, so comparing repeated wording, reference numbers, and web links across messages can reveal a coordinated scam that sender checks alone miss.

This is a concrete, resolved vendor-email compromise case with a useful detection lesson: authenticated emails from separate legitimate supplier tenants shared a lure fingerprint. It is relevant to email-security teams, but the incident is narrow, no click or downstream compromise was confirmed, and no urgent indicator beyond a partially redacted URL path is provided.
