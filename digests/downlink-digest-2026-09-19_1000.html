TAGSanthropicaslr-bypassclaude-opus-5cve-2026-32882debiandiscoursegithubgpt-5-6-solhacktronimagemagicklibheifopenairemote-code-executionsingle-sign-onunited-states

In plain words

Security researchers used Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5 to find a path from OpenAI’s public help forum into several OpenAI employees’ ChatGPT and Codex accounts, reaching an internal code repository without reading or changing its code. They reported the problem, OpenAI fixed its part within about 14 hours, and no customer data was touched. The case matters because a weakness in a public-facing service can become access to more sensitive company systems when both use the same sign-in, and old software used to process uploaded images can provide the first opening.

Summary
Hacktron researchers used Claude Opus 5 to chain an unpatched libheif image-processing flaw in OpenAI’s Discourse forum with an OpenAI SSO weakness, gaining controlled access to employee ChatGPT, Codex, and an internal GitHub repository. OpenAI fixed the identity issue and paid a $6,500 bounty, underscoring the risk of sharing SSO between public-facing services and sensitive internal tools.

Hacktron researchers used Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5 to chain flaws that let them take over the ChatGPT and Codex accounts of several OpenAI employees and reach an internal OpenAI GitHub repository. The researchers said this was controlled security research: they proved access with one harmless pull request, did not read source code or access customer data, and stopped afterward. OpenAI reportedly fixed the OpenAI-side issue about 14 hours after disclosure and paid Hacktron a $6,500 bounty. The award covered the OpenAI login finding, not testing of the Discourse forum, which was outside OpenAI’s bounty program.

The chain began with a specially crafted HEIC or HEIF image uploaded to OpenAI’s Discourse-based public forum, where an old libheif version used through ImageMagick could be manipulated to gain control of the forum server. Hacktron said the shared “Sign in with OpenAI” system then allowed access to staff accounts without victims taking any action, creating potential paths to connected services such as GitHub, Slack, and email. Although libheif 1.22.0 had fixed the issue in May 2026, the forum image running Debian 12 still used unpatched libheif 1.19.7 when the researchers tested it in July. The case highlights the risk of outdated image-processing software and of allowing a lower-trust public service to share sign-in access with sensitive internal tools.

  • A public forum compromise could affect OpenAI staff accounts without any action by the employees because the forum used the same OpenAI sign-in system as higher-value services.
  • The reported path to code execution depended on combining memory flaws to work around a protection that randomizes where software is placed in memory, even though the underlying public vulnerability record describes the issue more narrowly as an out-of-bounds read.
  • A security fix can be publicly available yet remain ineffective for deployed systems when an operating system’s packaged version has not incorporated it, as the forum image still contained libheif 1.19.7 after libheif 1.22.0 was released.
  • Hacktron said Claude Opus 5 reduced the time needed to create a working exploit from repeated unsuccessful sessions to hours, while the wider HEIF Heist project reportedly cost under $3,000 in AI usage.
Key Points
  • 01Hacktron researchers chained an image-processing flaw in OpenAI’s Discourse-based public forum with an OpenAI login weakness to take over the ChatGPT and Codex accounts of several OpenAI employees.
  • 02The researchers demonstrated internal GitHub repository access by triggering a single harmless pull request and said they did not read source code, merge or ship changes, or access customer data.
  • 03Hacktron reported the findings to OpenAI, which reportedly fixed the OpenAI-side issue about 14 hours later and paid the team a $6,500 bounty on September 1.
  • 04The forum server used unpatched libheif 1.19.7 on Debian 12 even though libheif 1.22.0, which fixed the relevant issue, had been released in May 2026.
  • 05Hacktron said Claude Opus 5 produced a working exploit within hours after Claude Opus 4.8 had struggled to do so with ASLR enabled.

ShinyHunters breached and defaced the Tor-based data leak site operated by the Clop ransomware group. In plain words

A scam email pretending to come from a national tax authority was sent to a senior employee at an international advertising group and included a PDF that looked harmless and nearly empty. Hidden inside the file was a record saying it was created with ChatGPT, even though the visible document details were blank and the email falsely claimed the file needed a password. The email’s download link led toward an attacker-controlled website, showing how criminals can use convincing government branding and ordinary-looking attachments to try to steal information. This matters because common email checks can miss these scams, and people and organizations may need to look beyond a file’s visible details before trusting it.

Summary
A phishing email impersonating a national tax authority used a seemingly blank PDF whose ordinary metadata was anonymous, but a hidden C2PA provenance manifest identified ChatGPT/gpt-5-5 and an OpenAI certificate chain. The incident shows that attachment triage should inspect signed provenance metadata as well as conventional PDF properties, especially when DMARC passes through DKIM-only shared-hosting mail.

A phishing email impersonating a national tax authority was sent to a senior advertising-operations lead at an international advertising group. The message used a one-page, 8,692-byte PDF that appeared harmless in ordinary checks: it had no JavaScript, links, automatic actions, or meaningful visible document properties. It falsely claimed the attachment was password protected and instructed the recipient to use “2026,” although the file was not encrypted.

The PDF contained a compressed C2PA provenance manifest that recorded c2pa.created, classified the document as trained-algorithmic media, named gpt-5-5 and ChatGPT, and included an X.509 certificate chain whose leaf subject was OpenAI OpCo, LLC. The article states that the manifest was not cryptographically validated and was likely invalidated when the file was re-saved 25 days after signing, but its readable generator information remained embedded because it was not stripped. The email passed DMARC through aligned DKIM from an unrelated shared-hosting sender domain with no SPF record, while its only download link redirected to an attacker-controlled .info domain behind a bot-verification interstitial.

  • The PDF’s ordinary metadata was anonymous and untitled, while its compressed provenance data retained generator details, showing that visible file properties can be far less informative than embedded provenance records.
  • The provenance record was likely no longer valid after the file was re-saved, but its readable contents remained in the document because the manifest was not stripped.
  • The attachment’s claims contradicted observable facts: it said the PDF was password protected and contained a sharing link, yet the file was unencrypted and contained no such link.
  • The email passed DMARC not because it represented the impersonated tax authority, but because the unrelated sending domain had aligned DKIM and a DMARC policy of p=NONE despite publishing no SPF record.
  • The article recommends hunting for inbound messages that pass DMARC with spf=none, a narrower signal than broadly investigating all authentication failures.

Summary
Talking Tilly, an AI video-call service tied to viral AI actress Tilly Norwood, requires a facial age-estimation check and continuously analyzes callers’ video and voice for emotional cues. Calls are recorded and processed by third parties, while transcripts and conversational memory may be retained, highlighting privacy implications of AI companion-style services and UK-driven age-verification rules.

Talking Tilly, a video-call service for AI character Tilly Norwood run by UK company Xicoia, requires every caller worldwide to pass an automated age check before the first call. Didit analyzes a video selfie to estimate the caller’s age, with government photo ID as a fallback if the estimate is unclear. Xicoia says selfies are sent directly to Didit, no biometric template is created, and the company retains only an approximate age band and reference number after the check.

During calls, the service analyzes callers’ camera feeds and voice tone to infer their emotional state, and this analysis cannot be disabled for an individual call. Calls are recorded, transcribed, and processed live by U.S. providers; transcripts can be retained for up to eight weeks, while conversation memory can be deleted on request. The article notes that the service’s age gate and mood analysis rely on legitimate interests rather than consent, and that a UK regulatory direction may have resulted in face-based checks being imposed on callers globally.

  • A UK-driven age-verification decision applies to every Talking Tilly caller worldwide, meaning users outside the UK must also complete the face-based age check.
  • Xicoia’s policy uses “legitimate interests,” rather than consent, as the legal basis for both age checking and mood analysis, even though the mood analysis cannot be disabled for an individual call.
  • The service’s automated abuse classifier withheld a weather-and-news conversation for alleged hateful or abusive language, showing that benign calls can be incorrectly flagged and made unavailable unless a human reviewer releases them.
  • Purchased call time is unusually time-limited: all free and paid minutes expire when the service closes permanently on September 27, and unused minutes are forfeited.
