Attackers Use Passkey Phishing to Hijack Microsoft Cloud Accounts and Exfiltrate Data

In plain words

Microsoft says criminals sent more than a million fake payment emails that appeared to come from company leaders, hoping finance staff would send money for a nonexistent ServiceNow subscription. In a separate effort, callers and text messages pretending to be IT support persuaded employees to update their account security, then used fake Microsoft sign-in pages or approval requests to take over cloud accounts. The victims include U.S. businesses in IT services, consumer goods, real estate, and manufacturing, and the criminals can then read email, download company files, and keep access by adding their own phone number or authentication app to the account.

Summary
Microsoft warns that attackers are using AI-assisted executive invoice scams and passkey-themed phishing/vishing to compromise Microsoft cloud accounts. The cloud intrusions establish attacker-controlled MFA methods and then abuse Microsoft Graph to persist, reconnoiter tenants, and exfiltrate SharePoint, OneDrive, and email data.

Microsoft described two campaigns: an AI-assisted invoice fraud effort and a passkey-themed social-engineering campaign targeting cloud accounts. From August 3 to 5, 2026, attackers sent more than one million emails impersonating company CEOs and sought ACH payments for a fake ServiceNow annual subscription. The messages used trusted third-party delivery infrastructure, forged invoices and email threads, spoofed domains, and recipient-specific executive names to make the payment requests appear legitimate.

Since May 2026, another campaign has used calls and texts posing as IT help desk staff to pressure users into updating passkeys, multi-factor authentication, or single sign-on settings. Victims were directed to counterfeit Microsoft sign-in sites or device-code authentication flows, enabling attackers to capture credentials or obtain account access through an approval process. After access, attackers registered their own phone numbers, authenticator apps, or software one-time-password tokens, then used Microsoft Graph to conduct reconnaissance, collect mailbox data, and download files from SharePoint Online and OneDrive for Business.

Microsoft linked the observed initial-access activity to several actors, including Storm-3121 and Storm-3032, the latter of which it identifies as UNC6671. The actors used separate IP addresses for authentication, reconnaissance, and data collection, while sustained file and email exfiltration could continue for hours or days. Microsoft said this behavior is difficult to detect from isolated Graph API calls and should instead be assessed through the broader sequence of sign-ins, new authentication methods, reconnaissance, and high-volume downloads.

  • The invoice fraud used a layered narrative—executive impersonation, vendor branding, fabricated invoices, and forged supporting conversations—rather than relying on a single deceptive message.
  • The passkey-themed lures can enable account takeover through device-code approval flows, allowing attackers to gain control without stealing a victim's password or browser session and bypassing MFA safeguards.
  • Attackers used victim-specific subdomains on passkey- and SSO-themed domains, such as "<company name>.<malicious domain>.com," to make targeted phone-based phishing more convincing.
  • Microsoft cautioned that individual Microsoft Graph requests may not look suspicious, so detecting this activity requires correlating the progression from unusual sign-ins and new authentication methods to reconnaissance and high-volume data downloads.
  • The actors rotated infrastructure and used different IP addresses for sign-in, reconnaissance, and data theft, reducing the usefulness of isolated network-based indicators.
Key Points
  • 01Microsoft reported that attackers sent more than one million CEO-impersonation payment scam emails from August 3 to 5, 2026, seeking ACH transfers for a fake ServiceNow annual subscription.
  • 02The invoice-fraud campaign used trusted third-party email delivery infrastructure, forged invoices and email threads, impersonation domains, and AI-assisted recipient-tailored email templates.
  • 03Since May 2026, a separate campaign has used calls, texts, and counterfeit Microsoft sign-in sites with passkey, MFA, and SSO update lures to compromise cloud accounts.
  • 04After gaining access, attackers registered authentication methods they controlled, such as phone numbers, authenticator apps, or software one-time-password tokens, to maintain access to victim accounts.
  • 05Microsoft observed the compromised accounts being used for Microsoft Graph reconnaissance, mailbox collection, and high-volume downloads from SharePoint Online and OneDrive for Business.

The W-9 Was Real. That Was the Point.
ironscales.com · 13 Sep 2026 11:00 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra
TAGSbrand-impersonationcloudflaredmarcironscalesirs-form-w-9linkedinmicrosoft-365spearphishing-attachmentthemisunited-states

In plain words

A scammer sent a fake $31,471 LinkedIn invoice to the payment mailbox of a pharmaceutical manufacturing company and included a real, signed LinkedIn tax form to make it look trustworthy. The tax form contained genuine company details but did not prove that the person sending the email worked for LinkedIn. The email was eventually identified as malicious and no money was reported lost, but it shows how criminals can use real documents to trick companies into starting a payment conversation. Businesses need to confirm payment requests by contacting vendors through phone numbers or contacts they already trust, not information supplied in an unexpected email.

Summary
A fraudulent LinkedIn invoice used a genuine, signed IRS W-9 to make the scam appear legitimate, demonstrating that authentic vendor paperwork does not verify the identity of the sender. Organizations should confirm payment requests and remittance changes through an out-of-band contact method using trusted vendor records.

A fraudulent email sent to the accounts-payable mailbox of a pharmaceutical CDMO’s specialty-chemistry subsidiary included a $31,471.35 invoice impersonating LinkedIn Corporation. It was accompanied by a completed, authentic IRS Form W-9 bearing LinkedIn’s real tax number, California address, and a January 2025 signature. The invoice was fabricated, but the genuine tax form made the request appear credible despite proving nothing about whether the sender was authorized to seek payment.

Neither attachment contained malicious code or banking information; instead, the invoice repeatedly sought a reply or confirmation so the attacker could continue the payment conversation. The message used a forged internal forward, an invented vendor persona on a newly registered LinkedIn lookalike domain, a separate newly registered reply domain, and a legitimate company’s authenticated transactional sending path that passed DMARC. Microsoft 365 initially quarantined the email for user impersonation before it was released roughly eighteen hours later, and IRONSCALES later classified it as malicious; the article reports no reply and no funds lost. The article’s central recommendation is to verify first payments and remittance changes through an out-of-band call to contact information already held for the vendor, rather than relying on paperwork or email-provided details.

  • A genuine vendor tax form can validate the company details printed on it while providing no proof that the person emailing it is authorized to request payment.
  • The absence of banking details was deliberate: the invoice repeatedly sought a reply, making the immediate objective to establish a conversation before sending payment instructions.
  • File scanning produced defensible but misleading results because the fabricated invoice was clean and the authentic W-9 was inert; the fraud depended on the relationship between the documents and the email’s claim.
  • The message passed DMARC through a legitimate company’s authenticated transactional sending path, showing that authentication success alone did not establish that the request was trustworthy.
  • A quarantine release can negate an earlier impersonation detection, so payment controls must remain effective after a suspicious message reaches a mailbox.
Key Points
  • 01A fake LinkedIn invoice for $31,471.35 was sent to the accounts-payable mailbox of a global pharmaceutical CDMO’s specialty-chemistry subsidiary alongside an authentic, completed LinkedIn IRS Form W-9.
  • 02The W-9 contained LinkedIn’s genuine federal tax number and California corporate address, was signed in January 2025, and contained no active content such as JavaScript, embedded files, or automatic actions.
  • 03The fabricated invoice used real LinkedIn product lines and genuine company details but contained no bank or routing information, instead seeking a reply or confirmation to begin a payment conversation.
  • 04Microsoft 365 quarantined the message as user impersonation before it was released about eighteen hours later, and IRONSCALES analysts ultimately classified it as confirmed malicious.
  • 05The article recommends verifying first payments or changes to payment details through an out-of-band callback using vendor contact information the organization already holds.
