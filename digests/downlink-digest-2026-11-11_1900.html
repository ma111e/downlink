Active ScreenConnect attacks breach MSPs, impact dozens of organizations

In plain words

Attackers have taken control of some companies’ ScreenConnect remote-support systems and used them to install their own remote-control software on customer computers. Huntress found six affected IT service providers, with at least 286 computers across 47 organizations involved, and says the total may grow. This matters because the attackers could gain access to many businesses through the outside companies trusted to manage their technology, and they may also be able to target the support workers’ own computers.

Summary
Attackers are compromising MSP ScreenConnect environments to push rogue remote-access clients to customer devices, affecting at least six MSPs and 286 endpoints across 47 organizations. ConnectWise and Huntress urge partners to avoid interactive sessions with suspected compromised devices, review Run Command history, revoke sessions, reset credentials, and check for listed indicators of compromise.

ConnectWise is investigating an active campaign targeting managed service providers that uses compromised cloud-hosted ScreenConnect environments to deploy rogue remote-access clients to customer devices. Huntress reported identifying six compromised MSPs and at least 286 affected endpoints across 47 organizations, with totals continuing to rise. The campaign began around 17:47 UTC on the day Huntress reported it.

Attackers are using ScreenConnect’s Run Command feature to install clients that provide access outside the legitimate MSP environment. Huntress also warned that a technician’s workstation could potentially be compromised when the technician opens a ScreenConnect session with an infected customer device, though the mechanism is unclear. ConnectWise advised partners to avoid interactive sessions with suspected compromised devices, review account activity and Run Command history, revoke active sessions, reset credentials from a trusted device, verify multifactor authentication, and check for the published indicators of compromise.

Huntress said its telemetry currently points to compromised credentials and hijacked MSP support sessions, but the initial access method has not been determined. Sequential attacker-assigned tags suggest that other MSPs may have been compromised earlier. BleepingComputer also found a July 2026 malicious batch script using one rogue ScreenConnect client ID, although it remains unclear whether that earlier activity is part of the current campaign.

  • The reported risk extends beyond customer devices: Huntress warned that a technician’s own workstation could be compromised when the technician starts a ScreenConnect session with an infected customer device.
  • Sequential attacker-assigned tags indicate that additional managed service providers may have been compromised before the campaign was detected, so Huntress recommends reviewing Run Command history even when no alert was received.
  • One rogue ScreenConnect client ID appeared in a malicious Social Security-themed batch script uploaded to VirusTotal in July 2026, showing that the identifier was associated with earlier malware activity.
  • The earlier script attempted to weaken Microsoft Defender protections, establish automatic startup persistence, download installers, and notify attackers through Telegram after confirming installation.
Key Points
  • 01Huntress identified an active campaign in which compromised cloud-hosted ScreenConnect environments at managed service providers were used to deploy rogue ScreenConnect clients to customer devices.
  • 02As of Huntress’s report, the campaign had affected six managed service providers and at least 286 endpoints across 47 organizations, with the number continuing to rise.
  • 03The attackers used ScreenConnect’s Run Command feature to install remote-access clients outside the legitimate managed service provider’s ScreenConnect environment.
  • 04ConnectWise said it was investigating the malicious activity and advised partners to avoid interactive sessions with suspected compromised devices, review account activity, revoke active sessions, reset credentials from a trusted device, and verify multifactor authentication.
  • 05Huntress said its telemetry pointed to compromised credentials and hijacked support sessions, but it had not determined how the attackers initially accessed the affected managed service provider environments.

eMudhra revokes 8 more CA certificates after private key mishap
bleepingcomputer.com · 11 Nov 2026 21:27 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra

In plain words

eMudhra, a company that helps verify secure websites and software, canceled eight more digital certificates after finding its process may have handled secret keys unsafely. This brings the total to 16 affected certificates, including eight whose secret keys were so weak that they could be guessed almost immediately. Major browser, phone, and computer software makers blocked the affected certificates because criminals could otherwise impersonate real websites or software and potentially spy on supposedly private connections. eMudhra says it found no evidence of misuse but will stop issuing publicly trusted website certificates under its remaining system.

Summary
eMudhra revoked eight additional intermediate CA certificates after a faulty key-transfer script exposed RSA private-key material in host memory, bringing the total affected intermediates to 16. The incident prompted major vendors to block the compromised trust chains, and eMudhra plans to exit the publicly trusted TLS certificate business.

eMudhra revoked eight additional RSA intermediate certificate authority certificates after its investigation found that a custom Python script handled their private-key information in the memory of the computer running it. The revocations bring the total affected intermediates to 16, including eight ECDSA certificates whose private keys were reduced to one byte and could be recovered by trying 256 possible values. The script had been used in April 2025 to transfer certificate authority keys between hardware security modules from different vendors. eMudhra said the RSA keys were not weakened in the same way, but it could not show that they had been handled in accordance with its certificate security policy.

Some of the affected RSA intermediates had issued TLS and code-signing certificates since May 2026, with the last certificate issued on November 3, and eMudhra said affected customer certificates were replaced or migrated. The company also found that a June 2025 issue involving revocation lists signed by nonmatching keys could have exposed the flawed process earlier, but it corrected the immediate issue without investigating the cause. Mozilla, Google, Apple, and Microsoft released protections to block the compromised eMudhra certificate chains because the recoverable keys could have enabled forged certificates for legitimate websites. eMudhra says it has found no evidence of malicious use, plans to leave the publicly trusted TLS certificate business, and commissioned a KPMG review of its key-management procedures.

  • eMudhra revoked the additional RSA intermediates not because they were shown to be weak, but because it could not demonstrate that their private keys had remained protected as required by its own certificate security policy.
  • A June 2025 mismatch between certificate revocation lists and their corresponding certificate keys was an early indicator of the faulty process, but eMudhra fixed the immediate symptom without investigating its cause.
  • The flawed transfer process affected both key types differently: eight ECDSA keys became trivially recoverable, while eight RSA keys were exposed to handling in ordinary computer memory.
  • Some affected RSA intermediates continued issuing certificates from May 2026 until as late as November 3, before their November 7 revocation.
  • The investigation found that eMudhra lacked checks to verify generated private-key strength and to confirm that signing keys matched those originally created in its secure hardware.
Key Points
  • 01eMudhra revoked eight additional RSA intermediate certificate authority certificates after finding that a custom Python key-transfer script processed private-key material in the host computer's memory rather than solely inside secure hardware.
  • 02The additional revocations bring the total number of affected eMudhra intermediate certificates to 16, including eight ECDSA certificates whose private keys were reduced to one byte and could be recovered by testing 256 values.
  • 03The flawed script was used in April 2025 to transfer certificate authority keys between hardware security module vendors after delays obtaining new hardware.
  • 04Mozilla, Google, Apple, and Microsoft released protections to block trust in the compromised eMudhra certificate chains.
  • 05eMudhra plans to leave the publicly trusted TLS certificate business, stop issuing under its remaining G1 hierarchy by November 16, and has commissioned a KPMG review expected by December 4.

JUSTIFICATION

This is a significant certificate-authority incident affecting 16 eMudhra intermediate certificates, including keys that could be recovered almost instantly and potentially used to forge trusted website certificates. Major platform vendors have blocked the affected trust chains, making it broadly relevant to organizations that rely on public TLS or code-signing certificates, although eMudhra reports no confirmed malicious use.

In plain words

Notion says that a break-in at Fireworks AI, a company it used to help run some of its AI features, exposed some Notion AI chat records. The affected records came from Notion’s Insights feature, and fewer than 1% of Notion users are believed to be affected. This matters because people may have entered passwords, API keys, or other sensitive information into those chats, so affected users should change any such information they shared.

Summary
Notion says a breach at AI vendor Fireworks AI exposed Notion AI chat transcripts from its Insights dataset for less than 1% of users. Affected users should review incident notices and rotate any passwords, API keys, or credentials entered in those chats.

Notion disclosed that a breach at its AI infrastructure provider, Fireworks AI, exposed Notion AI chat transcripts from a small number of workspaces. Fireworks said an attacker accessed its development environment through a third-party cloud provider on October 23, compromising resource metadata, billing information, model weights, and datasets for some customers. Notion learned on November 2 of an exposed dataset that Fireworks initially said contained only synthetic test data, but Fireworks confirmed on November 8 that customer data, including Notion user data, had been accessed.

The exposed transcripts came from the dataset for Notion’s Insights feature, which helps workspace administrators understand how their teams use AI and has now been disabled. Notion says fewer than 1% of its users were affected, and that most impacted transcripts were not labeled with their author or source workspace and did not include tool-call results. After the initial alert, Notion cut off traffic to Fireworks, deleted its data from the vendor’s systems, and halted training runs by November 3. Affected users are advised to review the available incident information and rotate any passwords, API keys, or other credentials entered into affected chats.

  • Fireworks AI initially told Notion that the exposed dataset contained only synthetic test data, but later confirmed that customer data, including Notion user data, had been accessed.
  • The affected transcripts were largely disconnected from specific users and workspaces and did not include tool-call results, limiting the information included in most impacted records.
  • The incident shows that Notion AI’s Insights feature involved storing and analyzing conversations through Fireworks AI, despite Fireworks having contractually committed to zero retention of Notion data.
  • Notion provides affected workspace owners a way to review the relevant data and share a download link with individual members, making it possible for users to identify chats that may have contained credentials.
Key Points
  • 01Notion disclosed that a security breach at its AI infrastructure provider, Fireworks AI, exposed Notion AI chat transcripts from a small number of workspaces.
  • 02The exposed transcripts came from the dataset used for Notion’s Insights feature, which lets workspace administrators view how their teams use AI and has now been disabled.
  • 03Notion stopped sending traffic to Fireworks AI, deleted its data from the vendor’s systems, and halted training runs by November 3 after being alerted to an exposed dataset.

  • 05Notion advises affected users to rotate passwords, API keys, and other credentials that they entered into affected chats.

Log Analysis Field Guide: Tips for Spotting Patterns and Red Flags
blackhillsinfosec.com · 11 Nov 2026 21:57 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra
TAGSchinaevtx-attack-samplesmitre-attackmordor-projectnew-yorkultimate-windows-security

In plain words

This article is a beginner’s guide to reading the automatic records that computers and applications create when people sign in, download files, or make connections. It explains how to notice warning signs such as many failed password attempts, unusual late-night activity, or a person appearing to log in from far-apart places within minutes. This matters to anyone whose workplace or personal accounts use computers, because these records can help find unauthorized access or data being sent to unknown places before the problem becomes much larger.

Summary
This guide explains how to analyze logs by focusing on timestamps, repetition, normal behavior baselines, and targeted filtering to identify suspicious activity. Key red flags include repeated failed logins followed by success, after-hours access, impossible travel, unexpected account changes, and unusual processes or network connections.

Log analysis involves reviewing automatically generated records of logins, file downloads, network connections, and other system activity to determine who did what, when, and where. Common log fields include timestamps, source systems, event types, users or processes, and success or failure outcomes. The article emphasizes that analysts do not need to understand every entry, but should learn to identify meaningful patterns.

Analysts should establish what normal activity looks like, use filters to focus on relevant events, and document their searches. Important warning signs include repeated failed logins followed by success, unexpected after-hours access, impossible-travel indicators, account or permission changes, and unusual processes or outbound connections. Impossible-travel findings should be verified against source logs because VPN endpoints, cloud proxies, and mobile-device synchronization can produce false positives.

  • A single failed login may be a typing mistake, but ten failed attempts within two minutes are a pattern worth investigating.
  • An impossible-travel alert can indicate compromised credentials, but VPN endpoints, cloud proxies, and mobile device synchronization can also create false positives, so the source logs should be verified.
  • Failed events can be more informative than successful ones because a sequence of failed attempts followed by success may show that someone guessed a password.
  • Establishing normal behavior, such as typical transfer volumes or scheduled maintenance access, makes unusual activity easier to identify and reduces unnecessary escalation.
  • Documenting searches lets an analyst retrace the investigation rather than relying on memory.
Key Points
  • 01Logs record digital activity such as logins, file downloads, and network connections, helping answer who performed an action, what happened, when it happened, and where it originated.
  • 02Core log elements commonly include a timestamp, source system, event type, user or process, and whether the activity succeeded or failed.
  • 03Analysts should look for repetition, suspicious timing, and behavior that differs from normal activity, while using filters to focus on relevant entries.
  • 04Red flags include repeated failed logins followed by a successful login, unexpected after-hours activity, impossible travel indicators, modified accounts, and unusual processes or connections.
  • 05The article recommends documenting searches, checking whether activity has occurred before, comparing it with known bad behavior, and deciding whether escalation is needed.
OPEN SOURCE ↗
JUSTIFICATION

This is evergreen introductory guidance on log analysis rather than reporting a specific incident, threat campaign, or urgent vulnerability. It is useful for newcomers learning to recognize common warning signs, but it offers no time-sensitive or novel operational information.
