In plain words

Researchers found three related spying campaigns that targeted telecommunications companies, government agencies, military organizations, research groups, and other essential services in Latin America, Asia, and Europe. The attackers used fake website security warnings, compromised internet-connected computers, and deceptive files to trick people or gain entry, then installed tools that could steal passwords, monitor activity, copy files, and move through networks. The activity is linked to CopperDragon, which researchers believe may supply its software to several China-linked groups. This matters because access to telecom and government systems can expose sensitive information and provide long-term hidden access to organizations that people rely on.

Summary
Check Point Research links three espionage clusters targeting telecoms, governments, critical infrastructure, and research organizations across Latin America, Asia, and Europe to CopperDragon, a suspected supplier of the Sparrow malware framework to China-nexus actors. The campaigns use distinct access methods and implants—including watering holes, compromised Linux servers, SquadC, SparrowDoor/SparrowShell, CopperDoor, and CopperDNS—to enable credential theft, persistence, lateral movement, and covert command-and-control.

Check Point Research identified three linked intrusion clusters—SilentDelivery, OceanGate, and DarkWire—targeting telecommunications providers, governments, military entities, research institutions, and critical infrastructure across Latin America, Asia, and Europe. The company tracks the shared malware ecosystem as CopperDragon, which it assesses may be a developer and supplier of the cross-platform Sparrow framework to several China-nexus actors, including FamousSparrow, Earth Estries, TropicTrooper, and UAT-9244. The report emphasizes that shared malware or command-and-control infrastructure does not necessarily mean the same actor conducted every intrusion.

SilentDelivery used compromised websites to show selected visitors fake Chrome certificate warnings and deliver SquadC or SparrowDoor through fraudulent certificate-repair installers. OceanGate began by compromising internet-facing Linux servers, deployed AnnobinDoor and SparrowShell, captured credentials through trojanized SSH tools, and then moved into Windows environments to deploy tools for remote access, reconnaissance, keylogging, and credential theft. DarkWire focused on Latin American telecommunications and government targets, using CopperDoor and the DNS-tunneling CopperDNS backdoors, with operators also using DLL sideloading, proxy tools, LSASS dumping, SMB file transfers, and WMI-based remote execution.

  • SilentDelivery’s injected website code selectively controlled whether victims saw the fake certificate warning by checking in with its command server, and it stopped showing the warning after the installed backdoor reported a successful compromise.
  • SquadC can avoid automated analysis by refusing to run unless a common web browser is open, matching the expected condition of a victim who has just downloaded it through a browser.
  • Some SparrowDoor versions can conceal their communications by intercepting traffic sent to a legitimate service such as port 443, redirecting only connections from a specified source IP address to a hidden internal port, and forwarding all other traffic normally.
  • OceanGate operators replaced legitimate SSH client and server components with altered versions that captured administrator credentials, allowing them to move from one Linux system to others using harvested passwords.
  • DarkWire command servers were often located in Latin America, sometimes in the same country as the targeted organization, which the report says may help malicious traffic blend in with normal network activity.
Key Points
  • 01Check Point Research identified three operational clusters—SilentDelivery, OceanGate, and DarkWire—linked through shared infrastructure features and malware-code similarities in the Sparrow framework.
  • 02SilentDelivery used selective compromised-website campaigns in Europe and Asia to display fake Chrome certificate warnings and deliver SquadC or SparrowDoor through fraudulent certificate-repair installers.
  • 03OceanGate targeted government organizations and critical infrastructure primarily in Asia, initially compromising internet-facing Linux systems before moving into Windows environments for credential theft, lateral movement, and data collection.
  • 04DarkWire targeted telecommunications providers and government organizations, initially concentrated in Latin America, using CopperDoor and the DNS-tunneling CopperDNS backdoors.
  • 05Check Point tracks CopperDragon as a possible developer and supplier of the cross-platform Sparrow framework used by several China-nexus threat actors, including FamousSparrow, Earth Estries, TropicTrooper, and UAT-9244.

In plain words

Researchers say a Russian government-linked hacking group secretly broke into government email systems in Romania and Kazakhstan and used a new set of tools to copy selected messages. In Romania, the stolen emails included exchanges with NATO and European Union institutions, while the hackers hid their activity inside the victims’ own email servers. This matters because government and diplomatic communications can contain sensitive information, and the method made the spying harder to notice or shut down.

Summary
ESET says Russia-linked Turla hackers compromised government Microsoft Exchange servers in Romania and Kazakhstan using a new stealthy toolkit called Cereal to intercept sensitive emails, including NATO and EU correspondence. The group used the victims’ own Exchange infrastructure as a command hub, making the espionage campaign harder to detect and disrupt.

ESET researchers attributed a campaign against government email servers in Romania and Kazakhstan to Turla, a Russian state-linked espionage group associated with the FSB. The group used a previously unknown toolkit called Cereal to monitor email traffic, steal selected messages, and retain access to compromised Microsoft Exchange servers. ESET first found the toolkit on a Kazakh government server in November 2025 and later identified it at a Romanian government organization.

In Romania, the malware was configured to collect correspondence with NATO and European Union institutions. A component called CerealBox was embedded in an Exchange service that processes email traffic, enabling the attackers to identify and capture messages as they passed through the server. Rather than using external command servers, the attackers used the compromised Exchange servers as operational hubs, a method that reduced their exposure to detection or disruption; ESET could not determine how access was initially obtained or how much data was taken.

  • Turla avoided using separate command servers by turning compromised Exchange servers into its own operational control hubs, a technique ESET described as rare among advanced threat groups.
  • CerealBox was embedded in a legitimate Exchange service that processes email traffic, allowing the attackers to identify messages of interest as they passed through the server.
  • ESET said the method used by CerealBox had not previously been documented in other malware families, indicating detailed knowledge of Microsoft Exchange infrastructure.
  • Approximately 70% of countries targeted by Turla since 2022 have been former Soviet republics or European countries with historical ties to the Soviet Union.
Key Points
  • 01ESET attributed a campaign targeting government Microsoft Exchange servers in Romania and Kazakhstan to Turla, a Russian state-linked espionage group associated with the FSB.
  • 02The attackers used a previously unknown toolkit called Cereal, including a component named CerealBox, to monitor email traffic and capture selected messages.
  • 03In Romania, the malware was configured to collect emails exchanged with NATO and European Union institutions.
  • 04The attackers used compromised Exchange servers themselves to issue commands and manage the operation, reducing reliance on external infrastructure.
  • 05ESET said it could not determine how the attackers initially accessed the government servers or how much information they obtained.

In plain words

Binance warned that a newly published method could let a harmful iPhone app look at files belonging to other apps on phones running iOS 26 or 27. This could expose cryptocurrency wallet recovery phrases, private keys, and account sign-in details, especially for people who install apps from outside Apple’s official App Store. Apple’s newest updates do not say that they fix the issue, and no theft linked to it has been reported, but anyone who suspects exposure should create a new wallet on a clean device and sign out of active accounts.

Summary
Binance warned that the publicly released worse_query iOS sandbox escape may let malicious sideloaded apps access other apps’ data, potentially exposing crypto wallet seed phrases, private keys, and credentials on iOS 26 and 27. Apple’s latest updates do not confirm a fix, so users should remove untrusted apps and move funds to a new wallet if compromise is suspected.

Binance warned that a publicly released iOS sandbox escape called worse_query could allow an app to read files from other apps on iPhones running iOS 26 and 27. The exchange said this could expose cryptocurrency wallet seed phrases, private keys, and login credentials to malicious apps. No attacks or thefts involving the issue have been reported.

Researcher forcequit released the proof-of-concept code on GitHub, where it is described as requiring an app to set up an App Group container. Developers have used it to create WQ Files, a sideloaded file manager that can access, edit, delete, and clear other apps’ data without a jailbreak or kernel exploit. Apple’s iOS 27.2.1 and iOS 26.7.3 security notes do not mention worse_query or a sandbox fix, leaving the fix status unconfirmed.

Binance advised users to delete apps installed from untrusted sources, including sideloaded or manually signed apps, and to update iOS and all apps. People who suspect a breach were advised to move funds to a new wallet created on a clean device and reset active login sessions. The article notes that AltStore and SideStore can automatically create the App Group required by WQ Files when used with an Apple ID.

  • The publicly released proof-of-concept is described by its author as not useful on its own, but other developers have already turned it into installable apps such as WQ Files.
  • WQ Files can gain the required App Group setup automatically when installed with AltStore or SideStore using an Apple ID, making the issue particularly relevant to users of manually installed apps.
  • The reported scope differs among sources: WQ Files lists specific tested iOS versions, while Erosion’s developers only said they were “pretty sure” iOS 27.2.1 is supported.
  • Although Apple released iOS updates after worse_query was published, the associated security notes do not confirm a sandbox fix, leaving its current patch status unconfirmed.
Key Points
  • 01A researcher known as forcequit published worse_query, an iOS sandbox escape that can let an app access data containers belonging to other apps on iPhones running iOS 26 and 27.
  • 02Binance warned that exposed app data could include cryptocurrency wallet seed phrases, private keys, and login credentials, though no attacks or theft involving worse_query have been reported.
  • 03The WQ Files file manager uses worse_query to read, edit, delete, and clear other apps’ data without a jailbreak or kernel exploit, and it is installed through sideloading tools such as AltStore and SideStore.
  • 04Apple’s iOS 27.2.1 and iOS 26.7.3 security notes do not mention worse_query or an iOS sandbox fix.

Beyond the Alert: The Case for Rich Network Data

In plain words

The article says that a security warning alone is often not enough to show what an intruder did inside a computer network. It argues that security teams need detailed records of network activity so they can trace what happened before and after a warning, determine how far an intruder got, and stop further harm. This matters because automated tools can help review large amounts of information, but people still need clear evidence to check whether those tools are correct and make safe decisions.

Summary
Corelight argues that effective network incident response depends on rich, searchable network evidence—not just alerts—so analysts and AI can trace activity, validate findings, and understand an attack’s full scope. The sponsored article urges buyers to evaluate NDR tools by how deeply their data supports investigation beyond a guided demo.

The article argues that security prevention has limits and that teams must be able to find and disrupt activity that has already entered a network. Alerts can show that something occurred, but they may not reveal whether an intruder moved to other machines, sought additional access, or explored the environment. The ability to trace that activity is presented as the opportunity to intervene before data is stolen or systems are disrupted.

It distinguishes shallow network data, such as timestamps, IP addresses, and basic connection details, from richer data that preserves transaction detail and context. Richer data can support searches across time and hosts, packet-content review, file extraction, and reconstruction of what occurred before and after an alert. The article also says AI can process data at large scale and assist with unfamiliar information, but analysts must validate AI-generated explanations, queries, scripts, and commands against underlying evidence.

  • A failed search during an investigation may mean the necessary evidence was never collected, rather than proving that the activity did not occur.
  • The article treats the ability to inspect evidence underlying an AI-generated conclusion as a core measure of data quality, because AI output cannot yet be trusted completely.
  • A useful product evaluation should deliberately depart from the vendor’s guided demo by asking an unanticipated question and tracing the activity both backward and forward in time.
  • Rich data is presented as valuable not only for finding known threats, but also for helping AI identify subtle patterns across hosts and behaviors that shallow connection summaries cannot correlate.
Key Points
  • 01The article argues that preventing every security incident is not possible, so teams need to find and disrupt activity that has entered their networks.
  • 02An alert may identify a suspicious connection, but detailed network data is needed to trace activity before, after, and across systems and determine an incident’s scope.
  • 03Rich network data can include transaction details, packet content, extracted files, searchable history, and context that allows analysts to follow connections over time.
  • 04AI tools can process large amounts of security data and help investigate unfamiliar services, processes, and log formats, but their outputs still need to be checked against evidence.
  • 05The article recommends evaluating network security products by investigating an alert beyond a guided demonstration to see whether the available data supports unanticipated questions and deeper analysis.
