In plain words

This interview follows Ryan Fetterman’s path from security work for U.S. federal agencies to research roles at Splunk and Cisco Talos. He describes investigating an early long-running targeted intrusion called Pandora Song by examining activity on computers and searching large collections of records for suspicious patterns. The discussion matters because people protecting organizations must continually adapt as attackers change how they try to stay unnoticed, while also building careers that avoid burnout.

Summary
Security researcher Ryan Fetterman discusses his path from federal SOC work and Splunk research to Cisco Talos, emphasizing practical detection engineering and the evolving tactics attackers use to evade defenses. He also advocates project-based learning and sustainable career habits to avoid burnout.

Ryan Fetterman discusses his roughly 15-year security career, beginning with consulting and security operations work in the Washington, DC area. His experience with federal agencies and government consulting included training, certifications, and an early exposure to Splunk. He says querying log data and building visualizations and analytics in Splunk was the first work that felt like a natural fit.

Fetterman later joined Splunk’s SURGe research team and became part of Talos after Cisco acquired Splunk and reorganized the team. He recounts investigating the Pandora Song advanced persistent threat incident while working in a federal security operations center, using host-based sensors and Splunk analytics to examine the attacker’s activity. The interview also addresses how attackers change tactics to evade traditional defenses, as well as project-based learning and taking time away from the keyboard to build a sustainable security career without burnout.

  • Fetterman’s experience suggests that a security specialization may emerge through hands-on work rather than being chosen and pursued immediately at the start of a career.
  • His early work on host-based analytics illustrates that examining activity directly on computers became a major focus of later detection engineering.
  • The article highlights that attackers adapt their methods to remain ahead of traditional defenses, making detection work an ongoing and often frustrating process.
  • Fetterman emphasizes project-based learning and stepping away from the keyboard as part of building a sustainable security career without burnout.
