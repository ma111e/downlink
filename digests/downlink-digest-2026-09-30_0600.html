Attackers Exploit NetScaler Flaw for Root Access, Deploy WHIPSHOT and SLAPSHOT

In plain words

Unknown attackers are taking advantage of a recently fixed flaw in Citrix NetScaler internet-facing systems to gain complete control of affected devices. The activity has targeted organizations in North America and Europe, including government, finance, technology, education, legal, and professional-services groups. After getting in, the attackers can hide remote-control tools on the device, explore the victim’s internal network, and steal login details. This matters because these systems often sit at the edge of an organization’s network and can provide a path into more sensitive systems.

Summary
Attackers are actively exploiting the critical CVE-2026-88772 flaw in Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway appliances to gain root access, install WHIPSHOT web shells, and deploy the SLAPSHOT tunneler for internal reconnaissance and credential theft. Organizations should patch exposed NetScaler systems immediately and investigate for malicious PHP files, altered web-server configurations, and suspicious .ico or .sig requests.

Unknown threat actors exploited the recently patched CVE-2026-88772 flaw in Citrix NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway appliances to gain root-level access. Mandiant Consulting and Google Threat Intelligence Group observed the activity in September 2026 against organizations in North America and Europe, including government, financial services, technology, education, and legal and professional-services sectors. The flaw is a CVSS 9.5 memory overflow issue in DTLS handling within the NetScaler Packet Processing Engine.

After compromising an appliance, the attackers modified web-server configuration files so files disguised as .deb or .sig files could run as PHP web shells. They used WHIPSHOT to receive Base64-encoded commands through HTTP headers and SLAPSHOT to relay traffic to internal systems for reconnaissance and credential theft. GreyNoise reported that exploitation activity involving CVE-2026-88771 and CVE-2026-88772 escalated on September 28, 2026, from mass reconnaissance to broad web-shell and malware deployment associated with botnet recruitment and access brokering.

  • The attackers concealed web-shell activity by configuring requests for seemingly ordinary .ico image files under /vpn/media/ to run matching hidden .sig files, meaning HTTP 404 responses with unusually long processing times and large response sizes can still indicate successful malicious processing.
  • The campaign established persistence by changing permissions on /bin/sh and rebooting the full NetScaler appliance, rather than relying only on the initially installed web shell.
  • SLAPSHOT removes its port and lock files and stops itself after 10 minutes without commands or active sessions, reducing the traces left for investigators.
  • Attempts to access non-existent .sig files generated missing-file errors in NetScaler logs, which Google noted may indicate the attackers were managing similar web shells across multiple compromised environments.
Key Points
  • 01Unknown threat actors exploited CVE-2026-88772, a recently patched Citrix NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway flaw, to obtain root-level access to targeted appliances.
  • 02Mandiant Consulting and Google Threat Intelligence Group observed the activity in September 2026 against organizations in North America and Europe across government, financial services, technology, education, and legal and professional-services sectors.
  • 03The attackers deployed WHIPSHOT, a PHP web shell that receives Base64-encoded commands through HTTP headers, and SLAPSHOT, a Python tool that forwards network traffic to internal hosts.
  • 04The attackers modified NetScaler web-server configuration files to execute files disguised as Debian packages or signature files as PHP scripts and, in some cases, to make image-request URLs trigger hidden web shells.
  • 05GreyNoise reported that activity involving CVE-2026-88771 and CVE-2026-88772 escalated from mass reconnaissance to broad exploitation and web-shell and malware deployment on September 28, 2026.

OpenSSL Fixes High-Severity DTLS Flaw That Can Leak Heap Memory Unencrypted
thehackernews.com · 30 Sep 2026 08:09 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra
TAGScisacve-2026-75806cve-2026-84782cve-2026-84783debiandtlsopensslopenssl-projectubuntuwebrtc

In plain words

OpenSSL released fixes for a serious flaw in the part of its software used to protect some internet calls and WebRTC data connections. In certain situations, the bug could send leftover data from a program’s memory to the other side without protection or make the program stop working. Only software that uses OpenSSL for these UDP-based connections is affected, and both client and server programs may be at risk. Updating OpenSSL or installing a patched operating-system package matters because leaked memory can expose sensitive information and crashes can disrupt services.

Summary
OpenSSL has patched CVE-2026-84782, a high-severity DTLS flaw that can expose unencrypted heap memory to a peer or crash affected applications. Organizations using OpenSSL for DTLS should upgrade to 4.0.3, 3.6.5, 3.5.9, or 3.4.8, or apply their distribution’s patched package.

OpenSSL released fixes on September 29 for CVE-2026-84782, a High-severity flaw in its DTLS implementation. The issue can leak heap memory to the other side of a DTLS connection as unencrypted handshake data or crash the affected program. Only software that uses OpenSSL for DTLS is exposed, including software using DTLS to protect WebRTC data channels or establish encryption keys for internet calls. OpenSSL has not said whether an attacker can trigger the required condition, and it had not reported exploitation.

The bug occurs when DTLS pauses while sending a fragmented large handshake message and its resend timer retransmits an earlier message. Before the fix, the retransmission used the paused message’s buffer position rather than the beginning of the message that should be resent, causing leftover bytes from the larger message to be sent with an incorrect message label and potentially read beyond the buffer. Public fixes are available in OpenSSL 4.0.3, 3.6.5, 3.5.9, and 3.4.8, while fixes for OpenSSL 3.0, 1.1.1, and 1.0.2 require premium support. Ubuntu and Debian also released patched packages, and OpenSSL lists no workaround for users who cannot update.

  • The flaw can disclose leftover heap memory as unencrypted handshake data, meaning the affected data is not limited to the intended retransmitted message.
  • OpenSSL has not stated whether an attacker can reliably trigger the resend condition needed for the flaw, and no exploitation was reported at the time of disclosure.
  • Although OpenSSL 3.0 no longer receives public security fixes, Ubuntu shipped fixes for its OpenSSL 3.0-based 22.04 and 24.04 packages.
  • OpenSSL lists no workaround for users unable to update, making a move to a supported branch or a patched distribution package the available remediation path.
  • CISA rated the issue 8.2 out of 10 and recorded high availability impact, while OpenSSL classified it as High under its separate severity system.
Key Points
  • 01OpenSSL fixed CVE-2026-84782, a high-severity DTLS flaw that can leak heap memory as unencrypted handshake data to a connection peer or crash an affected program.
  • 02The issue occurs when a DTLS handshake message is only partly sent and the resend timer retransmits an earlier message using the wrong position in the buffer.
  • 03Public fixes are available in OpenSSL 4.0.3, 3.6.5, 3.5.9, and 3.4.8, while fixes for the older 3.0, 1.1.1, and 1.0.2 branches are available only to premium support customers.
  • 04Only software using OpenSSL for DTLS is exposed, and OpenSSL tested the fix for both client and server roles.
  • 05Ubuntu and Debian released patched OpenSSL packages, while OpenSSL listed no workaround for users who cannot update.

Ransomware Leverage is Growing by the Terabyte: Takeaways from ThreatLabz 2026 Ransomware Report
zscaler.com · 30 Sep 2026 06:47 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra

In plain words

The report says ransomware criminals are stealing far larger amounts of company data and using it to pressure victims into paying, even when the overall number of publicly known victims has changed little. They often trick employees—especially managers and people in finance, sales, operations, human resources, and marketing—through fake support requests, messages, or calls, then use legitimate remote-help software to get into company systems. This matters because a single successful intrusion can expose huge amounts of sensitive information and create major financial pressure on an organization.

Summary
Ransomware groups are increasingly using trusted support tools and social-engineering tactics to steal terabytes of data, making exfiltration-driven extortion more consequential even as victim counts and total payments decline. Defenders should prioritize stopping initial access, restricting lateral movement, and rapidly blocking data exfiltration.

Zscaler’s ThreatLabz 2026 Ransomware Report examines ransomware activity from April 2025 through March 2026, including victim targeting, attack techniques, stolen data, and payments. The report found that the top 10 groups by reported leak volume collectively exfiltrated 896.2 TB of data, a 275.8% year-over-year increase. This growth in data theft gives attackers greater extortion leverage even though publicly disclosed victim counts remained relatively stable.

Attackers increasingly target business users with influence and access to important workflows, with 62% of victims holding manager-level titles or above and roughly 75% working in finance, sales, operations, HR, or marketing. They combine spam bombing and Microsoft Teams vishing with legitimate remote-support tools such as Quick Assist, then use further tooling for reconnaissance, persistence, lateral movement, data theft, and encryption. While known ransom payments declined 15.8% to \(327.8 million, the average payment rose 5.3% to \)431,995, underscoring the importance of stopping initial access, limiting movement within an organization, and blocking unauthorized data transfers.

  • Ransomware pressure is increasingly driven by the ability to steal large amounts of data, meaning stopping data removal after an intrusion is as important as preventing file encryption.
  • Employees with business influence and access to critical workflows can be high-risk targets even when they do not have formal IT administrator roles.
  • Legitimate collaboration and remote-support tools can become an entry point for attackers when paired with fake support interactions, so these everyday workflows are part of the ransomware exposure.
  • Frequent group shutdowns, rebrands, and ranking changes do not necessarily reduce risk because affiliates can carry the same access methods and tools into new operations.
  • Lower total ransom payments do not mean incidents are becoming less costly, since the average recorded payment increased as attackers extracted more from victims that paid.
Key Points
  • 01Among the top 10 ransomware groups by reported data-leak volume, combined stolen data increased 275.8% year over year to 896.2 TB during April 2025 through March 2026.
  • 02ThreatLabz found that 62% of ransomware victims held manager-level titles or above, while roughly 75% worked in finance, sales, operations, HR, and marketing.
  • 03Threat actors increasingly combine spam bombing and Microsoft Teams voice phishing with legitimate remote-support tools such as Quick Assist to gain initial access.
  • 04Publicly disclosed ransomware leak sites listed 7,366 victims, a 3% year-over-year decline, while 60% of the top 15 groups by victim volume were new to the rankings.
  • 05Known ransom payments declined 15.8% year over year to $327.8 million, but the average payment increased 5.3% to $431,995.
