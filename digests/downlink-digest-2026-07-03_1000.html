    In plain words

    A group called Armored Likho has been linked to attacks on government agencies and the electric power sector in Russia, Brazil, and Kazakhstan. The attackers send convincing emails with files that install BusySnake Stealer, a program that can steal documents, passwords, browser cookies, Telegram data, screenshots, and other sensitive information from Windows computers.     Summary
    Kaspersky says the previously undocumented Armored Likho group is targeting government agencies and the power sector in Russia, Brazil, and Kazakhstan with BusySnake Stealer, a Python-based malware that steals data, maintains persistence, and can deploy tunneling and remote-access tools. The campaign overlaps with Eagle Werewolf activity and shows increasing sophistication, including LNK exploitation, obfuscation, and built-in reverse SSH tunneling.

    A previously undocumented threat actor called Armored Likho has been attributed to cyber attacks targeting government agencies and the electric power sector in Russia, Brazil, and Kazakhstan. Kaspersky said the group combines financially motivated campaigns against private individuals with targeted cyber espionage against organizations. The group’s toolkit includes obfuscated, modular remote access tools and information stealers designed to bypass dynamic analysis.

    The attacks use tools such as Go2Tunnel for remote access and network tunneling, and Kaspersky reported possible overlaps between Armored Likho and BI.ZONE’s Eagle Werewolf cluster. The campaign includes a previously unreported Python-based Windows information stealer called BusySnake Stealer, with one version capable of stealing browser cookies. Attack chains begin with spear-phishing emails using government notice or social program lures, delivering RAR archives with EXE droppers or Windows shortcut files that exploit CVE-2025-9491.

    BusySnake can steal clipboard data, enumerate files, upload documents, capture screenshots, log keystrokes, collect Telegram session and credential data, gather cryptocurrency wallet files, extract browser cookies and passwords, and establish a reverse SSH tunnel using Go2Tunnel. It also maintains persistence through VBScript files and scheduled tasks, and newer versions include a task-management framework for tracking commands from the command-and-control server. Kaspersky said the campaign reflects Armored Likho’s growing technical maturity, tool polymorphism, and increasing use of more complex methods to evade security solutions.

    • Armored Likho appears to blur criminal and espionage activity, with Kaspersky describing both financially motivated campaigns against individuals and targeted espionage against organizations.
    • The group’s tooling shows rapid adaptation because Go2Tunnel’s reverse-tunneling capability, previously used as a standalone utility, has been integrated directly into BusySnake Stealer as a built-in feature controlled by the C2 server.
    • BusySnake’s RustDesk abuse is notable because the malware prompts the victim to enter credentials into the legitimate remote desktop tool, then captures and exfiltrates a screenshot of those credentials.
    • Kaspersky found signs that some first-stage loaders and stagers may have been generated with AI assistance, based on redundant comments and code blocks in the payloads.
    • The newer BusySnake version adds a task-management framework with statuses such as SCHEDULED, IN_PROGRESS, SUCCEEDED, and FAILED, indicating a more organized system for tracking victim-side operations.

In plain words

A procurement employee received a blank email with a Word document that looked like a personal salary update and included their real name and email address. The document asked them to scan a square phone code, which secretly led to a fake sign-in page meant to steal their password. This matters because the dangerous web address was hidden as a picture inside the file, so normal email checks did not see it, and using a phone could take the person outside workplace protections.

Summary
A payroll-themed phishing email used a Word attachment containing only a QR-code image that led to a credential-harvesting site, bypassing normal email URL scanning and pushing the victim to use a phone outside corporate protections. The key lesson is that defenses must inspect attachments, decode embedded QR codes, and correlate context like failed authentication and self-addressed sender patterns.

A procurement employee received an email titled “Important Update to Your Salary and Compensation Structure” with a blank body and a Word attachment named Revised_Pay_Structure_Notice.docx. The document appeared to be an HR memo, greeted the employee by full name, referenced the next payroll cycle, and asked the recipient to scan a QR code to view updated compensation details. The QR code decoded to a credential-harvesting URL, making the QR code the entire attack payload.

The article explains that the attack mattered because the malicious link was not present as clickable text in the email body. Instead, it was encoded in pixels inside an image embedded in an Office document, so inline URL and reputation scanners had nothing to extract from the message body. The article also notes that scanning the QR code with a phone could move the web request outside the corporate network, secure web gateway, or inline proxy.

The message failed SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and Microsoft composite authentication, and the sender address matched the recipient’s own address. The DOCX was 43,915 bytes, contained no macros, and included an embedded PNG with the QR code. The article says layered detection should read inside documents, decode embedded QR codes, evaluate the resulting destination, and consider context such as a self-addressed message with failed authentication.

  • A phishing message can fail every major email authentication check and still be convincing to a recipient if the attached document uses personal details such as the person’s real name, email address, and payroll timing.
  • Moving the malicious destination into a QR code inside a Word file creates two separate blind spots: automated email tools may not extract the link, and the victim may open it on a phone outside corporate web protections.
  • The absence of macros in the DOCX makes macro-focused attachment detection insufficient for this type of attack, because the harmful content is the embedded image and its decoded destination.
  • A self-addressed message combined with failed authentication and a QR-only payload is presented as a strong combined warning signal even when each individual sign might appear survivable.
Key Points

  • 02The only payload in the document was an embedded QR code image that decoded to a credential-harvesting URL on the domain weststrionvde[.]vu.
  • 03The malicious destination was not inspected by inline URL scanners because the link was encoded in pixels inside an image nested in an Office document rather than appearing as text in the email body.
