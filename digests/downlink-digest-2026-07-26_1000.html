DKIM and DMARC Passed. The $97,500 Wire Was Fraud.

In plain words

A company’s accounts-payable team received what looked like a legitimate invoice asking for a same-day $97,500 wire payment, and the email passed the usual checks that show it came from the claimed sender’s domain. The fraud was hidden in the reply address, which would have sent any questions or payment confirmations to the attacker instead of the real institution. This matters because busy finance teams can be tricked into sending large payments even when an email appears to be properly verified, so money requests need to be confirmed through a separate trusted channel.

Summary
A $97,500 wire-fraud email passed DKIM and DMARC because it was sent through a legitimate institution’s authorized mail stream, but a mismatched Reply-To redirected responses to the attacker. The key takeaway is that email authentication proves domain control, not that a payment request is legitimate, so high-value transactions still require out-of-band verification.

An accounts-payable team received an invoice demanding a same-day $97,500 ACH or wire payment, styled as a reply to an already-approved item. The message passed DKIM and DMARC, was signed by a real federal cultural and research institution’s own mail stream through SendGrid, and received a Microsoft composite authentication score of 100. The article emphasizes that nothing cryptographic was broken and that the message was not a simple spoof.

The fraud depended on a mismatched Reply-To header that reused the trusted display name but pointed to mahalaxmitraders@waareepartners[.]com, an unrelated domain. If a finance clerk replied to ask about bank details or confirm payment, the response would go to the attacker rather than the institution. The email also included a SendGrid open-tracking pixel, which the article describes as live-target confirmation when the message was rendered.

The article’s main significance is that email authentication validated control of the sending domain, not the legitimacy of the payment request. IRONSCALES said its Adaptive AI analyst, Themis, flagged the message based on signals including the first-time sender, Reply-To mismatch, urgent large-dollar request, and community reputation signals, then quarantined it across all four affected mailboxes. The article recommends detection that weighs behavioral signals alongside authentication results and an out-of-band verification rule for new or changed bank details and same-day wires.

  • A clean DKIM and DMARC result can increase risk if teams treat it as proof that the business request is legitimate, because in this case authentication only proved the domain’s mail stream was used.
  • A single Reply-To mismatch can be enough to run a business email compromise scheme without any malicious link, attachment, or broken cryptography.
  • Open-tracking pixels can function as reconnaissance in payment-fraud emails by confirming that a target mailbox is active and monitored.
  • Behavioral signals such as first-time sender status, urgent payment language, Reply-To mismatch, and newly provisioned remittance domains can catch fraud that authentication checks alone miss.
  • The article recommends that same-day wires and new or changed bank details be verified through a known channel outside email, regardless of how authoritative or authenticated the message appears.
