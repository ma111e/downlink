In plain words

A Chinese hacking group called Vapor Panda used an AI-powered tool called ARTEX to target more than 200 organizations in 14 countries, including government offices, phone companies, drug makers, financial firms, and defense-related organizations. At least nine organizations were broken into, while other targets faced attempts to guess passwords and check stolen login details. The AI tool could rapidly search systems, find weak points, steal access information, and try other routes when blocked, while human operators stepped in for important decisions. This matters because it shows attackers can use AI to run many break-in attempts at once, making large-scale spying campaigns faster and easier to carry out.

Summary
Chinese-linked Vapor Panda used the ARTEX AI multi-agent penetration-testing framework, alongside commercial language models, to automate cyberespionage against more than 200 organizations in 14 countries, compromising at least nine. The campaign shows AI agents can conduct reconnaissance, exploitation, credential theft, and lateral activity across multiple intrusions under human supervision.

Chinese-linked threat actor Vapor Panda used the ARTEX AI multi-agent penetration-testing framework in a cyberespionage campaign against more than 200 organizations in 14 countries. The operation, which began October 17, targeted government ministries, telecommunications providers, and organizations in pharmaceutical, semiconductor, defense, nuclear, and financial sectors. CrowdStrike said at least nine organizations were compromised, while at least 12 additional high-value targets experienced password-spraying and credential-scanning activity.

CrowdStrike reported that ARTEX AI, DeepSeek, and another unidentified commercial language model helped coordinate planning and worker agents across multiple intrusions, with human operators intervening for critical decisions. The agents conducted reconnaissance, exploited vulnerabilities, deployed web shells, obtained credentials, and attempted alternate paths, while being directed to avoid Windows systems protected by CrowdStrike Falcon. Separately, the Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure said ARTEX AI autonomously gained root privileges in its network within 12 minutes by chaining a Zammad zero-day with a second local-privilege-escalation zero-day, generating more than 450,000 requests and 2,500 scripts.

  • The campaign combined autonomous AI-agent activity with human decision-making at critical points, allowing one operator to direct multiple intrusions simultaneously.
  • The operators instructed the agents to avoid Windows systems protected by CrowdStrike Falcon and instead focus on unprotected hosts.
  • CrowdStrike estimated that AI agents used more than 1.4 billion input tokens during one intrusion.
  • DIVD said the ARTEX AI activity was highly detectable despite its speed, generating more than 450,000 requests and 2,500 scripts during the attack.
  • ARTEX AI reportedly reached root-level access in DIVD’s environment within 12 minutes by combining two previously unknown software flaws.
Key Points
  • 01CrowdStrike says the China-linked threat actor Vapor Panda used ARTEX AI in a cyberespionage campaign targeting more than 200 organizations across 14 countries beginning on October 17.
  • 02The campaign targeted government ministries, telecommunications providers, and organizations in the pharmaceutical, semiconductor, defense, nuclear, and financial sectors, with at least nine organizations compromised.
  • 03Vapor Panda used DeepSeek and another commercial language model to coordinate AI agents that performed reconnaissance, exploited flaws, deployed remote-control tools, obtained credentials, and pursued alternative paths.
  • 04In an intrusion against a Greek government entity, the agents forged an administrator token and read mailboxes using authentication keys from a 2022 mail-server directory export.
  • 05The Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure said ARTEX AI autonomously reached root-level access on its network in 12 minutes by chaining a Zammad zero-day with a second zero-day that raised its privileges.

In plain words

Stores, butchers, and grocery chains in Belgium, Estonia, Ireland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and Sweden reported that someone remotely changed their refrigerator settings overnight. In some locations, temperatures were raised to 50°C (122°F), spoiling or melting food. Reports suggest the affected equipment was Danfoss StoreView, the same type of refrigeration controller involved in earlier attacks on French grocery stores. The events show that internet-connected equipment can cause immediate food loss and business disruption when it is accessed by unauthorized people.

Summary
Cyberattacks disrupted commercial refrigeration systems at grocery stores across six European countries, reportedly raising temperatures to 50°C and spoiling food. The incidents again appear linked to internet-exposed Danfoss StoreView controllers, though many exposed systems remain unpatched.

On November 2, grocers, butchers, and small shops across Belgium, Estonia, Ireland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and Sweden reported cyberattacks on commercial refrigeration units. The attacks reportedly raised refrigerator temperatures to 50°C (122°F), causing food to spoil or melt in dozens of stores. Carrefour, Coop, Delhaize, ICA, Selver, SuperValu, and Spar were among the affected chains.

The incidents resembled attacks against French grocers three weeks earlier in their early-morning timing, targeting, and temperature changes. Reports suggest Danfoss StoreView refrigeration controllers may have been involved in both waves, although no group has claimed responsibility and it is unconfirmed whether the same actor was responsible. Censys observed just over 1,000 exposed StoreView instances globally; France had fallen from 1,825 exposed systems before the October attacks to 220 at the time of analysis, with 41% appearing to use patched firmware.

  • France’s exposed Danfoss StoreView systems fell sharply from 1,825 before the October attacks to 220 at the time of analysis, indicating a major reduction in publicly reachable interfaces after the French incidents.
  • Patching levels varied substantially among affected countries: 68% of observed Belgian StoreView instances and 58% of Swedish instances appeared to run version 4.3.1 or later, compared with 41% in France.
  • The reported overnight timing and temperature changes to 50°C meant store employees were less likely to be able to respond immediately, increasing the potential for food spoilage.
  • Despite the similarity to the earlier French incidents, no group had claimed responsibility and it remained unconfirmed whether the same actor was responsible.
Key Points
  • 01On November 2, grocers, butchers, and small shops in Belgium, Estonia, Ireland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and Sweden reported attacks on commercial refrigeration units.
  • 02Attackers reportedly raised refrigeration temperatures to 50°C (122°F), causing food to spoil or melt in dozens of stores.
  • 03Reports indicated that Danfoss StoreView refrigeration controllers, which were involved in attacks on French grocers on October 11, may also have been involved in this wave.
  • 04Censys observed just over 1,000 internet-exposed Danfoss StoreView instances globally, with France having the highest concentration.
  • 05Censys found that France had 220 exposed StoreView instances at the time of analysis, down from 1,825 before the October attacks, and 41% of the remaining French instances appeared to run patched firmware.
