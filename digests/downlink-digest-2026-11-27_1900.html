pCloud confirms password manager hack after suspicious login alerts
bleepingcomputer.com · 27 Nov 2026 19:26 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra
TAGSluxembourgpcloudpcloud-pass
78
In plain words

pCloud says someone attacked its pCloud Pass password manager, exposing a small number of users’ email addresses and causing alerts that an unfamiliar device had tried to connect to their accounts. The company says its file-storage service and the passwords, notes, and other information held in the password manager were not exposed, because those items are locked using each user’s main password. It has not said whether attackers copied the locked password collections, which matters because they could try to guess weak or reused main passwords outside pCloud’s normal login protections. Users should also watch for fake messages, since pCloud says it will never ask for a main password or recovery words by email, phone, or chat.

Summary
pCloud confirmed that its pCloud Pass password manager was targeted in a contained cyberattack that exposed a small number of user email addresses and triggered suspicious login alerts, including for accounts protected by 2FA. The company says password vault contents were not compromised, but has not said whether encrypted vaults were accessed or stolen.

pCloud confirmed that its pCloud Pass password manager was targeted in a cyberattack that the company says was promptly contained. Users had reported email alerts saying an unfamiliar device named “pd” connected to their accounts from an IP address in Luxembourg, including users who had two-factor authentication enabled. pCloud said a small number of pCloud Pass user email addresses were exposed and login attempts were made, which triggered automatic notifications.

The company said the incident was isolated to pCloud Pass and did not affect its cloud-storage services. It also said passwords, notes, and other data in pCloud Pass were not compromised because they are encrypted on customers’ devices and require their master passwords to decrypt. However, pCloud did not disclose whether encrypted password vaults were accessed or stolen, a significant omission because stolen encrypted vaults could be targeted with offline password-cracking attempts outside normal login rate limits and two-factor authentication protections.

  • Two-factor authentication did not prevent users from receiving suspicious-login alerts, showing that the notifications reflected login attempts rather than necessarily successful account access.
  • Some recipients said they had never used pCloud Pass, and one said the alert went to an email address tied to a storage account requested for deletion nearly a year earlier.
  • The notification’s “Open settings” link pointed to passqa1.pcloud.com, which appears to be a quality-assurance environment rather than the production password-manager site.
  • Although pCloud says vault contents remain encrypted on users’ devices, it did not say whether attackers obtained encrypted vaults; such copies could be targeted with password-guessing attempts outside normal login limits and two-factor authentication protections.
Key Points
  • 01pCloud confirmed that a cyberattack targeting its pCloud Pass password manager was promptly contained.
  • 02The company said a small number of pCloud Pass user email addresses were exposed and login attempts were made, triggering automatic notifications.
  • 03Users reported alerts about an unfamiliar device named "pd" connecting from an IP address in Luxembourg, including some users with two-factor authentication enabled.
  • 04pCloud said its cloud-storage services were not affected and that passwords, notes, and other pCloud Pass data were not compromised because they are encrypted on customers' devices.
  • 05pCloud did not disclose whether encrypted password vaults were accessed or stolen during the incident and warned users to watch for phishing attempts.

Preparing for the future AI cryptography break
aikido.dev · 27 Nov 2026 20:58 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra

In plain words

The article discusses unconfirmed reports that advances in AI could eventually find weaknesses in the mathematical methods used to protect websites, software updates, and private data. Nothing has been shown to break the protections commonly used today, so companies do not need to make emergency changes. However, organizations that know where these protections are used can replace them faster if a real weakness is announced, helping protect customers, services, and long-lived connected devices.

Summary
Rumors of AI-driven cryptographic breakthroughs do not yet justify immediate changes, but organizations should inventory their cryptography and build algorithm agility now. This preparation will speed response to any future weakness in RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography and support migration to post-quantum, hybrid encryption.

Unconfirmed reports and online rumors have raised the possibility that AI labs may have results affecting cryptography used in real systems, including claims about the difficulty of factoring the large numbers behind RSA. The article stresses that no lab has confirmed a practical break and that organizations do not need to change anything immediately. However, it argues that AI could accelerate cryptanalysis and reduce the warning time available before an algorithm is weakened.

A practical weakness in RSA-2048 or elliptic-curve systems such as ECDSA and X25519 could affect TLS connections, website certificates, and software signing, while current AI results do not threaten deployed modern key sizes. The article recommends building a cryptography inventory, designing for algorithm agility, and moving toward post-quantum cryptography with hybrid schemes where appropriate. It also notes that quantum computing threatens the same public-key systems, while NIST’s first post-quantum standards were published in August 2024.

  • A cryptographic result is only operationally dangerous if it works against the key sizes organizations actually use; a mathematically efficient method may still be far too slow in practice because of large constants or exponents.
  • A cryptography inventory must extend beyond an application's own code because encryption can be introduced through dependency defaults, public-site TLS settings, certificates, and long-lived devices such as those used in medtech and automotive.
  • Hybrid post-quantum TLS key exchange can protect data captured today from later decryption because an attacker would need to defeat both the existing classical method and ML-KEM.
  • Google DeepMind's reported method made additional 1024-bit RSA keys roughly 30 times cheaper to factor after a large upfront computation, but its reported run still required 391 CPU core-years and was not said to threaten 2048-bit or larger keys.
  • The article notes that weak algorithms such as MD5 and SHA-1 can persist years after retirement because organizations often lack visibility into every place they are used.
Key Points
  • 01Reports that AI labs may have results affecting deployed cryptography remain unconfirmed, and the article says they should be treated as a thought experiment rather than a reason for immediate changes.
  • 02A practical break of RSA-2048 or elliptic-curve cryptography could affect TLS connections, website certificates, and software signing, while current AI results against AES and cryptographic systems do not threaten modern deployed key sizes.
  • 03The article recommends that organizations create a cryptography inventory, design systems so algorithms can be replaced, and accelerate post-quantum cryptography migration using hybrid schemes where appropriate.
  • 04Aikido's CBOM feature is described as identifying cryptographic algorithms, protocols, and certificates in repositories, including use through libraries, while flagging weak algorithms such as MD5 and SHA-1.
  • 05NIST published its first post-quantum cryptography standards in August 2024, and the article states that a sufficiently capable quantum computer could also break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography.
