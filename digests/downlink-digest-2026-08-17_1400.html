C2Looper: A New Backdoor Likely Tied To Ransomware With GitHub C2

In plain words

Zscaler ThreatLabz found a new harmful program called C2Looper that can let an attacker control an infected Windows computer, gather information from it, run commands, and download more tools. The researchers believe it may be used by criminals connected to ransomware, where victims’ data or systems can be held hostage for payment. A newer version can use GitHub to send instructions and store results, showing that the people behind it are still improving it and making it harder to spot.

Summary
Zscaler ThreatLabz identified C2Looper, a new Rust-based backdoor likely tied to ransomware activity that supports command execution, reconnaissance, and second-stage payload deployment. A newer version uses GitHub for command-and-control, suggesting the malware is actively evolving.

Zscaler ThreatLabz identified a new Rust-based malware family called C2Looper in July 2026. The malware is likely leveraged by a ransomware-related threat actor and is used to establish a foothold for lateral movement. ThreatLabz assesses with low to medium confidence that C2Looper has been delivered through a multi-stage ClickFix infection chain.

C2Looper supports backdoor commands for executing arbitrary commands, performing reconnaissance, and deploying second-stage payloads. Older variants use plaintext HTTP to communicate with a command-and-control server through endpoints such as /api/beacon and /api/result/, while also encrypting strings and dynamically resolving Windows APIs. A newer version, described as C2Looper v2, uses GitHub for command-and-control operations and adds capabilities such as file listing, drive listing, expanded reconnaissance, shellcode injection, and modified upload and run commands.

  • C2Looper v2 moved all command-and-control activity to GitHub, using per-bot directories and separate cmd.json, result.json, and beacon.json files for instructions, output, and beacon status.
  • The older C2Looper variant contained a parsing bug in the seq task ID field that caused all reported results to use task ID 0, while C2Looper v2 fixed this issue.
  • C2Looper’s upload command in older variants abuses a legitimate OneDrive executable to load a malicious wtsapi32.dll file from the user’s local OneDrive directory.
  • ThreatLabz observed an older C2Looper variant downloading the newer C2Looper version through the upload command, indicating the malware has its own update path.
  • C2Looper v2 includes debug text such as “!!! v2 !!! pongv2 from,” which the article identifies as evidence that the developer internally refers to this variant as version 2.
Key Points
  • 01Zscaler ThreatLabz identified a new Rust-based malware family called C2Looper in July 2026 that is likely used by a ransomware-related threat actor.
  • 02C2Looper supports backdoor commands including arbitrary command execution, reconnaissance, and deployment of second-stage payloads.
  • 03ThreatLabz assesses with low to medium confidence that C2Looper has been delivered through multi-stage ClickFix infection chains.
  • 04Older C2Looper variants use plaintext HTTP and send host information to a command-and-control server through the /api/beacon endpoint every second.
  • 05A newer C2Looper version uses GitHub for command-and-control operations and adds capabilities such as directory listing, drive listing, expanded reconnaissance, shellcode injection, and modified upload and run commands.

The real-world risk is that a client, job seeker, or employee may believe the fake page or message is real, hand over information, or send money before anyone can stop it.

Summary
Manual, ticket-based takedowns are too slow for brand and executive impersonation attacks, which can harvest credentials or trigger fraud within hours. Professional services firms need continuous monitoring, verified alerts, and managed takedown workflows to reduce exposure and protect client trust.

The article argues that manual brand impersonation takedowns fail because attackers can launch phishing pages, spoofed domains, fake executive profiles, and fraudulent listings faster than ticket-based abuse reports can remove them. It says these pages and profiles often do their damage within hours, while manual removal can take days. The article presents managed takedown programs as a way to reduce the exposure window from days to hours through continuous monitoring, verification, and managed removal.

The article highlights a 2026 campaign tracked by Google’s Mandiant in which UNC3753, also known as Luna Moth or Silent Ransom Group, targeted dozens of U.S. professional, legal, and financial services organizations. The campaign used benign-looking emails about data migration or unpaid invoices, follow-up phone calls from people posing as IT support, and requests to install remote monitoring software. The article uses this example to show that brand and executive impersonation can be an entry point for extortion and fraud without relying on an initial software exploit or malware drop.

The article says consulting and professional services firms are especially exposed because they hold sensitive client information and depend heavily on client trust. It describes a Cyble case study involving a U.S. consulting organization whose brand, executives, and digital infrastructure were targeted through fake social profiles, spoofed domains, fraudulent job postings, and phishing campaigns. The recommended operating model combines brand and executive monitoring, verification before escalation, and managed takedown services so confirmed threats are removed without internal teams chasing registrars and platforms one abuse ticket at a time.

  • The article argues that the most important metric for brand protection is not how many fake pages are reported or removed, but how quickly each live threat is detected, verified, and taken down.

  • For consulting and professional services firms, impersonation risk extends beyond direct financial loss because fake domains or executive profiles can damage the client trust that their business depends on.
  • Verification before escalation is presented as a practical way to reduce alert noise, so internal security teams do not have to handle every unconfirmed impersonation lead themselves.
  • The article frames brand and executive impersonation as an entry point into broader fraud or extortion campaigns, rather than as a marketing or reputation issue to clean up occasionally.
