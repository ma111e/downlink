In plain words

The article describes how Midnight Blizzard, a group linked to Russia’s foreign intelligence service, gained access to Microsoft by guessing weak passwords in a less-protected test environment. The attackers then created accounts and fake sign-in applications that gave them broad access to Microsoft’s corporate email. It matters because a small security gap in a testing system can become a route into sensitive business communications, showing why organizations must protect every part of their systems and closely review who and what has access.

Summary
Midnight Blizzard (APT29) reportedly used password spraying against a poorly secured Microsoft test tenant, then abused legacy OAuth app permissions and Entra ID roles to create persistent malicious applications with broad Exchange Online mailbox access. The incident highlights the need for MFA everywhere, strict least-privilege controls, and continuous auditing of app registrations, service principals, and consent grants.

The article describes a campaign against Microsoft attributed to Midnight Blizzard, also known as APT29 or Cozy Bear, which it links to Russia’s Foreign Intelligence Service. The attackers reportedly gained initial access by password spraying a non-production test tenant that lacked multi-factor authentication. The article presents the incident as an example of how a less-protected test environment can become an entry point into a corporate environment.

After gaining access, the attackers allegedly abused a legacy OAuth application and elevated Microsoft Entra ID permissions to create a new user and assign high-privilege roles. They then created malicious OAuth applications, obtained consent for them, and granted them the Office 365 Exchange Online full_access_as_app permission, which the article says enabled access to corporate mailboxes. The article recommends enforcing MFA, applying least privilege, auditing users, applications, service principals, and permissions, monitoring unusual consent and application activity, and securing legacy and test systems.

  • A less-protected test tenant can serve as a bridge into a corporate environment when it contains legacy applications with elevated permissions.
  • Several directory and application permissions can be combined to create users, assign powerful roles, and authorize applications to access sensitive resources such as mailboxes.
  • Creating multiple malicious OAuth applications gives attackers redundant access paths, making full removal more difficult if only one application is discovered.
  • A newly created administrative user can be used to approve malicious applications, causing service accounts for those applications to be established inside the corporate tenant.
  • Monitoring new application registrations, unusual consent activity, and high-risk permission grants can reveal this attack path before mailbox access is used.

How Okta Passwords Can Be Compromised: Uncovering a Risk to User Data
mitiga.io · 29 Sep 2026 21:23 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra
TAGSmfa-fatiguemitigaoktaokta-fastpassphishingsession-hijackingsplunk

In plain words

The article says that when an Okta user accidentally types their password into the username box, Okta may save that password in readable form in records of failed sign-in attempts. Anyone who can view those records—either in Okta or in a company system that collects the records—could potentially obtain users’ passwords, including those of administrators. This matters because stolen passwords could let criminals access company accounts, data, and applications, even though extra sign-in checks can reduce but not completely remove the risk.

Summary
Okta audit logs can store passwords in plaintext when users mistakenly enter them in the username field during a failed login, exposing credentials to anyone with access to those logs or forwarded SIEM data. Organizations should identify and rotate affected credentials, restrict log access, and enforce phishing-resistant MFA.

Mitiga researchers found that Okta audit logs can contain passwords in plaintext when users accidentally enter their password in the username field during a failed login. The failed request records the erroneous username value, while a later successful login may record the user’s actual username. Mitiga reported finding hundreds of potentially exposed passwords and credentials for customers, including administrator-level users.

The risk affects anyone able to read Okta audit logs, including users of SIEM platforms where the logs are forwarded and third-party services with Okta Read-only Administrator permissions. An attacker could use obtained credentials to attempt access to Okta-connected applications or seek higher privileges if an administrator password is exposed. Mitiga recommends using its detection query to identify possible exposures, rotating affected passwords, limiting and monitoring log access, educating users, and enforcing MFA; Okta also recommends username-field validation, clear labels, FastPass, and phishing-resistant MFA.

  • The exposure can extend beyond Okta administrators because audit logs are often forwarded to SIEM platforms, where read-only security personnel may be able to see the logged values.
  • Mitiga’s detection method links a failed login containing a likely password to a later successful login using the date, hour, device fingerprint, source IP address, and user agent.
  • A third-party product with Okta’s Read-only Administrator role may be able to read audit logs, so a breach of that service could expose credentials found in the logs.
  • The article notes that access to login patterns in the logs could let an attacker send an MFA approval request shortly after a genuine login, making it appear more legitimate to the user.
  • Okta recommends custom username-character restrictions, clear field labels, and FastPass to reduce the chance that users enter passwords in the username field.
Key Points
  • 01Okta audit logs can record a password in plaintext when a user mistakenly enters it in the username field during a failed login attempt.
  • 02People who can read Okta audit logs, including through a SIEM system where those logs are forwarded, may be able to access exposed user passwords.
  • 03Mitiga reported identifying hundreds of potentially leaked passwords and credentials for customers, including administrator-level users, by matching failed and successful login events.
  • 04Multi-factor authentication can reduce the risk from exposed passwords, but the article notes that phishing, repeated approval requests, and session hijacking may still be used to bypass it.
  • 05Mitiga recommends identifying affected users with its query, rotating their passwords, limiting and monitoring access to logs, educating users, and enabling MFA.
OPEN SOURCE ↗
JUSTIFICATION

This is a concrete research finding affecting Okta audit logs, with potential exposure of plaintext passwords to administrators, SIEM readers, and certain third-party integrations. It is broadly relevant to organizations using Okta and forwarded audit logs, although it does not report active exploitation or a newly issued patch.
