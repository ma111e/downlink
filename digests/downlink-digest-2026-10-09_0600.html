In plain words

The FBI and U.S. Justice Department took control of seven internet addresses used by a China-linked hacking group to search for weaknesses and, in some cases, break into important organizations’ computer systems. This matters because the activity could expose confidential information and threaten services people rely on, while showing how government-linked groups can use commercial contractors and compromised devices to reach targets around the world.

Summary
The FBI and DOJ seized seven domains used by China-linked Flax Typhoon and Integrity Technology Group to operate reconnaissance and spear-phishing tools targeting U.S. and foreign critical infrastructure. The FBI and U.S. Department of Justice seized seven domains and blocked access to platforms allegedly used by China-linked Flax Typhoon to scan for weaknesses and, in some cases, infiltrate U.S. critical infrastructure. Flax Typhoon, also tracked as Ethereal Panda and RedJuliett, is associated with Beijing-based Integrity Technology Group, which authorities say contracts with the Chinese government. The seized domains included c0cc[.]cc, 98aiblog[.]com, outlook3650[.]com, and linkedinns[.]net.

Court documents allege that Integrity Technology Group operated the Raptor Train botnet using a Mirai variant and managed it through an application called Sparrow. A server associated with the botnet held records for more than 1.2 million infected devices as of June 5, 2024, including more than 385,000 unique U.S. devices, while more than 260,000 devices were actively infected. The group also allegedly used MicroScan, a tool with more than 1,300 penetration-testing scripts, and FishHub for spear-phishing, remote access, and collecting selected files from victim networks.

Reported targets included a U.S. power company, airports in Japan and Poland, Taiwanese natural-gas and power organizations, and Taiwanese universities. Authorities said the actors also used cross-site scripting for credential harvesting, SoftEther VPN clients for persistence, EBurst to target Microsoft 365 accounts, and office-cli to access mailbox data. A joint advisory from agencies in several countries said Integrity Technology Group enabled China-linked actors to target organizations worldwide by acquiring or developing cyber tools and compromising networks.

  • Integrity Technology Group’s infrastructure reportedly tracked more than 1.2 million infected devices, including over 385,000 unique U.S. devices, showing the scale available to support its operations.
  • MicroScan was reportedly in use as early as 2017 and contained more than 1,300 scripts for checking specific software weaknesses, indicating a long-running and broad reconnaissance capability.
  • The article describes both automated scanning through botnets and hands-on methods such as credential harvesting and Microsoft 365 account brute-forcing, rather than reliance on a single intrusion method.
  • Although the Raptor Train botnet was disrupted in September 2024, authorities said related scanning infrastructure remained accessible through at least September 9, 2026.
  • FishHub-related activity was confirmed at 20 Taiwanese universities, where the tool could provide remote access or search for and send selected files to Integrity Technology Group-controlled servers.

GoBalance Flaw Lets Attackers Hijack .onion Addresses by Recovering Tor-Format Keys
thehackernews.com · 09 Oct 2026 09:03 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra

In plain words

A bug in GoBalance, software used by some dark-web sites to stay online during disruption, can let anyone take control of a site’s Tor web address using information the site publicly shares. Attackers can then send visitors to a fake version of the site, although the bug does not give them access to the site’s servers or stored data. Dread and Omega said they moved to new addresses after the issue, and other sites using the affected setup may also be at risk. Because an exposed address-control secret cannot be made safe again, affected sites must create entirely new addresses and users need to verify announced replacements before trusting them.

Summary
A flaw in GoBalance can expose a Tor hidden service’s long-term private key from a public descriptor, allowing attackers to hijack its .onion address and redirect visitors. Affected operators must migrate to a newly generated address because patching cannot revoke keys already exposed.

A flaw in GoBalance, a Go rewrite of Tor’s Onionbalance load balancer used with the EndGame toolkit, can let attackers recover the private key that controls an affected .onion address from public information. Searchlight Cyber disclosed the issue on October 8 and said a recovered key can be used to publish valid records that redirect visitors to an attacker-controlled copy of a site. The takeover does not provide access to the target site’s servers, databases, or stored user data.

The problem occurred because GoBalance supplied only the first 32 bytes of a 64-byte Tor private key during signing, making a secret signature value predictable and allowing recovery of the long-term master key from one public descriptor. Dread said both of its .onion addresses were taken over between October 5 and 7, while Omega publicly moved away from its old address because of the GoBalance bug. Tor and the original Onionbalance are not affected, and not all GoBalance deployments are exposed; however, sites that used the vulnerable setup must generate a new .onion address because patching cannot make an already exposed key safe again.

  • A single public Tor service descriptor can be sufficient to recover an affected service’s long-term address-control key, meaning an attacker does not need access to the site’s servers.
  • The exposed key is a long-term master key rather than a temporary key, so an attacker who recovers it can create valid records for the compromised address well into the future.
  • Not every GoBalance deployment is affected: the risk is limited to sites using Tor’s own key format, while keys created by GoBalance’s setup tool use a safer format.
  • Dread’s second address takeover was viewed as stronger evidence of exploitation of the GoBalance flaw, while its first takeover may have resulted from a separate accidental private-key upload.
  • Replacing or patching the software alone is insufficient after exposure, because operators must abandon the old .onion address and establish a new one.
Key Points
  • 01A flaw in GoBalance can allow someone to recover the private key controlling a Tor .onion address from a single publicly available service descriptor and redirect visitors to an attacker-controlled site.
  • 02The issue stems from GoBalance passing only the first 32 bytes of a 64-byte Tor private key to its signing process, making a normally secret signature value predictable.
  • 03The original Onionbalance software and Tor itself are not affected, and GoBalance sites using the safer key format created by its setup tool are not exposed.
  • 04Dread reported that both of its .onion addresses were taken over between October 5 and 7, while Omega said it moved away from its old address because of the GoBalance bug.
  • 05Patching GoBalance cannot protect a key that was already exposed, so affected operators must generate and migrate to a new .onion address.

JUSTIFICATION

This reports a concrete, apparently exploited flaw that allows takeover of affected Tor .onion addresses from publicly available data, with Dread and Omega citing impact. It is especially important for operators using GoBalance, though the affected deployment set appears narrower than broadly deployed enterprise software.
