A zero-day vulnerability dubbed “StyleSmuggler” affects all versions of Magento and Adobe Commerce and is being exploited to deploy a backdoor. Sansec recorded the first exploitation incident on September 4 against a target running the latest security updates. Adobe Enterprise Support confirmed it is working on a fix but did not provide a release timeline.

Magento is an Adobe open-source e-commerce platform installed on more than 160,000 websites, including 14,000 of the top 1 million sites. The exploit observed by Sansec abuses Magento’s template system through PHP code injection to generate a fake “failed-payment” email, which triggers code execution. Successful exploitation installs a small Rust-based backdoor as a background process on Linux.

The backdoor has been disguised as [kworker/u:8:0], while newer versions disguise the process as fc-cache and copy it to ~/.cache/fontconfig/fc-cache. Sansec says the attacker also adds a cron job that repeats every 30 minutes for persistence, and newer samples disguise communications as Network Time Protocol traffic over UDP port 123. Until fixes are available, Sansec recommends disabling GraphQL, monitoring for suspicious emails, processes, cron entries, and temporary files, and rotating Magento credentials if compromise is suspected.

  • An unexpected surge of Magento “Payment Transaction Failed Reminder” emails is a practical warning sign of possible exploitation because the exploit generates a fake failed-payment email to trigger code execution.
  • The backdoor’s newer versions disguise their network traffic as NTP by sending UDP packets to port 123 and using time-sync-like hostnames, which may help the traffic pass through firewalls unnoticed.
  • The malware checks Linux’s TracerPid value and, if tracing is active, still installs but does not beacon, meaning analysis or monitoring activity can change its visible behavior.
  • Newer backdoor samples disguise themselves as fc-cache and copy themselves to ~/.cache/fontconfig/fc-cache, giving defenders a specific process name and file path to inspect.
  • Sansec recommends rotating Magento credentials if compromise is suspected, indicating that cleanup should include credential changes in addition to searching for the backdoor and cron entries.

Summary

Organizations should prioritize phishing-resistant MFA, Conditional Access, and monitoring for residential-proxy token replay and bulk SharePoint/Exchange access.

Threat hunters disclosed details of a widespread data theft and extortion threat cluster targeting Microsoft 365 and other SaaS offerings. Arctic Wolf tracks the activity as PREY-0058 and said it shares significant tradecraft similarities with UNC6671. The activity mainly targets directors, vice presidents, and other executive staff, with targets primarily in the United States across sectors including construction and engineering, healthcare and pharmaceuticals, real estate and property management, finance, and professional services.

The attack chains begin with threat actors impersonating internal IT or help desk personnel in phone calls and directing targets to authentication-themed URLs. These pages lead to operator-controlled Microsoft 365 login flows designed to harvest credentials, MFA approvals, and authenticated session tokens. The captured tokens are then used in session replay attacks from proxy infrastructure such as NodeMaven and from IP addresses matching the victim’s geography and ASN.

After gaining access, the threat actors perform discovery against SharePoint and Entra ID, then collect and exfiltrate data from SharePoint, OneDrive, Exchange, and Box before sending extortion demands. Arctic Wolf noted that PREY-0058 does not involve endpoint malware deployment or network-based lateral movement. The article says defenders can disrupt the activity by detecting anomalous residential-proxy token replay, SharePoint discovery and bulk access, mailbox harvesting, and newly registered authentication-themed lure infrastructure.

  • The threat activity does not rely on endpoint malware or network lateral movement, so detection efforts need to focus on cloud account behavior such as token replay, SharePoint discovery, bulk access, and mailbox harvesting.
  • The attackers use residential proxy infrastructure and IP addresses matching the victim’s geography and ASN, which can make suspicious sign-ins appear more legitimate if defenders only check for unusual locations.
  • The lure infrastructure is broad and tailored, with hundreds of subdomains impersonating real companies and authentication-themed domains such as passkey and MFA registration sites.

In plain words

A service called BigBear 2.0 was used to trick Microsoft 365 users at 258 organizations and steal more than 5,000 login records, including codes and sign-in sessions that let attackers get in even after victims completed extra login checks. The affected organizations used Microsoft 365 services such as email, Teams, SharePoint, OneDrive, and Microsoft’s sign-in system. This matters because a stolen signed-in session can expose someone’s email and files and may also open the door to other workplace apps connected through the same login.

Summary
BigBear 2.0, an Evilginx2-based phishing-as-a-service platform, bypassed Microsoft 365 MFA at 258 organizations by stealing passwords and authenticated session cookies. Defenders should revoke sessions, reset exposed credentials, and enforce phishing-resistant authentication such as FIDO2/WebAuthn with managed-device conditional access.

A phishing-as-a-service framework called BigBear 2.0 has been used to bypass multi-factor authentication at 258 organizations and steal more than 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials. CloudSEK researchers gained administrator access to the control panel and found 42 VPS nodes configured to target Microsoft 365. The operation affected victims across more than 40 countries and was still active at the time of CloudSEK’s writing.

According to CloudSEK, BigBear uses an Evilginx2-based adversary-in-the-middle framework to intercept passwords and authenticated session cookies after victims complete MFA. The service uses a configuration called “offy” to place a proxy between the victim and Microsoft’s legitimate authentication infrastructure. CloudSEK reported 5,137 credential records, including 474 complete MFA-bypassed authentications, 1,032 plaintext passwords, and 4,148 session cookies.

The article says BigBear also uses custom JavaScript to interfere with FIDO2/WebAuthn authentication and geo-matched residential proxies for 69 countries to reduce suspicious signals during Microsoft authentication. Compromising a Microsoft 365 session can expose email and files and may provide access to other applications connected through single sign-on. CloudSEK notified law enforcement and several affected organizations, and potentially affected organizations were advised to reset exposed passwords, revoke active sessions, refresh tokens, and force re-authentication for high-privileged accounts.

  • BigBear’s custom JavaScript specifically interferes with FIDO2/WebAuthn browser functionality, showing the campaign attempted to push victims away from stronger authentication methods toward weaker ones.
  • The use of geo-matched residential proxies across 69 countries means location-based trust signals alone are not sufficient, because the attackers tried to make sign-ins appear to come from the victim’s region.
  • CloudSEK’s finding that the panel served at least five affiliate operators through Telegram exfiltration bots indicates BigBear operated as a shared phishing service rather than a single-operator campaign.
  • The article recommends requiring managed devices through Conditional Access policies, which is more actionable than relying on geo-location signals that BigBear was designed to evade.
  • Although the administration panel remained online, the phishing infrastructure had been offline for nearly three weeks at the time of writing, creating a distinction between the service’s control layer and its active phishing delivery infrastructure.
Key Points
  • 01A phishing-as-a-service framework called BigBear 2.0 has been used to bypass multi-factor authentication at 258 organizations and steal more than 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials.
  • 02CloudSEK researchers gained administrator access to the BigBear control panel and found 42 VPS nodes configured to target Microsoft 365 in the observed operation.
  • 03BigBear uses an Evilginx2-based adversary-in-the-middle framework to intercept passwords and authenticated session cookies, allowing attackers to hijack accounts after victims complete MFA.
  • 04CloudSEK reported that the panel exfiltrated 5,137 credential records, including 474 complete MFA-bypassed authentications, 1,032 plaintext passwords, and 4,148 session cookies affecting 3,331 unique victim IPs across more than 40 countries.
  • 05CloudSEK found that BigBear uses custom JavaScript to interfere with FIDO2/WebAuthn authentication and geo-matched residential proxies for 69 countries to reduce suspicious signals during Microsoft authentication.
