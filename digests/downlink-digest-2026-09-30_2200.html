In plain words

The U.S. government sanctioned 10 people and several companies it says helped a Venezuelan criminal group profit from breaking into cash machines and making them dispense all their money. Officials tracked at least 1,500 such incidents, with losses of $40.7 million, affecting banks and other operators of cash machines. The case matters because investigators say stolen money was hidden through digital currency and businesses in several countries, showing how organized crime can use technology and financial networks to move money across borders.

Summary
The U.S. Treasury sanctioned 10 people and related companies allegedly tied to Tren de Aragua for laundering proceeds from Ploutus-powered ATM jackpotting attacks that caused at least $40.7 million in losses. Authorities say the network used cryptocurrency and businesses across Latin America to move stolen cash, though independent experts have not confirmed direct links between Ploutus and the gang.

The U.S. Treasury Department sanctioned 10 people and several companies it says participated in an ATM cash-dispensing scheme tied to Venezuela’s Tren de Aragua criminal group. The sanctions target Venezuelan nationals and entities allegedly used to launder money stolen from ATMs. U.S. officials have tracked at least 1,500 incidents causing $40.7 million in losses, and Treasury described the activity as a key revenue source for the group.

The scheme allegedly used Ploutus software after attackers physically opened ATMs, connected laptops, and installed the software to force machines to dispense their cash. Authorities said proceeds were transferred to Tren de Aragua members and concealed through cryptocurrency or companies in Mexico and other countries; Chainalysis said the funds moved through channels also used for drug-trafficking money. At least 98 people have been indicted and five men pleaded guilty to related charges last month, although the article notes that experts have not found links between Ploutus and either Anibal Alexander Canelon Aguirre or Tren de Aragua.

  • The alleged laundering network used shared cryptocurrency infrastructure that Chainalysis said also handled money connected to Colombian and Mexican drug cartels.
  • The ATM theft method described required physical access to machines: suspects allegedly opened the top of ATMs, connected a laptop, and installed software that forced cash dispensing.
  • Although U.S. agencies allege links between Ploutus, Anibal Alexander Canelon Aguirre, and Tren de Aragua, the article states that experts have not found links between the malware and Aguirre or the group.
  • The government response extends beyond sanctions: at least 98 people have been indicted, and five men pleaded guilty to related charges last month.
