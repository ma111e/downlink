In plain words

A fake invoice email pretending to come from an accounting brand was sent to a shared accounts-payable mailbox at a financial-advisory firm. Microsoft first caught it and held it back because the sender checks failed, but six days later it reached the inbox after being released from quarantine by a process tied to an internal mailbox. This matters because the email had no link or attachment for scanners to inspect; its goal was simply to get someone to reply to an attacker-controlled address, which is a common path into invoice fraud and business email scams.

Summary
A spoofed QuickBooks-style invoice email that failed SPF, had no DKIM, and failed DMARC under a reject policy was correctly quarantined by Microsoft but later delivered after being resubmitted from quarantine by an internal account. The key risk is that quarantine release can undo strong authentication enforcement, especially for payload-free invoice fraud that relies only on getting a victim to reply.

A spoofed accounting-brand invoice notice reached a shared accounts-payable mailbox at a financial-advisory firm after it had already been quarantined by Microsoft’s antispam engine. The original evaluation showed SPF failed, there was no DKIM signature, and DMARC failed against a sending domain that published an enforced reject policy. The article says the receiver did not refuse the message at the SMTP layer, but quarantined it, which made later release possible.

The delivered copy contained headers showing it was a resubmission rather than a normal inbound delivery. These included an Exchange generated-message-source value of Antispam Quarantine Agent, Auto-Submitted: auto-generated, and an X-MS-Exchange-QuarantineResubmitTime header. Other headers attributed the release to an internal mailbox at the firm, though the article says they do not show whether it was an end-user self-service release or an administrator action.

The message had no attachment, link, embedded image, or tracking pixel, and its only attack surface was a reply address at accounts@ap2-linkedin[.]com. That domain was created on April 16, 2026 through Dynadot behind a privacy service and had no relationship to the spoofed accounting brand or LinkedIn. The article’s main takeaway is that a recorded authentication failure and quarantine decision can be undone by later release, so organizations should monitor quarantine resubmission headers and verify new payment or remittance contacts through an already known channel.

  • A published DMARC reject policy did not guarantee non-delivery because the receiver applied a local override to quarantine, and quarantined messages can later be released.
  • The released message could appear better authenticated to downstream controls because a later ARC hop recorded a DKIM pass from the recipient organization’s own internal forwarding domain, while the original failure remained deeper in the headers.
  • Quarantine release should be treated as a privileged action for messages that originally failed DMARC under an enforced policy, rather than as a routine self-service convenience.
  • A resubmit timestamp combined with an original failing authentication verdict is described as a high-confidence, easily matchable signal for detecting messages that were released after being blocked.
  • For payload-free invoice fraud, the article emphasizes detecting sender-brand and reply-path mismatches, because there may be no attachment, link, image, or redirect chain for security tools to analyze.
