Your Own Mailing List Signed the Phishing Email

In plain words

A phishing email was sent to an equipment rental company’s internal accounting group, which automatically forwarded it to four employees and made it appear to have passed through the company’s own email system. The message used a fake shared-spreadsheet invitation and a link that redirected through legitimate services to a compromised business website, aiming to steal credentials. This matters because criminals can abuse open internal mailing groups and other innocent companies’ systems, making dangerous messages look more trustworthy and harder to block based only on where they came from.

Summary
A phishing email authenticated by a legitimate external provider was accepted by an externally open Google Workspace accounting group, which redistributed and DKIM-signed it internally to four recipients. Restrict external posting to internal distribution groups and investigate cases where your own domain signs a message whose visible From address is external.

An externally authenticated phishing email was sent to a regional equipment rental company’s accounting distribution group in Google Workspace, which redistributed it to four accounting mailboxes in the same second. The message presented a shared-spreadsheet lure with a single button and displayed the accounting list’s own address as the person sharing the file. No compromised employee mailbox was required; the group’s ability to accept external posts enabled the internal fan-out.

Google Groups rewrote the envelope sender, applied a DKIM signature using the recipient company’s domain, sealed upstream authentication information in ARC, and appended a list footer that caused its own DKIM signature to fail at delivery. The resulting SPF pass, DKIM fail, DMARC fail, and ARC pass combination resembled ordinary mailing-list forwarding, even though the message had entered from outside the organization. The button redirected through a shared marketing click tracker to a compromised WordPress path on a real manufacturer’s website, while the article states that the consumer ISP, tracker, Google Workspace tenant, and manufacturer site were not attacker-owned. The recommended remediation is to restrict external posting to distribution groups that do not need it and to scrutinize messages signed by the recipient’s own domain when their visible From address is external.

  • A combination of SPF pass, DKIM fail, DMARC fail, and ARC pass can be normal mailing-list behavior, but it becomes suspicious when the recipient organization’s own domain signs a message whose visible From address is external.
  • The attacker did not need a compromised employee mailbox to reach four accounting recipients; an externally open internal group provided the fan-out mechanism.
  • Infrastructure-based reputation was less useful because the sender, click tracker, and compromised destination were legitimate third-party services rather than attacker-owned assets.
  • Both relevant domains used DMARC monitor-only policies, so authentication failures were logged but were not configured to trigger rejection.
  • The article attributes the phishing verdict primarily to language, sender inconsistencies, and community evidence from similar messages seen in other organizations, rather than to a single authentication or reputation signal.
Key Points
  • 01An externally authenticated phishing email was accepted by a regional equipment rental company’s Google Workspace accounting group and redistributed to four accounting mailboxes in the same second.
  • 02Google Groups rewrote the message envelope, applied a DKIM signature for the recipient company’s domain, added an ARC chain, and appended a list footer that caused its own DKIM signature to fail at delivery.
  • 03The email’s button redirected through a shared marketing-platform click tracker to a compromised WordPress administrative path on a real manufacturing business’s website.
  • 04The article states that neither the consumer ISP used for sending, the marketing click tracker, the recipient’s Google Workspace tenant, nor the compromised manufacturer website belonged to the attacker.
  • 05The recommended remediation is to restrict external posting to distribution groups that do not need to receive email from outside the organization.
