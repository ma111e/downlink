Open VSX Unblocks Extension IDs Used in Malware Campaign
socket.dev · 24 Aug 2026 05:48 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.5
TAGScarbon-vscodeeclipse-foundationjsoniq-vscodemanifold-securitynamesquattingopen-vsxopen-vsx-evil-twin-campaignopm-flow-editor-supportreact-hooks-snippetssocketvisual-studio-codevs-code-marketplace

In plain words

Open VSX, a place where people get editor add-ons, removed blocks on three add-on names that had earlier been used by copycats in a malware campaign, so the real maintainers could publish their legitimate versions. This affects extension maintainers and organizations using Open VSX, because a name that once pointed to harmful code can later point to real software. The broader issue is that tracking only the add-on name is not enough; people also need details like the exact version, file identity, publisher, and source location to tell past harmful copies apart from current legitimate ones.

Summary
Open VSX unblocked three extension IDs that had been abused in an evil-twin malware campaign so legitimate maintainers could reclaim and publish them. The case shows why teams should track extension versions, hashes, publishers, and repositories—not just extension IDs—because a once-malicious name can later point to legitimate software.

Open VSX unblocked three extension IDs between August 16 and August 20: AlDuncanson.react-hooks-snippets, magne-sjaastad.opm-flow-editor-support, and rumbledb.jsoniq-vscode. All three IDs had been used by impostors in the 77-extension evil-twin campaign documented by Manifold Security earlier in the month. Legitimate versions of the OPM Flow Editor Support and RumbleDB JSONiq extensions are now live, while React Hooks Snippets had been unblocked but had not appeared on Open VSX as of publication.

The case shows a problem caused by namesquatting across the VS Code Marketplace and Open VSX, where attackers used names already established in Microsoft’s marketplace but not yet claimed by their real owners on Open VSX. Open VSX’s malicious-extension list stores entries as extension ID strings without version, file hash, publisher account, repository, or date, which becomes ambiguous when a legitimate owner reclaims a previously abused name. The article recommends that organizations record extension IDs together with exact versions, file hashes, publisher identities, and source repositories so historical deny rules remain tied to the malicious artifact rather than the reused name.

  • An ID-only malicious-extension list can create a conflict between blocking known bad packages and allowing legitimate maintainers to reclaim their names, because the same identifier can represent both a past malicious artifact and a current legitimate extension.
  • The Open VSX Git history preserves evidence of previously blocked malicious IDs, but security tools or inventories that store only the current extension ID may lose that historical distinction once the ID is removed from the current blocklist.
  • The article highlights a marketplace-specific risk: attackers abused names that were established in Microsoft’s VS Code Marketplace but had not yet been claimed by the real owners on Open VSX.
  • Maintainers can reduce the chance of namesquatting by claiming their Open VSX namespace even if Open VSX is not their primary distribution channel.
  • Organizations transferring extension recommendations or configuration files from VS Code to an editor that uses Open VSX should verify the publisher, because matching extension names across marketplaces do not guarantee matching owners or code.
Key Points
  • 01Open VSX unblocked the extension IDs AlDuncanson.react-hooks-snippets, magne-sjaastad.opm-flow-editor-support, and rumbledb.jsoniq-vscode between August 16 and August 20 after all three had been used by impostors in a 77-extension evil-twin campaign documented by Manifold Security.
  • 02Legitimate versions of OPM Flow Editor Support and RumbleDB's JSONiq and XQuery extension are now live on Open VSX, while React Hooks Snippets had been unblocked but did not yet have a legitimate Open VSX listing as of publication.
  • 03Open VSX had removed all 77 malicious packages by August 3 and added their IDs to its malicious-extension list, but the legitimate publishers were not compromised and were not involved in the campaign.
  • 04The article says Open VSX's malicious-extension list stores only extension ID strings, which becomes ambiguous when a legitimate owner reclaims an ID that was previously used for malicious code.
  • 05The article recommends that organizations using Open VSX record extension IDs together with exact versions, file hashes, publisher identities, and source repositories so historical deny rules remain tied to the malicious artifact rather than the reused name.

The Rise of Service-Centric Credential Compilations
constella.ai · 24 Aug 2026 05:00 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.5
TAGSchatgptclaudegeminigoogle-workspaceinitial-access-brokersmicrosoft-365netflixsession-hijacking
55
In plain words

The article says criminals are taking stolen login data from infected computers and repackaging it into ready-made bundles for specific services like ChatGPT, Netflix, Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, and social media platforms. These bundles can include not just passwords but also pieces of browser data that may let someone get into an account that is already signed in. This matters because accounts for AI tools like ChatGPT can contain private company information, source code, legal documents, financial analysis, and confidential conversations. Employees and organizations using these services could have sensitive business knowledge exposed, which could help criminals plan more effective scams or other attacks.

Summary
Cybercriminals are increasingly turning raw infostealer logs into service-specific packages that bundle credentials with cookies, tokens, and session data, making account takeover faster and more reliable. The biggest concern is AI platforms like ChatGPT, where stolen sessions can expose sensitive corporate knowledge and require defenders to protect and revoke active sessions, not just passwords.

Cybercriminals are increasingly repackaging raw infostealer logs into service-specific compilations focused on platforms such as ChatGPT, Netflix, Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, and social media services. These datasets are curated packages containing information tied to a particular online service rather than random collections of credentials from infected endpoints. The article describes this as part of the professionalization of the cybercriminal ecosystem and a shift toward selling ready-to-use access instead of raw data.

Initial Access Brokers are playing a growing role by enriching stolen information before selling it to other criminals. These packages may combine fresh credentials with browser cookies, persistent session cookies, refresh tokens, JWTs, CSRF tokens, device identifiers, and other authentication artifacts recovered from a victim’s browser. Because these artifacts can represent an already authenticated session, attackers may be able to reuse access while the tokens remain valid, depending on the service’s authentication model.

The article highlights particular concern around AI platforms such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and other enterprise AI assistants because compromised accounts may expose sensitive corporate knowledge, proprietary source code, strategic plans, financial analysis, legal documents, internal procedures, and confidential conversations. Access to these services can support intelligence gathering, social engineering, exposure of development practices, disclosure of customer information, or visibility into strategic initiatives. The article recommends rapid session invalidation and token revocation, continuous monitoring of exposed identities, adaptive MFA and device-risk analysis, and visibility into underground marketplaces where these compilations are traded.

  • The criminal market is shifting from selling raw stolen data to selling ready-to-use access packages, which reduces the buyer’s effort and increases the chance of successful account compromise.
  • Password resets alone may be insufficient after exposure because valid browser cookies and authentication tokens can preserve access to active sessions.
  • AI assistant accounts are becoming especially valuable targets because their conversation histories may function as repositories of corporate intellectual property and internal business context.
  • Fresh stolen data can move through multiple criminal resellers within hours of the original infection, making rapid session invalidation and token revocation time-sensitive.
  • The article frames cookies, tokens, and active sessions as assets that require protection comparable to passwords, expanding the scope of identity security beyond traditional credentials.
Key Points
  • 01Cybercriminals are increasingly organizing raw infostealer logs into service-specific compilations focused on platforms such as ChatGPT, Netflix, Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, and social media services.
  • 02These curated packages may include credentials, browser cookies, persistent session cookies, refresh tokens, JWTs, CSRF tokens, device identifiers, and other authentication artifacts recovered from victims’ browsers.
  • 03Initial Access Brokers are enriching stolen information by combining fresh credentials from infostealer infections with browser artifacts, session cookies, and authentication tokens before selling it to other criminals.
  • 04Compromised AI platform accounts can expose sensitive corporate knowledge, proprietary source code, strategic business plans, financial analysis, legal documents, internal procedures, and confidential conversations.
  • 05The article recommends rapid session invalidation and token revocation, continuous monitoring of exposed identities, adaptive MFA and device-risk analysis, and visibility into underground marketplaces where service-centric compilations are traded.
