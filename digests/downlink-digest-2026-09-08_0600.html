In plain words

Adobe released a fix for a serious problem in Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source, software used by online stores. Attackers are already using the problem to break into affected store sites, run their own code, and install hidden tools that let them keep control. Online merchants using the listed versions are affected, and Adobe says they should apply the patch and rotate their encryption keys.

Summary
Adobe released an emergency hotfix for a critical Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source zero-day, CVE-2026-75650, that is already being exploited to achieve unauthenticated remote code execution. Attackers have used the flaw to deploy a Rust-based Linux backdoor and PHP web shells, so affected merchants should apply the VULN-39341 patch and rotate encryption keys immediately.

Adobe released security patches for a maximum-severity flaw affecting Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source that is being actively exploited in the wild. The vulnerability is tracked as CVE-2026-75650, has a CVSS score of 10.0, and has been codenamed StyleSmuggler by Sansec. Adobe said the flaw could result in arbitrary code execution and that it is aware of exploitation targeting Adobe Commerce merchants.

The flaw abuses Magento’s template system through PHP code injection to generate a “Payment Transaction Failed Reminder” email, which triggers code execution. Affected products include multiple versions of Adobe Commerce, Adobe Commerce B2B, and Magento Open Source up to the listed August 2026 releases. Adobe said affected users must apply the VULN-39341 patch for their version and rotate their encryption keys.

Sansec reported zero-day exploitation beginning on September 4, 2026, and said attackers used the vulnerability to deploy a Rust-based Linux backdoor that connects to an external server and waits for instructions. The issue has also been used to deliver a PHP dropper on vulnerable sites that writes a web shell capable of executing arbitrary PHP code. Disrex said a Magento server it managed was compromised 50 minutes after the first confirmed StyleSmuggler exploitation was reported.

  • Applying the hotfix alone is not the full remediation Adobe recommends; affected merchants must also rotate their encryption keys.
  • Systems that had already installed the August 2026 releases are still listed as affected, so being recently patched does not rule out exposure.
  • The reported compromise of a Magento server 50 minutes after the first confirmed StyleSmuggler exploitation shows that vulnerable sites may have been targeted very quickly once exploitation began.
  • Attackers used more than one follow-on payload, including a Rust-based Linux backdoor and a PHP web shell, so defenders may need to look for multiple signs of compromise rather than a single malware artifact.

In plain words

Researchers found a long-running operation called BengalSEO that pushes fake help and download pages to the top of Bing search results, including pages pretending to help with services like Bitdefender, smart TVs, streaming, tax tools, games, and gift cards. People who click these results can be sent through several hidden websites and end up downloading harmful software called MayaBot, getting a cryptocurrency miner installed, or being tricked into calling a scam support number. It matters because the group uses well-known sites like GitHub, Google Sites, Read the Docs, and Cloudflare-hosted pages to look trustworthy, making ordinary search results harder to trust. The article also notes a separate campaign abusing Brazilian government and school websites to boost fake app-store pages, showing how trusted websites can be misused to reach many more victims.

Summary
Researchers detailed BengalSEO, a long-running India-linked SEO poisoning operation that manipulates Bing results with fake support and software pages to deliver MayaBot malware, XMRig miners, or tech support scams. The campaign uses trusted hosting platforms, cloaking, CAPTCHA challenges, and traffic distribution systems to filter victims and evade detection.

Cybersecurity researchers disclosed details of a search engine optimization poisoning campaign called BengalSEO that enables malware delivery and tech support scams. The DFIR Report discovered the campaign in March 2026 and said it has operated out of Rajasthan, India since at least 2015. The operation is linked to WeConnect Solutions LLC and Garage2Global, with evidence indicating that Garage2Global develops malicious web infrastructure used in the campaigns.

BengalSEO creates and promotes fake lure pages that appear high in Microsoft Bing search results for technical support, service activation, software downloads, tax utilities, gaming software, antivirus tools, and card activation topics. Victims are routed through redirector domains and filtering systems before reaching payload pages or scam contact pages. One payload is MayaBot, a custom malware used for command-and-control, system monitoring, and delivery of an XMRig cryptocurrency miner.

The campaign uses black hat SEO techniques including backlinks, DOM injection, DOM shuffling, keyword stuffing, and user-generated content spam to boost search visibility and evade spam filters. Its traffic distribution system uses redirect chains, Cloudflare Turnstile or hCaptcha challenges, and Matomo tracking to profile visitors and screen out unwanted traffic. The article also describes a separate Check Point Research report on Gambling Goblin, which has targeted Brazilian government and educational websites with malicious Apache modules to manipulate search rankings and proxy visitors to phishing pages.

  • BengalSEO’s use of legitimate hosting platforms such as github.io, pages.dev, sites.google.com, and readthedocs.io shows that high-reputation services can help malicious lure pages rank well and appear trustworthy to users.
  • The group constantly updates GitHub-hosted lure pages to rotate redirector domains or temporarily replace them with legitimate URLs, indicating that takedowns and blocking are actively anticipated in its workflow.
  • Matomo tracking on lure and landing pages, including the domain stats.us3.org with more than 1,100 urlscan.io results, provides a concrete infrastructure clue for hunting BengalSEO-linked activity.
  • The campaign does not rely on a single end goal: the same search-poisoning and redirection infrastructure can lead either to MayaBot payload delivery or to tech support scam phone pages.
  • The separate Gambling Goblin activity shows a related SEO-abuse pattern in which trusted Brazilian government and educational websites are hijacked so attacker-controlled phishing pages inherit the legitimate domain’s reputation.
Key Points
  • 01The DFIR Report disclosed a search engine optimization poisoning campaign called BengalSEO that has operated out of Rajasthan, India since at least 2015 and is tied to WeConnect Solutions LLC and Garage2Global.
  • 02BengalSEO promotes malicious lure pages in Microsoft Bing search results to deliver MayaBot malware, deploy an XMRig cryptocurrency miner, or redirect victims to tech support scam call centers.
  • 03The campaign uses black hat SEO techniques including backlinks, DOM injection, DOM shuffling, keyword stuffing, and user-generated content spam to boost lure pages and evade spam filters.
  • 04BengalSEO uses a traffic distribution system with redirector chains, Cloudflare Turnstile or hCaptcha challenges, and Matomo tracking to filter, fingerprint, and route visitors to final landing pages.
  • 05Check Point Research also reported a separate Gambling Goblin campaign that compromises Brazilian government and educational websites with malicious Apache modules to proxy visitors to phishing pages for SEO manipulation.

In plain words

A travel records system linked to Vietnam was left reachable online because of setup mistakes, exposing more than 220 million passenger and crew records with names, birth dates, passport details, and flight information. The records could involve people of many nationalities who flew to, from, or through Vietnam between 2017 and 2026. The problem matters because this kind of personal and travel information could be misused if someone copied it, and researchers cannot confirm whether that happened before access was fixed.

Summary
A misconfigured Elasticsearch APIS database linked to Vietnam exposed over 220 million passenger and crew travel records, including passport details and flight information spanning 2017 to 2026. Researchers said access was secured on June 8, but it is unknown whether anyone copied or abused the data before then.

An Advance Passenger Information System database containing more than 220 million passenger and crew records was accessible online through a chain of security misconfigurations. The system appears linked to a Vietnamese organization, and the records span January 2017 to April 2026. The exposed data could involve travelers of many nationalities who flew to, from, or through Vietnam during that period.

Kinryū Labs discovered the Elasticsearch cluster, named pax-info, on June 3 while surveying exposed databases as part of research into ransomware activity. The cluster contained 29 indices and about 107 GB of data, including 210,318,069 passenger records and 10,465,631 crew records. The exposed information included names, dates of birth, sex, nationalities, passport or travel-document numbers, document expiration dates, issuing countries, and flight details such as airlines, routes, seat assignments, baggage references, and flight times.

Researchers said the database was reachable by chaining two misconfigurations: the direct internet endpoint returned an HTTP 401 response, but a cloud-based path led to the cluster, which accepted default credentials. Kinryū Labs reported the issue to Vietnamese authorities, airlines represented in the database, and national computer emergency response teams beginning June 3, and said access was remediated on June 8. It remains unclear whether the database was downloaded, sold, ransomed, or otherwise exploited before it was secured, because the researchers did not have access to server logs.

  • The exposure was not simply an open database on the public internet; researchers said access required chaining a cloud-based path with default credentials after the direct endpoint returned an HTTP 401 response.
  • The dataset’s size does not equal the number of affected people, because the article states the 220 million entries are travel records and repeat travelers or crew may appear multiple times.
  • Although the records span more than nine years, the article says the actual duration of public retrievability is unknown because researchers could not determine when the second access path became usable.
  • The article notes there is no indication that the airlines whose passenger records appeared in the database operated the exposed system or had their own networks compromised.
  • Researchers found no ransom notes, unfamiliar indices, or online sale listing for the dataset, but they could not determine whether the data had been copied because they lacked server logs.
Key Points
  • 01Kinryū Labs discovered an exposed Elasticsearch cluster named “pax-info” on June 3 that contained 210,318,069 passenger records and 10,465,631 crew records.
  • 02The exposed records included names, dates of birth, sex, nationalities, passport or travel-document numbers, document expiration dates, issuing countries, and associated flight details.
  • 03The records span January 2017 to April 2026 and could involve travelers of many nationalities who flew to, from, or through Vietnam during that period.
  • 04Kinryū Labs said the database was reachable through a cloud-based path and accepted default credentials, despite the direct internet endpoint returning an HTTP 401 response.
  • 05Kinryū Labs reported the issue beginning June 3, and the researchers said access to the database was remediated on June 8.
