UK faces ‘unprecedented rise’ in cyberattacks in coming months, warns cyber chief

In plain words

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre says Britain could soon see a major increase in computer security incidents because artificial intelligence can help find and abuse old, unfixed flaws much faster. The warning affects government, critical services, businesses, and the public, since successful disruptions could leave food unavailable, turn off lights, or cause companies to fail. The government is testing AI tools to find weaknesses in its own systems and plans to expand them across important national services, but says this will require investment and prompt upgrades to outdated technology.

Summary
The UK’s NCSC warns that AI will soon enable attackers to find and exploit long-neglected weaknesses at unprecedented scale, potentially driving a sharp rise in cyber incidents. The government is responding with AI-based initiatives including Project DROMOS, the NERV Centre, and Cyber Shield to identify and block threats.

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) will warn that Britain faces the real prospect of an unprecedented increase in cyber incidents in the coming months. Chief Executive Richard Horne says the risk comes not from AI inventing entirely new attack methods, but from its ability to find and exploit long-tolerated weaknesses at a scale previously impossible. The agency says major technology providers are fixing hundreds of flaws in single updates, while the Linux kernel project patched more than a thousand vulnerabilities in one month.

The NCSC assesses that AI-powered malware is highly likely to be used in a ransomware attack on UK critical infrastructure by 2028, and that China and Russia will routinely use AI in cyber operations by then. It says many state-linked intrusions still rely on internet-facing applications and publicly available patches that organizations have not installed quickly enough, reflecting technical debt and delayed upgrades. To improve defence, the government is testing AI through Project DROMOS, plans a £70 million National Exposure and Real-Time Vulnerability Centre, and has allocated £60 million for the first phase of Cyber Shield, which will use AI agents to detect and block attacks.

  • The NCSC attributes the near-term risk primarily to long-tolerated and unpatched weaknesses becoming discoverable at machine scale, rather than to AI creating entirely new attack methods.
  • Although vendors are finding and fixing vulnerabilities faster, the article notes that experts have not yet observed a matching increase in exploitation, suggesting the warning is based on an accelerating exposure trend rather than a documented surge in attacks.
  • Project DROMOS was expanded from a planned four-week pilot into live government environments after it identified hundreds of potential security issues, including critical flaws.
  • The NCSC believes defenders retain a structural advantage because they control and can monitor their own environments, but says that advantage depends on investment, clear direction, and disciplined use of AI.
  • The article links insecure technology to commercial decisions such as operating end-of-life systems and deferring upgrades for cost reasons, despite available patches and known ways to build secure products.
Key Points
  • 01NCSC chief executive Richard Horne will warn that the UK faces a real prospect of an unprecedented rise in cyber incidents in the coming months as AI enables vulnerabilities to be found and exploited at far greater scale.
  • 02The NCSC says AI did not create existing security weaknesses, but is exposing years of technical debt and failures to implement basic protections, including unpatched internet-facing applications.
  • 03The agency assesses that AI-powered malware is highly likely to be used in a ransomware attack on UK critical infrastructure by 2028, and that China and Russia will routinely use AI in cyber operations by then.
  • 04The UK government has tested frontier AI systems through Project DROMOS, which found hundreds of potential security issues, including critical vulnerabilities, in live government environments.
  • 05The NCSC plans to fund the £70 million NERV Centre and the first £60 million phase of Cyber Shield to use AI for identifying weaknesses and detecting and blocking attacks across the UK.

In plain words

Unit 42 says recent cyber threats have largely involved government-backed groups seeking sensitive information and criminal groups stealing data, demanding money, or redirecting payments. These activities can affect organizations whose accounts, systems, or business communications are exposed, while newer AI tools let criminals create more convincing fake messages and identities, write harmful code faster, and impersonate executives more easily. The stakes are loss of confidential information, stolen funds, and longer-lasting access to an organization’s systems.

Summary
Unit 42 reports that cyberespionage and financially motivated groups are increasingly combining stealthy intrusion methods, credential theft, data extortion, and fraud with AI-assisted phishing, scripting, reconnaissance, and impersonation.

Unit 42’s Nov. 2, 2026 threat-intelligence overview identifies strategic intelligence collection and financial extortion as prominent recent adversary activities. It also highlights the cross-cutting use of artificial intelligence tools across multiple threat disciplines. The article summarizes observations from in-depth threat-intelligence bulletins available to Unit 42 Threat Intelligence Services customers.

State-sponsored and advanced persistent threat campaigns sought strategic intelligence, intellectual property, and geopolitical data. Observed behaviors included use of built-in administration tools to evade endpoint defenses, exploitation of zero-day vulnerabilities, targeted spear phishing, credential harvesting, and gradual data staging for long-term persistence. Financially motivated groups used data theft, extortion, ransomware or public-exposure threats, exposed credentials, infostealer logs, unpatched perimeter assets, business email compromise, and payment-diversion schemes.

The overview also reports that threat actors are incorporating generative AI and large language models into offensive workflows. These tools were used to generate tailored social-engineering lures and synthetic personas, assist with scripting, payload debugging, and code obfuscation, and support automated reconnaissance and executive impersonation through multimodal tools such as deepfakes. Unit 42 characterizes AI-enabled activity as an emerging trend that spans multiple attacker motivations.

  • The article shows that the same AI capabilities are being applied across several stages of harmful activity, from creating convincing messages and fake identities to reconnaissance, scripting, debugging, and disguising code.
  • Financially motivated groups are not relying on a single pressure tactic: they combine stolen data with ransomware deployment or threats to publish the data.
  • Long-term espionage activity is designed to be difficult to notice, using legitimate built-in administration tools and slowly preparing stolen data rather than moving it all at once.
  • Initial access is frequently enabled by already-compromised credentials, including logs collected by information-stealing software, exposed passwords, and unpatched internet-facing systems.
Key Points
  • 01Unit 42 highlighted recent threat activity centered on strategic intelligence collection and financially motivated extortion, alongside growing use of AI tools across multiple threat disciplines.
  • 02State-sponsored and advanced persistent threat campaigns used built-in system administration tools, zero-day vulnerabilities, targeted phishing, credential harvesting, and gradual data staging to maintain long-term access.
  • 03Cybercrime groups used multi-extortion approaches involving data theft, ransomware or public-exposure threats, while gaining access through infostealer logs, exposed credentials, and unpatched internet-facing assets.
  • 04Criminals also used business email compromise and payment-diversion schemes to carry out fraudulent wire transfers.
  • 05Threat actors used generative AI and large language models to create tailored social-engineering lures and fake identities, assist with scripting and code obfuscation, and support reconnaissance and executive impersonation with tools such as deepfakes.
