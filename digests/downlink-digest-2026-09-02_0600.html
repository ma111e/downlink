UK Cybercrime Journal: ExfilSquad Emerges
blog.bushidotoken.net · 02 Sep 2026 09:00 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.5

In plain words

A new criminal group called ExfilSquad stole data from several UK organizations, including the Department for Education, the Police National Legal Database, and Newcastle University, and threatened to publish it if they were not paid. The people affected include parents, staff, students, applicants, police officers, civil servants, and criminal justice professionals whose names, contact details, or related records were exposed. This matters because the stolen information could be used for scams, targeted messages, or safety risks long after the original break-in.

Summary
ExfilSquad is targeting UK public sector, education, and law enforcement organizations with data-theft-only extortion, exploiting exposed SaaS, CRM, and Microsoft Power Pages configurations rather than deploying ransomware. The key risk is that misconfigured cloud-facing business portals can leak large volumes of sensitive contact and administrative data, which the group then distributes via torrents to increase pressure on victims.

In mid-2026, an emerging cybercriminal group called ExfilSquad launched a high-profile extortion campaign against prominent UK organisations in the public sector, education, and law enforcement, as well as other firms worldwide. The group does not use encryptors or destructive malware, but instead steals data and threatens to publish it on an onion-based Data Leak Site if victims do not pay. Confirmed UK victims include the Department for Education, the Police National Legal Database, and Newcastle University.

The Department for Education had approximately 600,000 Help Portal records stolen, including parent and staff contact details, along with around 7,000 records from the Turing Portal. The Police National Legal Database lost 1.9 GB of data, including around 135,000 records with contact information for over 100,000 serving police officers, staff, and criminal justice professionals, plus around 21,000 “Ask the Police” public inquiry records. Newcastle University reported approximately 440,000 compromised records involving applicant and student contact information, personally identifiable information, and admissions database records, caused by a technical configuration flaw connected to an admissions system.

Analysis of the group’s leak site details indicated that ExfilSquad’s main attack vector involves exploiting misconfigurations in cloud portals, CRM platforms, internal case management systems, and Microsoft Power Pages data tables left publicly accessible without proper authentication. To prove its claims and pressure victims, ExfilSquad uploaded multi-gigabyte torrent files for each victim to its TOR leak site, with Resecurity noting that the group assigns a distinct Torrent Tracker and initial Web Seed per victim. The article says this approach makes leaked files distribute rapidly across peer-to-peer networks and creates lasting risks such as social engineering, spear-phishing, and physical security concerns for affected organisations and individuals.

  • ExfilSquad’s activity shows that organizations can face ransomware-style extortion even when no files are encrypted and no malware is deployed, because exposed cloud and SaaS data can be enough to support a public leak threat.
  • The article highlights Microsoft Power Pages, CRM databases, helpdesks, admissions portals, and case management systems as high-risk data stores because they often contain large volumes of sensitive contact and interaction records while being internet-facing.
  • ExfilSquad’s use of separate Torrent Trackers and Web Seeds for each victim makes leaked data harder to contain because distribution can spread rapidly across peer-to-peer networks.
  • Contact-directory breaches can create lasting real-world risk even when the stolen data is not described as highly sensitive, because exposed names, work emails, and organizational structures can support social engineering, spear-phishing, and physical security threats.
  • Incident response plans need to account for data-theft-only extortion and torrent-based leak distribution, not just endpoint malware or file-encrypting ransomware scenarios.
Key Points
  • 01In mid-2026, the cybercriminal group ExfilSquad launched an extortion campaign targeting prominent UK organizations across the public sector, education, and law enforcement, as well as other firms worldwide.
  • 02ExfilSquad operates as a pure data extortion group that steals data and threatens to publish it on its onion-based Data Leak Site if a ransom is not paid, rather than deploying encryptors or destructive malware.
  • 03The UK Department for Education, the Police National Legal Database, and Newcastle University confirmed breaches linked to ExfilSquad involving hundreds of thousands of records and, in the PNLD case, data affecting over 100,000 police officers, staff, and criminal justice professionals.
  • 04Analysis of ExfilSquad’s leak site details indicated that its primary attack vector involves exploiting misconfigurations in cloud portals, CRM platforms, internal case management systems, and publicly accessible Microsoft Power Pages data tables.
  • 05ExfilSquad uploaded multi-gigabyte torrent files for each victim to its TOR leak site and assigned distinct Torrent Trackers and initial Web Seeds per victim.

In plain words

The article says many organizations have written rules for using AI, but very few can automatically enforce those rules when employees type, paste, or receive sensitive information in AI tools. This affects businesses using AI in work such as procurement, financial analysis, and customer support, where customer and operational data may be involved. The real-world concern is that sensitive information can spread through AI tools before security teams notice, because older protections were mainly built to watch files rather than text being copied, rewritten, or summarized.

Summary
Most organizations have AI data security policies, but fewer than one in 10 can enforce them consistently in real time across AI tools. The key risk is that sensitive data now moves through prompts, copy-paste, and AI-generated outputs faster than traditional file-based controls can track or block.

The article argues that many organizations have AI policies on paper but lack consistent technical enforcement in practice. According to the 2026 Unified Data Security Report, nearly half of surveyed organizations still rely on written policies, post-event monitoring, or inconsistently enforced controls. Only 11% enforce data protection policies in real time across most environments, and for AI tools specifically that figure drops to 8%.

The article says this gap matters because AI is becoming embedded in business-critical workflows such as procurement, financial analysis, and customer support, where it can access sensitive operational and customer data. Traditional data protection systems were largely built for files, while sensitive information now often moves through prompts, AI-generated summaries, and copy-pasted excerpts. The article notes that 53% of organizations can consistently govern file uploads to cloud services, but only one in five can apply consistent policy to prompt and text submissions into AI tools.

The article says closing the gap requires inline enforcement at the point of action and data protection that still works after AI changes the format of sensitive information. It describes Netskope One Data Security and Netskope One AI Security as connecting discovery, classification, data loss prevention, lineage, and policy enforcement across environments including email, web, SaaS, cloud storage, private applications, and AI tools. It also cites Netskope’s DLP On Demand integration with Claude Enterprise inference hooks, which inspects prompts and tool-call data before they reach the model and returns an allow or deny verdict based on existing DLP profiles.

  • The article frames AI data risk less as a lack of policy and more as a timing problem: controls that act only after an event are ineffective when sensitive data can be exposed at the moment a prompt is submitted or a response is generated.
  • AI adoption is moving fastest into business-critical workflows such as procurement, financial analysis, and customer support, while enforcement coverage is weakest in AI environments, creating a mismatch between where sensitive data is used and where controls are applied.
  • File-focused data protection can give organizations a false sense of coverage because the original file may never move while its sensitive contents still spread through pasted text, summaries, or rewritten AI outputs.
  • The article suggests that effective AI governance requires recognizing sensitive data even after it changes form, not merely tracking known files or exact content matches.
  • Netskope’s Claude Enterprise integration is presented as an example of moving enforcement directly into the AI interaction path by checking prompts and tool-call data before they reach the model.
Key Points
  • 01Nearly half of surveyed organizations still rely on written policies, post-event monitoring, or inconsistently enforced controls as their primary approach to data security enforcement.
  • 02Only 11% of organizations enforce data protection policies in real time across most environments, and only 8% do so for AI tools specifically.
  • 03Although 67% of organizations maintain a formal AI policy, fewer than one in 10 enforce it consistently in AI environments.
  • 04Most data protection architectures were built for files, while sensitive information increasingly moves through prompts, AI-generated summaries, and copy-pasted excerpts.
  • 05Only one in five organizations can apply consistent policy to prompt and text submissions into AI tools, and fewer than one in ten can recognize sensitive data once it has been modified, reformatted, or partially copied.
