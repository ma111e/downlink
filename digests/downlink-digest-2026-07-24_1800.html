Hermes AI agent used to automate attack on Thai Finance Ministry

In plain words

Researchers found evidence that an attacker used an open-source AI tool called Hermes to help carry out activity against systems connected to Thailand’s Ministry of Finance, though the ministry has not confirmed a breach. The exposed files included tools, logs, stolen credentials, and references to internal ministry systems, suggesting the attacker may have been expanding access inside the network. This matters because it shows how AI tools can help attackers move faster with less hands-on control, raising the risk that real government systems and sensitive records could be targeted more efficiently.

Summary
Attackers reportedly used the open-source Hermes AI agent in unattended “YOLO” mode to automate post-exploitation activity against Thailand’s Ministry of Finance, including privilege-escalation checks, service enumeration, and file system searches. Exposed attacker directories revealed tools, logs, credentials, web shells, and implants, though the ministry has not confirmed a breach.

A threat actor used the open-source Hermes AI agent in unattended “YOLO” mode to automate post-exploitation activity during an alleged breach of Thailand’s Ministry of Finance. Hunt.io and security researcher Bob Diachenko uncovered the activity after finding exposed web directories containing hundreds of files tied to the operation. The Ministry of Finance has not confirmed a breach, and some recovered artifacts only show that specific systems were targeted rather than successfully compromised.

The exposed directories were found on a Hong Kong-hosted server between July 9 and July 13 and contained 585 files totaling about 470 MB. The files included exploit code, web shells, HTTP tunneling tools, custom scripts, stolen credentials, compiled payloads, and Hermes AI agent logs. They referenced Ministry of Finance systems by name, hostname, and internal IP address, and included scripts aimed at services such as Hadoop, Apache Ambari, GlassFish, and an administrative web panel.

Recovered Hermes logs showed the operator enabled YOLO mode, allowing the agent to execute commands without human approval at each step. The agent was used to look for privilege escalation paths, scan for kernel vulnerabilities, enumerate services, inspect containers, search SUID and SGID binaries, and traverse file systems. Hunt.io said the artifacts depicted an active intrusion with staged tools and expanding access, but researchers could not determine how the attackers initially gained access.

  • Hermes did not independently choose the target; the exposed logs showed a human operator supplied objectives and tools while YOLO mode removed repeated approval prompts for routine commands.
  • The exposed attacker infrastructure provided unusually broad visibility into the operation, including session files, deployed web shells, credentials, compiled payloads, and AI agent output logs.
  • Hunt.io linked additional attacker-controlled servers using shared TLS certificate characteristics, including a JA4X fingerprint, and later tied one server to a command-and-control address embedded in a recovered implant.
  • The agent cataloged older personnel and performance documents from a finance office web directory, but Hunt.io said it found no evidence that those files were exfiltrated.

  • 02The exposed files included exploit code, web shells, HTTP tunneling tools, custom scripts, stolen credentials, compiled payloads, and Hermes AI agent logs.
  • 03Recovered Hermes logs showed the operator enabled YOLO mode, allowing the AI agent to run commands without human approval at each step.
  • 04The Hermes agent was used for post-exploitation tasks including privilege-escalation checks, kernel vulnerability scanning, service enumeration, SUID and SGID binary searches, container inspection, and file system traversal.
  • 05Thailand’s Ministry of Finance has not confirmed a breach, and Hunt.io said some artifacts only showed that systems were targeted rather than successfully compromised.

OnTrac notifies customers of data breach after network hack

In plain words

OnTrac, a U.S. parcel delivery company, says hackers got into its company network in March and may have viewed files containing customers’ personal details, including names. The company has not said exactly what other information was exposed or how many customers are affected, but it is offering 12 months of credit monitoring and identity protection. This matters because stolen personal information can be used for fraud, so customers are being told to watch their credit reports and account statements and consider extra protections like a fraud alert or credit freeze.

Summary
OnTrac says hackers breached its corporate network in March and may have accessed customer personal information, though it has not disclosed exactly what data or how many people were affected. The company is offering 12 months of credit monitoring and says it has no evidence the stolen information has been published or misused.

OnTrac is notifying customers that hackers breached its corporate network and may have accessed personal details belonging to customers. The incident was detected on March 23, and an internal investigation found that the attacker accessed certain files between March 20 and March 22. Apart from names, it is unclear what information was exposed because the company redacted the data elements in the notification sample shared with authorities.

OnTrac is a private American parcel-delivery company specializing in last-mile e-commerce deliveries, formed in 2021 from the merger of OnTrac Logistics and LaserShip. The company operates at 102 locations across 35 states, covers roughly 70% of the U.S. population, and works with more than 7,000 independent delivery contractors. In response to the incident, OnTrac hired a third-party specialist to help determine the scope of the breach and said it took steps to ensure the data was re-secured and not distributed.

OnTrac says it is not aware of any fraud or publication of stolen information resulting from the incident and has no reason to believe misuse will occur. The company is offering affected customers 12 months of free credit monitoring and identity protection through CyberScout, with a 90-day enrollment deadline. BleepingComputer contacted OnTrac for more details about the attack, the number of affected clients, and whether a ransom was paid, but had not received a response by publication time.

  • OnTrac’s statement that it took steps to ensure the data was “re-secured and not distributed” is notable because the article says this suggests a possible agreement with the attackers, typically a ransom payment.
  • The public notification sample does not reveal the specific data elements involved beyond names, which limits what can be independently understood about the sensitivity of the exposed information.
  • Affected customers have a limited 90-day window to enroll in the free 12-month credit monitoring and identity protection service offered through CyberScout.
  • Although the article notes signs consistent with data extortion, no ransomware or data extortion group had claimed responsibility at the time of writing.
Key Points

  • 02The incident was detected on March 23, and OnTrac’s investigation found that the attacker accessed certain files between March 20 and March 22.
  • 03Apart from names, the types of exposed information are unclear because OnTrac redacted the data elements in the notification sample shared with authorities.
  • 04OnTrac is offering affected customers 12 months of free credit monitoring and identity protection through CyberScout, with a 90-day enrollment deadline.
