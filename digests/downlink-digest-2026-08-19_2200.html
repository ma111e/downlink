AI pentesting evaluation checklist: What to look for in an AI pentesting vendor
aikido.dev · 20 Aug 2026 01:00 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.5
TAGSaikidoaustraliatyro-payments
45
In plain words

The article is a checklist for companies choosing an AI pentesting vendor, because products using that label can work very differently in practice. It says buyers should watch each tool run live and compare whether it finds real, repeatable problems, stays within agreed limits, works quickly, and fits how their teams release software. This matters because many companies change their software often but do not check security on every release, so weak testing can leave serious issues undiscovered.

Summary
AI pentesting vendors vary widely, so buyers should evaluate them with live, comparable tests focused on validated findings, workflow depth, code access, reliability, scope controls, and fit with release cycles. The key takeaway is to judge tools by proven exploitability and operational performance, not demos or reports alone.

The article presents a checklist for evaluating AI pentesting vendors, noting that the category is still new and that products using the term can behave very differently. It says some tools resemble automated scanners, while others explore application workflows, attempt multi-step attacks, and validate findings against a live system. The article recommends looking beyond terminology and watching comparable tests run live rather than comparing final reports alone.

The article says important evaluation areas include validation and signal quality, depth of testing, visibility and reporting, speed and workflow fit, operational reliability, setup, scope control, data protection, and model flexibility. It emphasizes that findings should be proven, reproducible, validated against a live system, and tied to actual exploit paths. It also argues that code access can significantly improve results, citing more than 1,000 AI-driven penetration tests where code access surfaced a median of 7x more high and critical vulnerabilities at roughly half the agent cost per finding.

The article highlights safety and operational concerns, including technical enforcement of scope, blocked out-of-scope requests, pre-flight checks, isolated execution environments, restricted outbound access, and the ability to monitor or stop tests. It lists red flags such as unreliable test completion, long setup, unproven findings, no built-in retesting, surface-level testing, and scope controls that are not technically enforced. It also cites Tyro Payments, where AI pentesting reportedly took five and a half hours and found about 30 issues, while incumbent human penetration testers took about 15 days and found five issues.

  • The article argues that code access is the strongest differentiator in AI pentesting outcomes, citing more than 1,000 tests where code-access engagements found a median of 7x more high and critical vulnerabilities at roughly half the agent cost per finding.
  • The article recommends scoring vendors while tests are running because setup effort, reliability failures, consumed budget, and testing capacity can materially change an apples-to-apples comparison.
  • The article treats technical scope enforcement as a core safety requirement, warning that vendors without system-level guardrails are relying on the model’s judgment for unpredictable agent behavior.
  • The article suggests speed is not only an operational convenience but also affects coverage, citing a case where a two-hour AI pentest with codebase access found 13 valid issues after a 120-hour manual pentest found none.
  • The Tyro Payments example shows the article’s preferred model is not replacing humans entirely, but using AI pentesting and AI code audit alongside human review to increase coverage while reducing cost.
Key Points
  • 01The article says AI pentesting vendors should be evaluated by watching comparable live tests run, rather than by comparing final reports alone.
  • 02The checklist emphasizes validation and signal quality, including findings that are proven, reproducible, validated against a live system, and tied to actual exploit paths.
  • 03The article states that deeper AI pentesting should cover workflows, roles, application state, multi-step issues, and, when possible, source code or internal context.
  • 04The article says scope control and safety should be enforced technically during execution, including domain limits, production restrictions, automatic blocking of out-of-scope requests, and pre-flight checks.
  • 05The article cites Tyro Payments as a proof of concept where AI pentesting found about 30 issues in five and a half hours, while incumbent human penetration testers found five issues in approximately 15 days.

JUSTIFICATION

This is an evergreen evaluation checklist for AI pentesting vendors rather than coverage of a specific incident, active exploitation, vulnerability, or urgent finding. It may be useful for teams assessing AI pentesting tools, but it is low priority for security professionals tracking time-sensitive security developments.
