In plain words

Maersk says someone gained access to an older local computer system connected to LF Logistics, a company it bought in 2022. A group calling itself WhiteArmor says it took 100GB of LF Logistics data and wants $5 million, but Maersk says it has found no sign that sensitive information or other company and customer systems were affected. The event matters because shipping and ports depend increasingly on digital systems, and similar incidents can delay cargo, disrupt scheduling and billing, and affect supply chains.

Summary
Maersk says a cyber incident was confined to a legacy system inherited through its 2022 acquisition of LF Logistics, with no evidence of sensitive-data exposure or disruption to core systems. The disclosure follows a WhiteArmor ransomware claim that it stole 100GB of LF Logistics data and demanded $5 million.

Maersk said threat actors accessed a legacy local system inherited through a previous acquisition. The company said the incident was isolated and found no indication that other Maersk systems, customer systems, or partner systems connected through APIs and EDI were affected. Maersk also said it had found no evidence that sensitive data was disclosed and did not expect customer disruptions.

The statement followed WhiteArmor’s claim that it attacked LF Logistics, a Hong Kong-based transportation and logistics company Maersk acquired in 2022. WhiteArmor alleged it stole 100GB of data, including information related to drivers, warehouses, and customers, and demanded $5 million within 30 days. The article notes that Maersk has previously experienced ransomware affecting company systems and specific port operations.

The incident comes amid other disruptions affecting shipping and ports, including an attack on Brazil’s Santos Port Authority. That attack caused a power outage, cargo loading and unloading delays, and manual scheduling and billing operations; the authority said it had recovered about 70% of systems through backups. Brazil’s Federal Police are investigating, while the article notes that ports in the United States, Europe, and Asia have also been repeatedly targeted over the past five years.

  • The Maersk incident highlights that systems inherited through acquisitions can remain relevant to an organization’s cyber risk even when the affected environment is described as isolated from core and customer-connected systems.
  • The Santos Port Authority’s ability to recover about 70% of its systems from backups shows that recovery can be partial and still leave essential port functions, including scheduling and billing, dependent on manual work.
  • Recent incidents at Maersk and the Port of Santos, alongside prior cases in the United States, Europe, and Asia, show that organizations supporting global shipping are repeatedly being targeted as operations become more digital.
  • The article distinguishes between a criminal group’s claim of stolen LF Logistics data and Maersk’s finding that it has no indication sensitive data was disclosed, underscoring that public extortion claims and an organization’s confirmed impact assessment may differ.
Key Points
  • 01Maersk said threat actors accessed a legacy local system inherited from a previous acquisition, with no indication that other Maersk, customer, or partner systems were affected.
  • 02The WhiteArmor ransomware group claimed it attacked LF Logistics, which Maersk acquired in 2022, stole 100GB of data, and demanded $5 million within 30 days.
  • 03Maersk said its findings indicated that no sensitive data had been disclosed and that it did not expect customer disruptions.
  • 04A cyberattack on Brazil’s Santos Port Authority caused a power outage, cargo-handling delays, and manual route scheduling and billing operations.
  • 05The Santos Port Authority said it had restored about 70% of its systems from backups, while Brazil’s Federal Police investigated the incident.

Why CIRA Implementation Matters for Incident Response
mitiga.io · 12 Nov 2026 16:39 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra
TAGSboxgartnermarketosalesforceworkday
35
In plain words

The article explains why companies using cloud services and online business apps need better ways to investigate and recover from security break-ins. Many security teams are experienced with systems they run themselves but may lack the skills, information, and tested processes needed when problems occur in cloud services. This affects large organizations whose departments independently use tools such as Workday, Salesforce, Box, and Marketo, making it harder to see what is happening and ensure company rules are followed. Better automated investigation and response tools can help these organizations be ready to limit damage and recover when an incident occurs.

Summary
Cloud Investigation and Response Automation (CIRA) helps organizations close cloud and SaaS incident-response gaps by improving specialized expertise, forensic telemetry collection, readiness testing, and visibility across decentralized SaaS environments.

Cloud Investigation and Response Automation (CIRA) is presented as a way for organizations to improve their ability to respond to and recover from cloud and SaaS security incidents. Gartner released a report titled “Emerging Tech: Security — Cloud Investigation and Response Automation Offers Transformation Opportunities,” covering critical insights, impacts, and recommendations for product leaders in data forensics and incident response. The article argues that organizations may have invested heavily in cloud-security prevention while still lacking the tools and processes to manage cloud breaches effectively.

CIRA can help fill gaps in cloud and SaaS incident-response skills, ensure relevant investigation data is continuously collected, and test whether response practices work in cloud environments before an incident occurs. Rapidly changing cloud environments can make it difficult for teams to gather and analyze the information needed for complete investigations. The article also highlights that SaaS applications are often independently selected and managed by business units, complicating visibility, policy enforcement, compliance, and recovery; CIRA is described as a complement to, not a replacement for, security controls and policies.

  • Strong on-premises incident-response procedures do not necessarily translate to cloud and SaaS environments, so organizations should validate their cloud readiness before a breach occurs.
  • Even organizations with skilled investigators and suitable vendors may lack the continuously collected data needed to conduct a complete cloud or SaaS investigation because cloud environments change rapidly.
  • Decentralized SaaS adoption by individual business units can make visibility, policy enforcement, and compliance difficult, even when an organization has established SaaS policies.
  • CIRA is presented as a complement to preventive security controls rather than a replacement for securing cloud and SaaS services or enforcing policies.
Key Points
  • 01Cloud Investigation and Response Automation (CIRA) is intended to help organizations respond to and recover from cloud and SaaS security incidents as effectively as they handle on-premises incidents.
  • 02CIRA can help address cloud and SaaS incident-response skills gaps by augmenting teams that have more experience responding to on-premises breaches.
  • 03CIRA solutions can support the ongoing collection and analysis of the forensic data needed for comprehensive cloud and SaaS investigations.
  • 04CIRA provides fit-for-purpose tools for testing whether an organization’s people, telemetry, and practices work effectively during cloud and SaaS incidents.
  • 05CIRA can improve visibility and support response and recovery across SaaS applications that are independently adopted and managed by business units.
