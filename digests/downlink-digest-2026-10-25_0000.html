A Fake China Domain Registry Warns Your CEO About .cn Names

In plain words

A UK industrial equipment manufacturer received an urgent email claiming someone was trying to register the company’s name as Chinese web addresses, supposedly from a Chinese registration office. The sender wanted the company to reply and confirm whether the applicant was a distributor, creating an opening for a later scam rather than asking for money immediately. The email was blocked before anyone responded, but it shows that scammers can use believable messages with no harmful files or web links to start conversations and target businesses.

Summary
A phishing scam posing as a Chinese domain registry used an urgent .cn registration warning to lure a UK manufacturer into replying, despite passing SPF, DKIM, and DMARC and containing no malicious links or attachments. The case shows that reply-only social engineering requires behavioral and language-based detection beyond authentication and payload scanning.

A UK industrial equipment manufacturer received an urgent email that claimed to come from a China domain registration center. It warned that a third party was about to register the company’s name under .cn, .com.cn, .net.cn, and .org.cn domains and asked whether that party was an authorized distributor. The message was directed to a shared partnership-inquiry mailbox as “Dear CEO,” with the intended first-stage outcome being a reply rather than an immediate payment or download.

The sender’s technical details did not support its claimed identity: its .net domain was registered through Name.com behind a privacy proxy, its sending server was geolocated by Microsoft to the United States, and the message used a Foxmail client header despite claiming to be based in Shanghai. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC all passed because the sender authenticated a domain it controlled, and the email included no attachments, QR codes, or meaningful links for scanners to assess. Microsoft routed it to Junk, while IRONSCALES quarantined and automatically resolved it as phishing based on language, community, and first-time-sender signals; no one replied and no harm was recorded.

  • Passing sender-authentication checks does not establish that an email is trustworthy, because the scammer used a domain they controlled and authenticated it correctly.
  • The scam’s first-stage objective was not a payment or a malicious download but a reply, meaning file and link scanning alone had little to inspect.
  • The article shows that checking whether the sender’s technical records match its claimed identity can expose a false business pretext, such as a supposed Shanghai registry using a privacy-protected .net domain and a U.S.-geolocated sending server.
  • The message targeted a shared partnership mailbox with an appeal to the CEO, illustrating how urgency and brand-protection concerns can be used to prompt internal escalation.
Key Points
  • 01A UK industrial equipment manufacturer received an urgent email posing as a China domain registration center and claiming that a third party sought to register the company’s name under four .cn-related domains.
  • 02The email contained no links, attachments, or QR codes beyond a hotlinked signature image, and its intended payload was to induce the recipient to reply.
  • 03The message passed SPF, DKIM, and DMARC because the sender controlled and authenticated its own domain, while Microsoft routed it to Junk with a spam verdict.
  • 04The sender’s claimed Shanghai registry identity conflicted with records showing a privacy-protected Name.com .net domain, a sending server geolocated to the United States, and a Foxmail client header.
  • 05IRONSCALES quarantined and automatically resolved the incident as phishing after matching its language and signature pattern to known China domain-registration scam templates.
