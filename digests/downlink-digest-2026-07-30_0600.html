In plain words

A Russian hacking group called Laundry Bear has been using a flaw in Microsoft Outlook Web Access to break into mailboxes when people simply open certain emails, with no links or attachments needed. The campaign has targeted U.S. and European government organizations and companies in telecommunications, finance, hospitality, and aerospace. It matters because the implanted code can keep giving the hackers access to a victim’s mailbox even after passwords are changed or the person’s device is completely rebuilt, allowing long-term spying on sensitive email.

Summary
Russian threat actor Laundry Bear/TA488 is exploiting a Microsoft Outlook Web Access XSS flaw to deploy OWAReaper, a browser-based implant that can persist even after password resets or device reimaging. The campaign targets government and major industry sectors in the U.S. and Europe using “half-click” emails that compromise victims when opened in vulnerable OWA.

Russian threat actors linked to earlier exploitation of a Zimbra vulnerability have been observed exploiting CVE-2026-42897, a cross-site scripting flaw in Microsoft Outlook Web Access. Proofpoint attributed the activity to Laundry Bear, also known as CL-STA-1114, TA488, UNK_PitStop, and Void Blizzard. The campaign began on July 22, 2026, and has targeted U.S. and European government entities as well as telecommunications, financial, hospitality, and aerospace organizations.

The attacks use vague emails with no links or attachments, relying on recipients opening or reading the message in a vulnerable version of OWA. The exploit uses JavaScript hidden in the message body’s social media icon HTML to assemble and execute encoded code. This leads to deployment of OWAReaper, a browser-based JavaScript implant described by Proofpoint as an evolution of ZimReaper and the most sophisticated backdoor delivered through these “half-click” exploit chains.

OWAReaper runs inside the OWA reading pane, removes exploit content from the email, gathers mailbox-related information, and stores an encrypted version of itself in browser localStorage for repeated execution. It can also abuse Outlook add-ins with ReadWriteMailbox permissions to steal OAuth tokens and grant Owner-level permissions to the Default user on every mail folder, creating server-side persistence that survives credential rotation and device re-imaging. The malware uses GitHub commit messages or attacker-sent emails for command-and-control, and exfiltrates data over HTTPS with a DNS tunneling fallback.

  • Credential rotation and device re-imaging are insufficient remediation for OWAReaper because the article says its persistent mailbox access lives on the Exchange server and requires deliberate removal from the server.
  • The campaign’s generic, low-detail emails are part of the evasion strategy, because Proofpoint assessed the broad, vague messaging as an effort to blend into mass-mailing spam and avoid drawing attention while still triggering compromise when opened.
  • OWAReaper’s use of both GitHub commit messages and attacker-sent emails for commands gives it multiple control paths inside normal web and mail activity, making the implant less dependent on a single attacker-controlled server.
  • The malware attempts to clean up and hide during execution by rewriting the email on the Exchange server to remove exploit content and by disabling OWA pop-ups and right-clicking while it runs.
  • Proofpoint said the earliest infrastructure for the campaign was created in March 2026, two months before Microsoft disclosed CVE-2026-42897, raising the possibility that the flaw may have been exploited as a zero-day.
Key Points

  • 02The campaign targets U.S. and European government entities as well as the telecommunications, financial, hospitality, and aerospace sectors using vague emails that require no recipient action beyond opening or reading the message in a vulnerable OWA client.
  • 03The exploitation deploys OWAReaper, a JavaScript browser-based implant that runs inside the OWA reading pane, removes exploit content from the email, collects mailbox-related information, and stores an encrypted copy of itself in browser localStorage.
  • 04OWAReaper can grant Owner-level permissions to the Default user on every mail folder, which Proofpoint said allows persistent mailbox access that survives credential rotation and even full re-imaging of the targeted user’s device.

In plain words

The article describes GenieLocker, a new tool used by the Toy Ghouls group to lock up files and demand payment, seen mainly against organizations in Russia, especially manufacturers. In one March 2026 case, the attackers got in through a partner’s OpenVPN connection using valid stolen credentials, moved through Windows and Linux systems, and locked files and virtual machine disks. This matters because Toy Ghouls now appear to have their own tool that works across Windows, Linux, and ESXi, making them less dependent on other criminal software and potentially more capable against businesses.

Summary
Kaspersky analyzed GenieLocker, a new custom ransomware used by the Toy Ghouls group against mainly Russian manufacturing organizations, with Windows, Linux, and ESXi builds. The malware marks a shift from the group’s previous use of third-party ransomware to its own cross-platform encryption tool.

GenieLocker is a new ransomware family active since March 2026 and used in attacks against organizations in the Russian Federation, primarily in manufacturing. Open-source intelligence attributes the activity to Toy Ghouls, also known as Bearlyfy, Labubu, and Laboo.boo. The group previously relied on third-party ransomware such as RedAlert, LockBit, and Babuk, but GenieLocker appears to be a custom tool that reduces that reliance.

In a March 2026 incident, attackers entered the victim environment through an OpenVPN connection from an external partner’s network, likely using stolen but still valid credentials. They installed tools including OpenSSH, socks5.exe, SoftPerfect Network Scanner, and Mimikatz, and forensic analysis showed access to KeePassXC password managers on compromised machines. The attackers moved laterally with RDP and SSH, deployed the ransomware with PsExec and PAExec, and used a reverse SSH tunnel for command-and-control communication.

Kaspersky found GenieLocker in Windows PE builds and Linux/ESXi ELF builds, both using libsodium-based cryptography. The Windows version includes a required secret launch argument, anti-debugging checks, process and service termination, exclusion lists, and file encryption using XChaCha20-Poly1305 with Curve25519-XSalsa20-Poly1305 key encryption. The Linux and ESXi version is simpler but includes ESXi-specific features such as daemonizing the process and modifying /etc/vmware/welcome, and it defaults to targeting /vmfs/volumes.

  • GenieLocker does not create ransom notes or include attacker contact details, which the article says may help avoid detection triggered by mass creation of readme files.
  • The Windows build requires a secret hex argument before it runs, likely limiting execution in sandboxes or by unauthorized threat actors.
  • Forensic analysis in the described attacks found no evidence of data theft, matching Toy Ghouls’ pattern of not using double extortion or a data-leak site.
  • The Linux and ESXi build appears simpler than the Windows build but includes ESXi-specific behavior such as targeting `/vmfs/volumes` and modifying `/etc/vmware/welcome`.
  • GenieLocker encrypts only selected chunks of files based on a percentage setting while always encrypting the first chunk, allowing configurable partial encryption across Windows, Linux, and ESXi builds.
Key Points
  • 01GenieLocker is a new ransomware family active since March 2026 and used against organizations in the Russian Federation, primarily in the manufacturing sector.
  • 02Open-source intelligence attributes GenieLocker attacks to Toy Ghouls, a financially motivated extortion group also known as Bearlyfy, Labubu, and Laboo.boo.
  • 03Kaspersky found GenieLocker samples in Windows PE builds and Linux/ESXi ELF builds, with both variants using libsodium-based cryptography for file encryption.
  • 04In a March 2026 incident, the attackers entered through an OpenVPN connection from an external partner’s network using likely stolen but valid credentials, then used tools including OpenSSH, SoftPerfect Network Scanner, Mimikatz, PsExec, and PAExec.

In plain words

Amazon says North Korea was behind earlier tampering with popular software packages called debug and chalk, after those packages had been altered to steal cryptocurrency by changing wallet transaction details before users approved them. This matters because those packages were downloaded billions of times a week, so a single trusted software maintainer being tricked can put many apps and users at risk. The article also says Amazon’s public proof for linking that older incident to North Korea is limited, even though other companies had already linked a later related package incident to the same broader North Korean activity.

Summary
Amazon has attributed the 2025 npm hijacks of debug and chalk to North Korea’s Sapphire Sleet, linking them to later supply-chain compromises such as axios. The article notes that Amazon’s public evidence for tying debug and chalk to the group remains limited and partly disputed.

Amazon has tied the September 2025 hijack of the npm packages debug and chalk to North Korea, assessing with medium confidence that the group behind the March 2026 axios compromise was also responsible. The earlier incident had been publicly documented as a cryptocurrency theft campaign in which a maintainer was phished through a lookalike npm domain and a wallet-draining script was pushed into at least 18 packages with more than 2 billion weekly downloads. Amazon also linked the same activity to a March 2025 package called typo-crypto, which it described as a test run before later attacks on more popular packages.

The article emphasizes that Amazon’s public evidence for the debug and chalk attribution is limited, because its post cites shared tradecraft, trojanized packages, post-install hooks, code reuse, and overlapping command-and-control indicators without specifying which evidence ties which incident. The debug and chalk payload differed from the axios payload because it used a browser-side interceptor that hooked fetch, XMLHttpRequest, and wallet APIs to rewrite transaction addresses before a user signed, while leaving no persistence on the machine. Google and Microsoft separately attributed the axios compromise to North Korean-linked activity, but the article says no other reviewed vendor research publicly names an actor for the debug, chalk, and typo-crypto compromises.

The typo-crypto package contained a malicious core.js file that triggered on a hash input beginning 0098273 and pulled an operating-system-specific second stage from hardcoded command-and-control infrastructure. The Hacker News found that typo-crypto@4.3.0 was still published and installable on July 30, 2026, though it had no install script and would not run simply by being downloaded. The article also notes that npm v12 disables dependency lifecycle scripts by default and npm began scanning newly published packages for malware, but these measures do not close the maintainer-compromise path or automatically apply to packages already on the registry.

  • Amazon’s attribution is consequential but not fully transparent, because its post lists categories of evidence such as code reuse and command-and-control overlap without mapping those indicators to the debug and chalk incident specifically.
  • The typo-crypto registry history appears to conflict with Amazon’s description of all three campaigns beginning with a trusted maintainer being socially engineered, because typo-crypto had only one version and no earlier benign release to update.
  • The debug and chalk attack would not have been prevented by npm v12’s default blocking of dependency lifecycle scripts, because that compromise did not rely on an install or post-install script.
  • Publish-time malware scanning that npm began on July 28 would not automatically address already-published packages such as typo-crypto@4.3.0, which The Hacker News found was still installable on July 30, 2026.
  • The article highlights an indicator-quality issue: Amazon’s published hash information for typo-crypto appears mismatched, with one listed hash corresponding to the package rather than core.js and another not matching any file in the tarball.

The FCC added foreign-produced mobile robots and networked power inverters to a list that generally blocks new models from being imported, marketed, or sold in the United States, because officials are worried they could be remotely misused. Existing devices and already authorized models are not affected, and security/compatibility updates remain allowed under a waiver.

The Federal Communications Commission added foreign-produced mobile robots and networked power inverters to its Covered List on July 28. The action generally prevents new models from receiving the equipment authorization required for import, marketing, or sale in the United States. Previously authorized models can still be sold, devices people already own are unaffected, and federal purchases and use are also unaffected by this FCC action.

The FCC’s definitions are not based on a specific brand or named country, and the article says coverage depends on production and technical tests. “Foreign-produced” means an article that fails to qualify as a “domestic end product” under the Buy American standard cited in the article. Manufacturers can apply for Conditional Approval, with the Department of War able to approve robotic devices and the Department of War or Department of Homeland Security able to approve power inverters.

Welcome to Danglegeddon

In plain words

Silent Push studied thousands of forgotten web addresses from government and Fortune 500 organizations and showed that many could be taken over because they still pointed to online services that no longer existed. This could let criminals put fake login pages or harmful content under a trusted organization’s name, making scams much harder to spot. The affected groups include government agencies, banks, pharmaceutical companies, automakers, and other major industries, with possible real-world consequences ranging from stolen credentials to disruptions in public services, banking, supply chains, and health research.

Summary
Silent Push simulated large-scale dangling DNS and subdomain takeovers across government and Fortune 500 environments, showing that abandoned cloud-linked records can quickly become trusted launchpads for phishing, credential theft, and broader compromise. The key defense is continuous DNS monitoring and strict decommissioning hygiene: remove or reclaim DNS records before deleting the cloud resources they point to.

Silent Push conducted a research initiative called “Danglegeddon” to simulate large-scale exploitation of dangling DNS infrastructure across government and Fortune 500 organizations. The team targeted 12,500 apex domains, isolated 16,000 dangling subdomains, and automated the takeover of 4,000 of them in a controlled environment. The research initially focused on Microsoft infrastructure, including Azure Blobs, Azure VMs, Web Apps, APIs, and Power Pages instances.

The article explains that a taken-over subdomain is not itself an intrusion, but it can become a trusted launchpad for phishing pages, credential-harvesting forms, malicious content, OAuth redirection, or, in some misconfigured cases, interception of shared session tokens. Silent Push modeled potential impacts across government, banking, pharmaceutical, and automotive sectors, highlighting how trusted but abandoned infrastructure could affect public agencies, financial services, drug development, supply chains, and major manufacturers. The article says the ultimate severity depends on additional security failures such as weak multi-factor authentication, broad internal trust, or overly broad cookie scoping.

Silent Push describes dangling CNAME, MX, and NS records as carrying different risks, with dangling NS records presented as the worst because control of a delegated name server can affect an entire zone. The article says its platform finds dangling records through daily re-resolution of hostnames rather than one-off scans, allowing defenders to see added, removed, and unchanged exposures. It recommends fixing records in order of blast radius, deleting or correctly repointing abandoned DNS entries, reclaiming claimable resources defensively when needed, and ensuring DNS records are removed before cloud resources during decommissioning.

  • The article emphasizes that dangling DNS becomes far more dangerous when combined with other common hygiene failures, such as broad cookie scoping, weak multi-factor authentication, overly broad internal trust, or trusted third-party relationships.
  • Silent Push found that provider-side safeguards materially reduce takeover risk, citing 4,925 records that were not claimable because protections such as domain ownership verification were in place.
  • The article frames daily re-resolution of hostnames as more effective than target-specific scanning because forgotten records often do not appear in an organization’s own asset inventories or past testing scopes.
  • The recommended remediation order is based on blast radius rather than volume: dangling NS records should be addressed before MX records, external CNAMEs, and internal records, even though CNAMEs were far more common in the study.
  • The research links organizational change events such as employee attrition, layoffs, and mergers or acquisitions to orphaned DNS exposure, making DNS review an actionable part of change control and termination processes.
Key Points
  • 01Silent Push analyzed 12,500 apex domains from government and Fortune 500 organizations and identified 16,000 dangling subdomains, with automated takeover of 4,000 of them in a controlled simulation.
  • 02The simulation used dangling DNS takeover to demonstrate how abandoned DNS records could enable credential harvesting, OAuth redirection, phishing pages, cookie theft in some misconfigured cases, and abuse of trusted organizational domains.
  • 03Silent Push focused initially on Microsoft infrastructure, including Azure Blobs, Azure VMs, Web Apps, APIs, and Power Pages instances, and found takeover examples across government, banking, automotive, and pharmaceutical organizations.
  • 04The article states that dangling CNAME, MX, and NS records carry different risks, with dangling NS records described as the worst because control of the delegated name server can allow control over the entire zone beneath it.
  • 05Silent Push recommends deleting or correctly repointing dangling records, reclaiming claimable resources defensively where needed, removing DNS records before cloud resources during decommissioning, using domain ownership verification, assigning record owners, publishing CAA records, and continuously monitoring DNS changes.
