In plain words

Summary
Researchers found that physical attackers can bypass TPM PIN lockout on some AMD and Intel PCs by rolling back or forging flash-based counter state, reducing Windows Hello and BitLocker PIN cracking from years to days. AMD has acknowledged a firmware flaw and plans fixes through early 2027; users should use longer PINs and keep devices physically secure.

Researchers at Technische Universität Berlin reported that a person who opens certain PCs can reset the TPM’s count of incorrect Windows Hello or BitLocker PIN attempts. Their lab tests recovered a four-digit Windows Hello PIN on an AMD laptop and a six-digit BitLocker PIN on an Intel laptop after 236 attempts each. Based on measured speeds, the researchers estimate the method can reduce worst-case cracking times from 69 days to 40 hours for a four-digit Hello PIN and from 19 years to 20 days for a six-digit BitLocker PIN.

The affected systems use firmware TPMs that save encrypted state, including failed-attempt counts, on the mainboard’s flash chip. On AMD systems using RPMC flash counters, researchers used a board between the processor and flash chip to block counter updates, while a voltage glitch against certain Winbond chips could reveal a key used to forge counter values. AMD confirmed CVE-2026-43613 and plans firmware fixes for most affected chips between November 2026 and February 2027, while the researchers recommend adding at least two digits to Hello and BitLocker PINs and keeping PCs under physical control.

  • On the tested AMD laptop, Windows forced a restart after every five incorrect Windows Hello PIN entries, but the reset TPM state meant no lockout delay was applied.
  • Two AMD desktops without RPMC flash accepted an old flash-state copy written back with an ordinary programmer, so the researchers did not need the specialized interposer board on those systems.
  • The Intel laptop attack depended on the Winbond flash chip weakness rather than an Intel component; its firmware also supplied the same value to the chip at every boot, allowing the extracted key to remain useful after restarts.
  • Adding two digits substantially changes the researchers' estimated attack times: a four-digit Windows Hello PIN could take up to 40 hours on the tested AMD setup, while an eight-digit PIN could take up to 46 years.
  • A recovered Windows Hello PIN may unlock device-bound keys and workplace single sign-on, and in some configurations may satisfy a multi-factor authentication check.
