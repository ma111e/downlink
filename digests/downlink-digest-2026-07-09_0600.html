AI Authority Drift: The Permissions Nobody Approved

In plain words

The article says AI tools inside companies can quietly gain access to more systems over time, such as code, tickets, documents, and data stores, until no one knows exactly what they can reach. This matters because attackers have already taken over company AI agents and used their approved access to move quickly through defense, energy, and technology targets, and a Microsoft 365 Copilot flaw showed how one crafted email could pull data from OneDrive, SharePoint, and Teams. Companies using these AI agents are affected, and the broader risk is that sensitive business data can be exposed through access that seemed harmless when it was added piece by piece.

Summary
AI agents are accumulating permissions through integrations and inherited access faster than traditional reviews can track, creating “authority drift” that attackers can exploit using the agents’ own legitimate access. Organizations need live visibility and real-time enforcement of agent permissions rather than relying on static approval records or periodic reviews.

The article discusses “authority drift,” defined as the expansion of an AI agent’s permission footprint beyond what was originally approved until nobody can account for everything it can reach. It describes a case where a coding agent started with recorded read access to a code repository but later gained connections to a ticketing system, a documentation tool, and a data store. The article argues that this creates a gap between what security teams think an agent can do and what it can actually do.

The article says authority drift does not behave like traditional overprovisioning because AI agent permissions can change faster than quarterly access reviews can catch. It identifies three drivers of growth: inherited access from connected systems, new integrations added by developers, and broad access granted for convenience. It also cites September 2025 incidents where state-sponsored attackers hijacked enterprise AI agent instances across 30 defense, energy, and technology targets, and CVE-2025-32711, a Microsoft 365 Copilot zero-click prompt injection that extracted data from OneDrive, SharePoint, and Teams.

The article’s main significance is that organizations often lack a current map of what each AI agent can reach, while agent deployment is increasing across enterprise applications. It says static inventories from deployment quickly become outdated when integrations or data stores change. The article recommends a live map of agent access, restrictions to fixed tools or models, and an enforcement point that can scope access without waiting for the next scheduled review.

  • Traditional quarterly access reviews are poorly matched to AI agents because the article says an agent’s permissions can expand in weeks rather than years.
  • The article highlights that individually reasonable access decisions by separate teams can combine into an overall permission footprint that no single team approved or understands.
  • The article argues that visibility alone is insufficient because a live access map and tool restrictions only produce reports unless there is an enforcement point that can act before the next scheduled review.
  • The article warns that continuous monitoring can fail operationally if every integration change triggers an alert, because overstretched security teams may tune out the noise within a quarter.
  • The article frames the most useful governance question as procedural rather than static: who last checked what an agent can reach, and when.
Key Points
  • 01The article defines authority drift as an AI agent’s permission footprint expanding beyond what was originally approved until nobody can account for everything it can reach.
  • 02The article says AI agent access grows through inherited permissions from connected systems, new integrations added to solve immediate problems, and broad access granted for convenience.
  • 03In September 2025, state-sponsored attackers hijacked enterprise AI agent instances across 30 targets in defense, energy, and technology, using the agents’ accumulated access to move laterally in minutes.
  • 04CVE-2025-32711 was described as a zero-click prompt injection in Microsoft 365 Copilot that used a crafted email to extract data from OneDrive, SharePoint, and Teams through a trusted Microsoft domain.
  • 05The article argues that organizations need a live map of each agent’s access, restrictions to a fixed set of tools or models, and an enforcement point that can scope access without waiting for the next scheduled review.
