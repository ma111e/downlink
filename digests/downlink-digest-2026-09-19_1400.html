North Korean WaterPlum hackers infected 30,000 devices worldwide

In plain words

Authorities say a North Korean group called WaterPlum tricked job seekers with fake interviews and coding tests, causing at least 30,000 computers in more than 100 countries to be infected. The group stole cryptocurrency, account details, private wallet recovery information, and documents, and may have used access to victims’ workplaces to reach other networks. Investigators say more than $10.7 million was sent to North Korea, where the money may support the country’s weapons programs, while stolen identity documents can also be used to help North Korean workers obtain jobs under false identities.

Summary
A joint international advisory says North Korean WaterPlum operators infected at least 30,000 devices in more than 100 countries through fake job interviews and malicious developer projects, stealing credentials and cryptocurrency. The group allegedly moved $10.7 million to North Korea and supports fraudulent IT-worker operations using stolen identities.

A joint advisory from Japanese, U.S., Australian, and German authorities says the North Korean WaterPlum group infected at least 30,000 devices in more than 100 countries between December 2025 and July 2026. Investigators said the group stole funds or account credentials from more than 7,000 cryptocurrency wallets and transferred cryptocurrency worth 1.7 billion Japanese yen, or $10.71 million, to North Korea. WaterPlum is associated with the multi-year Contagious Interview campaign and is part of a broader North Korean ecosystem that generates revenue for the regime.

The group approaches job seekers by impersonating AI, cryptocurrency, and NFT companies or by using recruiting and freelance platforms. During fake interviews and coding tests, victims are asked to download projects, fix supposed video-call issues, or run malicious code. The advisory connects the operation to BeaverTail, InvisibleFerret, OtterCookie, OtterCandy, and StoatWaffle, including Node.js malware delivered through malicious Visual Studio Code projects that can run code after a folder is opened and trusted.

After infecting a device, the attackers seek browser credentials, clipboard contents, keystrokes, cryptocurrency private keys and seed phrases, documents, and screenshots. They may also use an infected person’s computer to move into an employer’s or client’s network for intellectual-property theft and espionage. Authorities also linked WaterPlum to North Korean fraudulent IT-worker operations, including the alleged use of stolen identity documents and AI face-swapping during online interviews.

  • The campaign does not stop at stealing cryptocurrency: access to infected job seekers’ computers can also be used to reach their employers’ or clients’ networks for intellectual-property theft and espionage.
  • Investigators directly linked WaterPlum to North Korean fraudulent IT-worker operations through shared IP addresses and the reuse of identity documents stolen during WaterPlum attacks.
  • The group reportedly uses AI face-swapping software in online job interviews, then turns off cameras and attributes the change to network problems.
  • One malware family, StoatWaffle, can be delivered through malicious Visual Studio Code projects that run code after a victim opens and trusts the project folder.
  • Japan’s authorities said they dismantled a North Korean IT-worker laptop farm in the country, the first such case identified there, with evidence that several hundred million yen had been transferred abroad.
Key Points
  • 01A joint advisory from Japanese, U.S., Australian, and German authorities says North Korean WaterPlum operators infected at least 30,000 devices in more than 100 countries between December 2025 and July 2026.
  • 02WaterPlum uses fake job interviews, coding tests, and impersonation of AI, cryptocurrency, and NFT companies to persuade job seekers to download or run malicious projects and code.
  • 03The advisory links WaterPlum to BeaverTail, InvisibleFerret, OtterCookie, OtterCandy, and StoatWaffle malware families.
  • 04Investigators said the group stole funds or account credentials from more than 7,000 cryptocurrency wallets and transferred cryptocurrency equivalent to $10.71 million to North Korea.
  • 05Authorities say WaterPlum is connected to North Korean fraudulent IT-worker operations, which may reuse identity documents stolen from victims to obtain jobs under false identities.

BragJack attacks hijack AI browser agents through malicious extensions
bleepingcomputer.com · 19 Sep 2026 14:56 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra

In plain words

A researcher showed that one harmful browser add-on can take over the AI helpers built into Chrome, Edge, Perplexity Comet, Opera Neon, and Claude for Chrome. Once installed, it may be able to make these helpers read files, view browsing information or email, take screenshots, and perform actions on websites as the user without the user clicking anything. Google and Microsoft fixed the flaws assigned to them, but the research shows that browser add-ons with broad permissions can turn increasingly capable AI features into a route for stealing information or acting in someone’s name.

Summary
Researchers demonstrated BragJack, a technique in which a malicious Chromium extension can hijack built-in AI browser agents and abuse their privileges to read sensitive data or act on a user’s behalf. Google and Microsoft have patched assigned flaws, but the findings affect multiple AI-enabled browsers and underscore the risks of installing broad-permission extensions.

Security researcher Gal Weizman of Forever Security disclosed BragJack, a proof-of-concept technique that uses a malicious browser extension to hijack AI assistants in Google Chrome, Perplexity Comet, Microsoft Edge, Opera Neon, and Claude for Chrome. The extension must already be installed, but can then operate without user interaction and use the AI agent’s existing privileges to access sensitive information or act for the victim. The research earned more than $20,000 in bug bounties and resulted in two CVEs, with Google and Microsoft resolving the flaws assigned to them.

BragJack uses Chromium’s declarativeNetRequest feature to alter web requests and pages trusted by privileged browser components that carry out AI-directed actions. In demonstrations, the resulting access could include local files, browsing history, screenshots, web content, and potentially a browser’s camera and microphone; the Comet agent was also forced to summarize a victim’s emails and send the results elsewhere. Weizman calls the approach Prompt Forcing because it provides an agent with complete prompts and follow-up instructions rather than placing malicious instructions inside content the AI reads. The findings show how an extension that traditionally accesses web content can become a route to AI features able to read data and act on websites for a user.

  • BragJack does not rely on hiding instructions inside content an AI happens to read; it can supply the agent with a complete prompt and follow-up instructions, a method the researcher calls Prompt Forcing.
  • Chromium’s declarativeNetRequest feature allowed the same malicious extension to be used across all five targets by altering request handling, including security headers and resource redirects.
  • Chrome blocked extensions from directly accessing its privileged AI component and injecting scripts into Gemini, but intercepting requests from the embedded Gemini web app still provided a route to execute code in its context.
  • In Perplexity Comet, a testing domain that lacked the main Perplexity site’s protections created a trust path to the built-in agent, while Edge’s separation of planning and action modes was bypassed through a timing flaw.
Key Points
  • 01Security researcher Gal Weizman disclosed BragJack, a proof-of-concept technique that uses one malicious browser extension to hijack AI assistants in five Chromium-based browsers or browser assistants.
  • 02The attack requires the malicious extension to already be installed, after which it can operate without user interaction and abuse the AI agent’s existing privileges to access sensitive information or act on the victim’s behalf.
  • 03The technique uses Chromium’s declarativeNetRequest capability to manipulate network traffic and pages trusted by privileged browser AI components.
  • 04Weizman demonstrated that compromised agents could access browsing history, screenshots, local files, and web content, while the Perplexity Comet agent was forced to summarize a victim’s emails and send the results to another address.
  • 05Google assigned CVE-2026-0628 to the Chrome issue and Microsoft assigned CVE-2026-55945 to an Edge race condition, and both vendors have resolved their assigned flaws.
