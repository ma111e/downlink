In plain words

ASUS released an update for Control Center Express, a tool businesses use to watch over and control many computers from one place. A serious weakness could let an outsider take over the control panel as an administrator without needing a password, potentially giving them control over the computers managed through it. Businesses and IT service providers using older versions should install version 1.7.29 and update the software running on their managed devices.

Summary
ASUS has patched critical flaws in Control Center Express, including a CVSS 10.0 hard-coded key vulnerability that lets unauthenticated remote attackers forge admin tokens and take control of the management console and managed devices. Organizations should upgrade to version 1.7.29 and update deployed client agents immediately.

ASUS released Control Center Express version 1.7.29 to address three critical vulnerabilities in its centralized dashboard for monitoring and remotely managing PC fleets. The most severe issue, CVE-2026-114202, has a CVSS score of 10.0 and stems from a hard-coded cryptographic key. It allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to forge an authentication token and obtain administrator privileges without user interaction.

The other flaws include CVE-2026-114204, which can enable a low-privileged remote attacker to forge a session token for a recently logged-in account, and CVE-2026-114205, which can let a low-privileged user create an administrator account. ASUS says versions earlier than 1.7.29 are affected, although the CVE records list affected versions only through 1.7.27, leaving version 1.7.28’s status unclear. Impacted users should install version 1.7.29, update agents on managed devices through Client Agent Updater, and verify their versions.

  • CVE-2026-114202 is the most immediately dangerous of the three Control Center Express flaws because it requires neither an existing account nor user interaction, unlike the other two issues.
  • Updating the Control Center Express console alone is not the full remediation process: ASUS also directs users to update and verify the agents installed on managed devices.
  • The affected-version information is inconsistent, as ASUS says versions before 1.7.29 are affected while the CVE records list versions only through 1.7.27, leaving version 1.7.28 unclear.
  • Because Control Center Express centrally monitors and remotely manages fleets of PCs, administrative access to its console can affect both the console and the devices it manages.
Key Points
  • 01ASUS released Control Center Express version 1.7.29 to fix three critical vulnerabilities affecting earlier versions.
  • 02CVE-2026-114202 has a CVSS score of 10.0 and allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to forge an authentication token and obtain administrator privileges.
  • 03CVE-2026-114204 can allow a remote attacker with low privileges to forge a session token for a recently logged-in account, while CVE-2026-114205 can let a low-privileged user create an administrator account.
  • 04ASUS advises affected users to install version 1.7.29, update agents on managed devices using Client Agent Updater, and verify the agent versions.
  • 05ASUS also issued updates for Control Center Enterprise and Armoury Crate to address separate critical and high-severity flaws.

In plain words

SBC Medical Group says unauthorized people accessed its customer databases and administrator accounts for Tribeau, a booking service used by 56 clinics in Japan. Personal details for about 1.6 million people may have been taken, including names, contact information, birth dates, account passwords, and some reservation and treatment information; records for about 20,000 employees were also affected. This matters because the stolen information could put customers and employees at risk of unwanted contact or misuse, although the company says it has not yet found evidence that the data has been used improperly.

Summary
SBC Medical Group disclosed that two November 2026 security incidents exposed personal data for roughly 1.6 million customers in Japan, plus employee records and booking information accessed through the Tribeau platform. The company is still investigating and says it has not yet found evidence that the data has been misused.

SBC Medical Group disclosed two cybersecurity incidents affecting its Japan-based businesses: unauthorized access to certain company-managed databases and access to clinic administrator accounts on the third-party Tribeau booking platform. The company discovered the database incident on November 16 and was notified of the Tribeau incident that same day. It said the intrusion method and full scope of the database incident remain under investigation.

The database incident exposed personal information for 1,624,000 people, potentially including names, contact details, dates of birth, patient card numbers, member IDs, passwords, and survey responses. It also exposed reservation requests, website inquiries, treatment-related records, and about 20,000 employee account records; bank account and tax data was included for 459 employees. Separately, the Tribeau incident involved reservation information for 12,641 people and treatment names, dates, and fees for 6,804 people, while SBC said it had not confirmed access to electronic medical records or the mySBC reservation site and had seen no evidence of data misuse as of November 18.

  • The incidents involved two distinct access paths: SBC-managed databases and administrator accounts on the third-party Tribeau booking platform.
  • The largest affected population was tied to basic customer data, while the Tribeau incident involved smaller, more specific sets of reservation and treatment information.
  • The employee impact included a particularly sensitive subset: 459 employees had bank account and tax information exposed.
  • SBC stated that it had not confirmed unauthorized access to electronic medical records or its mySBC reservation site, limiting the confirmed scope of the database incident.
  • As of its November 18 SEC filing, SBC had found no evidence of misuse and could not estimate the incidents’ financial impact.

Hardening GitHub Actions Against Insider Threats
socket.dev · 19 Nov 2026 19:17 UTC openai-codex / gpt-5.6-terra
TAGScodecovdevice-code-phishingghostactiongithubgithub-actionslitellmnpmoidcpypis1ngularityshai-huludstaged-publishingtj-actionstrusted-publishingwebauthn

In plain words

This article explains how attackers can use a stolen developer account or access key to make harmful software releases while appearing to be legitimate team members. Incidents involving npm package maintainers, Nx, TanStack, LiteLLM, and Codecov show that one compromised account can expose secrets, change release settings, or publish malicious updates used by millions of downstream users. It recommends requiring stronger login methods, limiting who can change release systems, requiring an independent person to approve releases, and quickly removing access when something suspicious happens. The larger lesson is that software supply chains are only as safe as the people and accounts allowed to publish updates.

Summary
GitHub Actions defenses must assume a maintainer credential can be compromised: reduce standing privileges, restrict workflow changes and execution, use OIDC and human-gated releases, and continuously detect and revoke suspicious access. The key measure is how many independently protected credentials an attacker must compromise to ship a malicious release.

This article explains how GitHub Actions release pipelines can be abused when an attacker obtains a valid maintainer account, token, SSH key, deploy key, or browser session. In these cases, actions such as changing workflows, publishing packages, approving deployments, or weakening rules can appear to GitHub and registries as ordinary authenticated work. The article uses incidents involving LiteLLM, npm maintainer Qix, GhostAction, Shai-Hulud, Nx, TanStack, and Codecov to show how stolen credentials and release-system access can affect downstream software users.

Its central measure is how many independently protected credentials an attacker must compromise to ship a malicious release, rather than how many accounts nominally participate in approval. It recommends phishing-resistant sign-in methods, short-lived and narrowly scoped credentials, restrictions on workflow-file changes, protections on who can trigger deployment workflows, OIDC-based publishing, staged publishing or human approval gates, secret scanning, provenance verification, and audit-log monitoring. The article also stresses that controls can be bypassed if one privileged account can reach multiple credentials or change the controls themselves, making rapid detection, revocation, and rehearsed incident response essential.

  • Multiple accounts do not create real separation of duties if one administrator or compromised credential can access the other accounts' tokens; the LiteLLM investigation described three accounts used in the incident but a single cascading compromise.
  • GitHub's “Only allow secure two-factor methods” setting still permits authenticator apps and GitHub Mobile, so it cannot by itself guarantee that organization members use phishing-resistant passkeys or hardware security keys.
  • A push ruleset that restricts workflow-file paths can stop an unreviewed workflow push on any branch, but an empty bypass list does not prevent an organization owner from editing or disabling the ruleset itself.
  • Trusted publishing proves which repository workflow published a package, not whether the package contents are safe, so a compromised workflow can still abuse that publishing identity.
  • Deleting a secret from the latest version of a repository does not remove it from prior history, clones, or forks; the article says exposed credentials must be rotated rather than merely deleted.
Key Points
  • 01The article focuses on insider-style threats in GitHub Actions, where attackers use valid stolen or legitimate credentials rather than exploiting malformed inputs or unauthenticated pull requests.
  • 02Phishing-resistant passkeys or WebAuthn security keys can stop real-time login-code phishing, but they do not stop device-code phishing or misuse of stolen personal access tokens, SSH keys, deploy keys, or active browser sessions.
  • 03Workflow-file protections must cover every branch because an attacker with write access can push a new workflow to a non-default branch, run it immediately, and delete the branch afterward without opening a pull request.
  • 04OIDC trusted publishing removes the need for long-lived package publishing tokens, but a compromised workflow can still publish malicious content unless releases are staged or otherwise require an independently protected human approval.
  • 05The article argues that the meaningful security measure is the number of independently protected credentials an attacker must compromise to ship a malicious release, not simply the number of accounts involved.
