In plain words

The article explains how Mitiga built FORGE, a system that uses many focused AI helpers to create and test ways to spot suspicious activity in cloud and online service data. It matters because these checks are tested against real customer information before a person reviews and approves them, helping defenders find more signs of attacks without burying teams in false alarms. The broader stakes are that security teams may be able to prove their protections work by safely recreating attacker behavior and making sure the warning actually appears.

Summary
Mitiga built FORGE, a multi-agent AI pipeline that drafts and validates cloud detection rules against strict review criteria and real customer telemetry, but still requires human approval before deployment. The key takeaway is that AI can scale detection engineering only when paired with explicit rules, prepared data, adversarial validation, and human oversight.

Mitiga built FORGE, an automated detection engineering system that adapts multi-agent AI ideas from vulnerability hunting to the defensive task of building detections. The author says the system grew from manual scripts and human judgment into reusable automation and then into an orchestrated pipeline. Its purpose is not to auto-deploy detections, but to create review-ready pull requests that humans approve and merge.

FORGE runs a staged process that includes threat-intel planning, parallel detector generation, deduplication, adversarial validation, red-team review, reachability testing, and reporting. Candidate detections are checked against strict metadata, style, MITRE ATT&CK mapping, dynamic severity expectations, and other written quality rules. The article emphasizes that the real scoreboard comes from testing detections against customer telemetry in Mitiga’s Cloud Security Data Lake, rather than relying only on unit tests.

The article describes several lessons from building the system, including failures caused by strict parsers, candidates that passed review but failed to load, and red-team review that initially rejected too much. Mitiga improved the pipeline by adding deterministic loading gates, more constructive red-team review, and automated repair attempts for rejected candidates. The author says more than 1,000 validated detections have shipped through this approach over the past several months, and the next planned step is validating detections by safely reproducing the attacker behavior they are meant to catch.

  • The article argues that the hardest part of AI-assisted detection engineering is not getting a model to draft rules, but creating a real validation scoreboard using normalized, enriched, queryable customer telemetry.
  • FORGE’s early failures showed that multi-agent systems can reject valid work for non-security reasons, such as overly strict parsing of a reviewer model’s output.
  • Mitiga found that an adversarial reviewer that demands perfect detections is counterproductive, because useful detections often have some false-positive risk or possible bypasses.
  • A major quality improvement came from repairing rejected detection candidates with bounded automated rewrite attempts instead of discarding them immediately.
  • The planned next validation step is to safely reproduce the attacker behavior a detector is meant to catch, so the detector is proven against a fresh controlled execution rather than only historical logs.
Key Points
