In plain words

A fake Crédit Agricole bank email reached a manufacturing worker’s mailbox looking trustworthy because it was sent through a real European logistics carrier’s approved email setup, which had been taken over. The bank was being impersonated, the logistics carrier was also a victim, and the recipient was pushed toward a password-stealing website created only a few hours earlier. This matters because the usual “is this email really from an approved sender?” checks all passed, showing that criminals can abuse trusted business email channels to make scams look legitimate.

Summary
A phishing email impersonating Crédit Agricole passed SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and Microsoft composite authentication because attackers abused a properly configured SendGrid sending channel belonging to a European logistics carrier. The key lesson is that email authentication proves domain control, not brand legitimacy, so defenses must also evaluate display-name/domain mismatch, link destinations, and newly registered landing domains.

A bank credential phishing message reached a manufacturing mailbox while SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and Microsoft composite authentication all passed. The email used Crédit Agricole branding, but the authenticated sending domain belonged to a real European logistics carrier with no plausible connection to retail banking. The article says the carrier was a second victim because its own email service provider domain authentication had been hijacked, allowing the attacker to inherit a reputable sending channel.

The message’s single call to action was a French device-renewal request that routed through SendGrid click tracking before landing on info-actualisation-client[.]com. That landing domain was created roughly four hours before the message was sent, had privacy-shielded registrant fields, was unsigned for DNSSEC, and used nameservers with a low-profile hosting operator. The subject line included invisible zero-width characters and a trailing word joiner, and the message included a one-pixel open beacon on the same provider account.

The article emphasizes that email authentication proved the carrier’s domain was in control of the sending channel, not that the visible bank brand was legitimate. Microsoft composite authentication returned its strongest pass result, while the same platform also marked the message as spam with an SCL score of 5. Themis flagged the call-to-action link as malicious, scored the message at 88% confidence for credential theft, and automatically resolved the incident as phishing for the single affected mailbox.

  • A full SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and composite authentication pass can become misleading when attackers control a legitimately configured sender channel, because the checks validate the carrier’s domain rather than the bank brand shown to the recipient.
  • The article treats disagreement between Microsoft’s strongest composite authentication pass and an SCL 5 spam verdict as an escalation signal, because the content-based score adds information after provenance has already been satisfied.
  • Domain age measured in hours is highlighted as more useful than day-level reputation checks, since the phishing domain was created roughly four hours before delivery and had not existed long enough to build a bad reputation.
  • Provider click-tracking can hide a malicious destination behind a familiar service hostname, so the article recommends resolving wrapped links to their final landing page before judging risk.
  • A display-name brand check against the authenticated domain is a practical control gap, because email authentication protocols do not evaluate whether the human-readable brand matches the domain that actually sent the message.
Key Points
  • 01A bank credential phishing email reached a manufacturing mailbox with SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and Microsoft composite authentication all passing because the authenticated sending domain belonged to a European logistics carrier whose email service provider domain authentication had been hijacked.
